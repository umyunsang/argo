\newcommand{\fRB}[1]{\left(#1\right)} \newcommand{\fSB}[1]{\left[#1\right]} \newcommand{\fCB}[1]{\left\{#1\right\}} \newcommand{\fVB}[1]{\left\vert#1\right\vert} \section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro} Large language models (LLMs) are taking over the world of AI. Recent years saw a rapid development of models primarily based on the decoder-only Transformer variant~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, such as GPT~\cite{radford2019language, radford2018improving, bubeck2023sparks, brown2020language}, PaLM~\cite{chowdhery2022palm}, or LLaMA~\cite{touvron2023llama}. \emph{Prompt engineering} is a resource-efficient approach for solving different LLM tasks. In brief, one includes the task description within the input sent to an LLM. If this description is appropriately formulated, the LLM solves the task using its autoregressive token-based mechanism for generating text. Such prompts may contain example tasks with solutions (few-shot prompting, also referred to as in-context learning (ICL)), or even no example tasks at all (zero-shot prompting). In recent years it was shown that this mechanism can be used to solve a broad set of tasks that involve mathematical, commonsense, or symbolic reasoning. Chain-of-Thought (CoT)~\cite{wei2022chain} is an approach for prompting, in which one includes the intermediate steps of reasoning within the prompt (intermediate ``thoughts''), besides the task input/output. CoT was shown to significantly improve the capability of LLMs to solve problems without resorting to any model updates. One major improvement over CoT, Self-Consistency with CoT (CoT-SC)~\cite{wang2022self}, is a scheme where multiple CoTs are generated, and then the best one is selected as the outcome. More recently, CoT and CoT-SC were extended with Tree of Thoughts (ToT)~\cite{long2023large, yao2023tree, xie2023decomposition}, which models the LLM reasoning process with a tree. This facilitates using different paths of thoughts, and offers novel capabilities such as backtracking from non-promising outcomes. Unfortunately, the ToT approaches still fundamentally limit the reasoning abilities within a prompt by imposing the rigid tree structure on the thought process. In this work, we argue that fundamentally more powerful prompting can be achieved by enabling LLM thoughts to form an arbitrary graph structure. This is motivated by numerous phenomena such as human reasoning, brain structure, or algorithmic execution. When working on a novel idea, a human would not only follow a chain of thoughts (as in CoT) or try different separate ones (as in ToT), but would actually form a more complex network of thoughts. For example, one could explore a certain chain of reasoning, backtrack and start a new one, then realize that a certain idea from the previous chain could be combined with the currently explored one, and merge them both into a new solution, taking advantage of their strengths and eliminating their weaknesses. % Similarly, brains form complex networks, with graph-like patterns such as recurrence~\cite{friston2008hierarchical}. % Executing algorithms also expose networked patterns, often represented by Directed Acyclic Graphs. % The corresponding \emph{graph-enabled transformations} bring a promise of more powerful prompting when applied to LLM thoughts, but they are not naturally expressible with CoT or ToT. We observe that these (and many other) thought transformations can be naturally enabled when \emph{modeling the reasoning process of an LLM as a graph}. For this, we propose \nameS (\nameA), an approach that \emph{enhances LLMs' capabilities through networked reasoning} (\textbf{contribution~\#1}). In \nameA, an LLM thought is modeled as a vertex, while an edge is a dependency between such thoughts. Using \nameA, one can aggregate arbitrary thoughts by constructing vertices that have more than one incoming edge. Overall, the graph abstraction harnessed by \nameAS seamlessly generalizes CoT and ToT to more complex thought patterns, \emph{without resorting to any model updates}. Yet, putting \nameAS to practice requires solving several design challenges. For example, what is the best graph structure for different tasks? How to best aggregate thoughts to maximize accuracy and minimize cost? To answer these and many other questions, we carefully design a modular architecture for implementing \nameAS (\textbf{contribution~\#2}), coming with two design highlights. % \if 0 Similarly to the ToT design by Long~\cite{long2023large}, our architecture harnesses a prompter agent (to construct prompts), a evaluator module (to verify or score the LLM thoughts), a memory module (to maintain the state of the conversation), and a controller (to manage the conversation). There are two significant enhancements that we employ. \fi % First, we enable a \emph{fine-grained control over individual thoughts}. This enables us to fully control the ongoing conversation with the LLM, and apply advanced thought transformations, such as combining most promising thoughts from the ongoing reasoning into a new one. Second, we ensure that our architecture can be seamlessly extended with novel thought transformations, patterns of reasoning (i.e., graphs of thoughts), and LLM models. This enables rapid prototyping of novel prompting ideas using \nameA, while experimenting with different models such as GPT-3.5, GPT-4, or Llama-2~\cite{touvron2023llama2}. % We further enhance \nameAS by harnessing \emph{different classes of thoughts}. For example, it was discovered that asking the LLM to first plan the next steps, and only then execute them, can improve the quality of ultimate solutions~\cite{??}. We embrace this in \nameAS the \emph{heterogeneous graph model} to harness % Two modes for taking decisions (fast, unconscious; slow, conscious)~\cite{stanovich1999rational, sloman1996empirical, kahneman2002representativeness, daniel2000thinking} % \maciej{Find use cases for the following} Next, GoT embraces the heterogeneous and higher-order graph models, which enables different classes of thoughts as well as hierarchical thoughts. This further advances the reasoning capability by harnessing hierarchies of thoughts, thoughts of different classes. % \maciej{multi-modal? Vertex as text and vertex as picture?} We illustrate several use cases for \nameAS (sorting, keyword counting for summaries, set operations, document merging) and we detail how to implement them using the graph-based paradigm (\textbf{contribution~\#3}). % We evaluate \nameAS and show its advantages over the state of the art (\textbf{contribution~\#4}). Overall, we observe that \nameAS is particularly well-suited for tasks that can be naturally decomposed into smaller subtasks that are solved individually and then merged for a final solution. Here, \nameAS outperforms other schemes, for example improving upon CoT and ToT by, respectively, $\approx$70\% and $\approx$62\%, in terms of the quality of sorting, while \emph{simultaneously} reducing costs by $>$31\% over ToT. We qualitatively compare \nameAS to other prompting schemes\footnote{Note that we do not include a recent scheme called Graph-of-Thought~\cite{yao2023beyond} because it is not a prompting scheme. While its name suggests close connections to ToT and CoT, as a fine-tuning scheme, it resorts to model updates, and is thus outside the focus of this work. It enables using LLMs efficiently, without any model updates. However, designing effective prompts is a challenging task. In this work, we propose \nameS (\nameA), a new paradigm that enables the LLM to solve different tasks effectively without any model updates. The key idea is to model the LLM reasoning as an arbitrary graph, where thoughts are vertices and dependencies between thoughts are edges. This enables novel transformations of thoughts, such as aggregation. Human's task solving is often non-linear, and it involves combining intermediate solutions into final ones, or changing the flow of reasoning upon discovering new insights. \nameAS reflects this with its graph structure. % % It also brings the LLM reasoning closer to that of a human being. \nameAS outperforms other prompting schemes, for example ensuring 62\% increase in the quality of sorting over ToT, while simultaneously reducing costs by $>$31\%. % % Even more importantly, \nameA's generic formulation based on arbitrary graphs opens a way to future novel powerful reasoning approaches that will benefit from the richness of graph structures. % We also propose a novel metric for a prompting scheme, the volume of a thought, to indicate the scope of information that a given LLM output could carry with it, where \nameAS also excels. This provides a step towards more principled prompt engineering. The graph abstraction has been the foundation of several successful designs in computing and AI over last decades, for example AlphaFold for protein predictions. Set Intersection} %\definecolor{mygreen}{RGB}{49,127,56} %\definecolor{myblue}{RGB}{40,0,249} We present the prompts only for the intersection of two 32-element sets, as those for 64-element and 128-element sets are identical, except for the \textit{split\_prompt} where the size of the split is adjusted proportionally. For set intersection, we employ two distinct types of operations that interact with the LLM, each with its corresponding prompts. First, there is the \textbf{Generate} operation, utilizing the \textit{intersect\_prompt} to guide the LLM in intersecting two input sets, and the \textit{split\_prompt} to direct the LLM to split a specified set into a designated number of distinct subsets. For document merging, we employ four distinct types of operations that interact with the LLM, each with its corresponding prompts. First, there is the \textbf{Generate} operation, utilizing the \textit{merge\_prompt} to instruct the LLM to merge the 4 NDAs into 1. Second, the \textbf{Score} operations instructs the LLM to score a given merged NDA using the \textit{score\_prompt}. Next, the \textbf{Aggregate} operation employs the \textit{aggregate\_prompt} to instruct the LLM to aggregate multiple merge attempts into a single, better one. Finally, the \textbf{Improve} operation leverages the \textit{improve\_prompt} to instruct the LLM to improve a merged NDA.
