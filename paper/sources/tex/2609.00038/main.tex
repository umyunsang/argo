\begin{abstract}
Outcome-only evaluation is the production default for LLM agents: show a judge the request and
the final reply and ask whether it was handled well. The metric is structurally blind to an agent
that reaches the right answer the wrong way. We measure that blind spot where ground truth is
known by construction: a deterministic tool-using support-desk environment, a scripted oracle
policy that always solves it, and a fault injector that breaks exactly one thing at a known
step, stratifying faults by whether the customer-visible outcome survived (\emph{silent}) or
not (\emph{loud}). Five judges (programmatic rules, outcome-only, step-rubric at two model
sizes, and a self-consistency ensemble) are scored on detection, step localisation, fault
typing, calibration, and cost over \tjNTraj{} trajectories. The outcome-only judge catches
\tjOutcomeLoudPct{} of loud faults but \tjOutcomeSilentPct{} of silent ones while flagging
\tjOutcomeFAPct{} of correct trajectories; a step-rubric judge reaches \tjStepQSilentPct{}
silent recall with zero false alarms at $3\times$ the cost. No judge reads the final reply: an
invented promise appended to an otherwise perfect trajectory evades the rules entirely and
the step judge \tjStepQUnsupMissPct{} of the time, and self-consistency triples
cost while improving nothing. We argue that judge evaluations must stratify recall by outcome
survival, and release the environment, the injector, all raw verdicts, and an analysis
pipeline that rebuilds every number offline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Agents that call tools are gated on outcome-level judgment. A model reads the user's request
and the agent's final answer, decides whether the case was handled well, and that verdict
feeds a dashboard, a release gate, or a reward signal. The failures that survive this filter
are precisely the ones it cannot see: the agent that skipped a required eligibility check,
acted against what a tool returned, or promised something no observation supports, whenever
the final answer nevertheless came out right. A refund of the correct amount issued without
ever checking eligibility reads, from the outside, exactly like the same refund issued
correctly. These are the faults that reach production, because the metric meant to catch them
is structurally blind to them.

Evaluating the evaluator normally requires human labels, and the labeller is then the thing
under test. We escape the circle by construction. A deterministic support-desk environment is
solved by a scripted oracle policy that is provably correct (a test suite asserts it passes
every process rule and reaches the expected outcome on every instance), and a fault injector
then breaks exactly one thing at a known step and replays the trajectory so its observations
stay internally consistent. Every trajectory therefore carries an exact label at zero
annotation cost: faulty or not, at which step, of which type, and whether the customer-visible
outcome survived. This paper holds the \emph{agent} fixed and puts the \emph{judges} on the
bench: every judge error is attributable to the judge.

The stratification by outcome survival is the move that makes the measurement possible.
Pooled over all faults, the outcome-only judge's recall is a respectable-looking
\tjOutcomePooledRecall{}. Split it, and the number decomposes into \tjOutcomeLoud{} on faults
that broke the answer against \tjOutcomeSilent{} on faults that did not. The gap \emph{is}
the result, and a single pooled recall averages it away.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(i)~A correct-by-construction testbed for judges of tool-using agents: a permissive
environment with a strict checker, a six-type single-fault injector with known step and
outcome-survival labels, and a rule-engine baseline whose coverage gaps are pinned by tests
(\S\ref{sec:testbed}).
(ii)~A controlled comparison of five judge designs on five axes (detection, localisation,
typing, calibration, cost) with stratified bootstrap confidence intervals
(\S\ref{sec:judges}--\S\ref{sec:results}).
(iii)~Findings with deployment consequences: the quantified silent blind spot; a fault type,
\ftype{unsupported\_claim}, that evades every judge including those shown the full
trajectory; a negative result on self-consistency ensembling; and an 8B judge that is an
always-say-faulty baseline wearing a judge's clothes (\S\ref{sec:analysis}).
(iv)~A fully offline-reproducible artifact: raw verdicts are released, and every table,
figure, and interval in this paper regenerates from them without a model call
(\S\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Reliability of LLM judges.} Model-as-judge evaluation is standard for open-ended
outputs \citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval,gu2024survey}, and its pathologies are
well documented: position and verbosity bias \citep{wang2023fair}, self-preference
\citep{panickssery2024self}, objective failures on instruction-following comparisons
\citep{zeng2024llmbar}, and misalignment that grows with task difficulty
\citep{thakur2024judging}. That line audits judges of \emph{single responses} against human
labels; we audit judges of \emph{multi-step tool trajectories} against labels correct by
construction, so a flag on a clean trajectory is a false positive with nothing to argue about.
The judge design itself (reasoning before verdict, a stated confidence) follows
\citet{mohammadi2026evalmoraal}, and treating a model's labels as measurements that need their
own reliability estimate follows \citet{mohammadi2025assessing}; \citet{mohammadi2026let} develops both at thesis length.

\textbf{Agent benchmarks.} Benchmarks for tool-using agents score the agent, increasingly on
the trajectory rather than the outcome alone: $\tau$-bench compares final database state
\citep{yao2024tau}, AgentBench and WebArena score task success \citep{liu2024agentbench,
zhou2024webarena}, AgentBoard tracks subgoal progress \citep{ma2024agentboard}, and recent
work scores reasoning trajectories directly \citep{kim2025beyond,he2025trajectbench}. We
invert the roles: the agent is a fixed oracle, and the evaluator is the system under test.

\textbf{Failure attribution.} Locating the failing step or agent in a trace is an emerging
task \citep{zhang2025whowhen,deshpande2025trail,cemri2025mast,ma2025failure}, and AgenTracer
injects faults into successful trajectories to mint labelled error pairs at scale
\citep{zhang2025agentracer}. Fault injection is the established part of our design; the delta
is what is done with it: a five-way instrument comparison that separates detection from
attribution and stratifies recall by outcome survival, which that literature does not report.

\textbf{Process versus outcome supervision.} Process reward models show step-level feedback
beats outcome-level feedback for training verifiers in mathematics
\citep{lightman2024verify,uesato2022solving,wang2024mathshepherd}, and ProcessBench measures
error localisation in reasoning chains \citep{zheng2024processbench,zhang2025lessons}. That
agenda trains scorers where the final answer is checkable; we ask the measurement question
underneath it, whether an off-the-shelf judge can see process faults \emph{at all} when the
final answer is right, and find the answer depends on the judge's view, not its metric.

\textbf{Calibration.} We score every judge's stated confidence with expected calibration
error \citep{naeini2015obtaining,guo2017calibration}. Verbalized confidence can be usable
\citep{kadavath2022language,tian2023just} but trends overconfident \citep{xiong2024can}; we
add the evaluator-side result that an ensemble's vote share (the ``principled''
alternative) is structurally coarser than a stated number at small $k$.

\textbf{Mutation testing.} Seeding known defects to measure a detector is half a century old
\citep{demillo1978hints,jia2011analysis}. Whether mutants substitute for real faults is the
classic validity question \citep{just2014mutants}; \S\ref{sec:organic} answers it here:
no for prevalence, yes for capability.

\textbf{Environment.} The environment is a customer-support desk with seven tools
(\texttt{get\_customer}, \texttt{lookup\_order}, \texttt{get\_policy},
\texttt{check\_eligibility}, \texttt{issue\_refund}, \texttt{escalate}, \texttt{reply}) and a
written standard operating procedure: verify the customer, look up the order, read the policy
for the item, confirm eligibility before moving money, refund exactly the authorised amount,
escalate when not eligible, and reply asserting only what the observations support. Instances
are generated in six strata: full-price refunds, restocking fees, expired windows,
non-refundable items, orders belonging to a different customer, and orders already
refunded, drawn round-robin from a single seeded stream (Appendix~\ref{app:env}).

\textbf{The environment is permissive and the checker is strict.} \texttt{issue\_refund} will
refund an order whose eligibility was never checked, exactly as a real payments API would.
Nothing in the world stops an agent from skipping the process; only the rule checker says it
was wrong. This is the load-bearing design choice: without it there would be no silent
failures to measure, which is why it is pinned by a test
(\texttt{test\_environment\_is\_permissive\_by\_design}) rather than stated in a comment.

\textbf{Notation.} A trajectory $\tau=(g, s_0,\dots,s_{T-1}, a)$ pairs a goal $g$ and final
answer $a$ with steps $s_t=(h_t, c_t, o_t)$: a thought, a tool call
$c_t=(\mathrm{tool}_t,\mathrm{args}_t)$, and an observation $o_t$. Its label is
$\ell(\tau)=(y, t^{*}, \phi, \omega)$: faulty or not, the failure step, the failure type
$\phi\in\Phi$ ($|\Phi|=6$), and whether the customer-visible outcome matched the instance's
expected outcome. A fault is \emph{silent} iff $y\wedge\omega$ and \emph{loud} iff
$y\wedge\neg\omega$. A judge maps a \emph{view} of the trajectory to a verdict
$(\hat y, \hat t, \hat\phi, \hat c, r)$ with stated confidence $\hat c$ and rationale $r$. The
outcome-only view is $V_{\mathrm{out}}(\tau)=(g,a)$; the step view $V_{\mathrm{step}}(\tau)$
is the full rendering. The two LLM judges in this paper differ in exactly this view and in
nothing else.

\textbf{Oracle.} The oracle is a fixed six-step script, identical across strata up to one
branch: verify, look up, read the policy for the SKU \emph{returned by the lookup
observation}, check eligibility, then refund the authorised amount if eligible and escalate
otherwise, and reply. A test parametrised over all instances asserts it violates no rule and
reaches the expected outcome; correctness is computed, not assumed.

\textbf{Fault injector.} Each mutation edits the call list only (one edit, one step) and
then \emph{replays} the edited list against a fresh environment, regenerating every
observation. A hallucinated SKU therefore genuinely fails its lookup; nothing is hand-written,
and a test asserts the replayed observations are consistent with the calls.
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} lists the six types. Injection is string-seeded per (instance, type),
so the set is deterministic. One discipline matters enough to state: mutations are constructed
to break exactly one rule. \ftype{skipped\_precondition}, for example, rewrites the refund
amount to the order total specifically so the argument stays grounded in the lookup
observation; otherwise a second rule would fire and the confusion matrix would measure the
injector rather than the judge. The judged set is \tjNTraj{} trajectories: \tjNClean{} clean,
\tjNSilent{} silent, \tjNLoud{} loud (\tjNPerType{} per type), deterministically shuffled so
any prefix is a stratified sample.

\textbf{The rule engine, and its two zeros.} A checker walks each trajectory once,
maintaining a pool of grounded values seeded from the goal, and enforces eight rules over
grounding, ordering, amounts, identity, and terminality (Appendix~\ref{app:checker}). It
covers four of the six fault types at 100\% and two at exactly 0\%, structurally.
\ftype{wrong\_tool} breaks no ordering or argument predicate: the checker has no notion of
tool \emph{intent}. \ftype{unsupported\_claim} appends prose to the reply, and no rule reads
the reply at all: prose is deliberately excluded from the grounding pool because identifiers
ground and sentences do not. Both zeros are pinned by a test that fails if they ever change.
This is the honest free baseline: the argument for LLM judges, stated as a measurement rather
than an assertion.

Table~\ref{tab:judges} lists the five judges \citep{qwen2025qwen,dubey2024llama}. The
engineering that keeps the comparison fair is worth itemising. Both LLM judges receive the
same standard operating procedure and the same failure taxonomy, word for word. Responses are
decoded under a JSON schema (grammar-constrained), with \texttt{reasoning} as the first
property: schema property order is generation order, so chain-of-thought is enforced by the
grammar rather than requested politely. The context window is set explicitly
(\texttt{num\_ctx}=8192) because the serving default would silently truncate a rendered
trajectory, and a judge scoring the half it happened to see is a bug that reads as a finding.
Confidence is clamped to $[0.5, 1.0]$; an unparseable response would vote \emph{clean} at
confidence $0.5$ rather than be dropped (silently dropping it would quietly improve whichever
judge fails most often), though none occurred. Judges cannot read the label. The outcome
judge's prompt carries explicit de-biasing (``not being able to see the steps is not evidence
of a failure''), so its false-alarm rate below is not an artefact of an under-specified
prompt. The self-consistency ensemble \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} runs the step judge
$k{=}3$ times at $T{=}0.7$ with a strict-majority vote; its confidence is the vote share,
which at $k{=}3$ can express exactly two values, $2/3$ and $1$. All prompts and schemas are
verbatim in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\section{Metrics and uncertainty}
\label{sec:metrics}

\textbf{Detection} is binary at the trajectory level: precision, recall, and F1 over all
\tjNTraj{} trajectories, plus the three-way partition that carries the paper: silent recall,
loud recall, and the false-alarm rate on clean trajectories.
\textbf{Localisation} is exact match on the failure step, scored only where both label and
verdict carry a step \emph{and} the judge flagged the trajectory: the denominator is
conditional on detection and therefore differs per judge (printed with every estimate; a judge
that flags more gets a larger denominator).
\textbf{Typing} is a six-class confusion restricted to genuinely faulty trajectories, scored
as macro-F1. A false alarm is charged once, to precision, not a second time here; a flagged
trajectory with no type named counts as missed, not as a free pass.
\textbf{Calibration} is expected calibration error \citep{naeini2015obtaining} with ten
equal-width bins, population-weighted, against the binary verdict; we also report Brier
scores in Appendix~\ref{app:full}. Two disclaimers travel with the ECE column: the rule
engine's confidence is two hand-set constants (its ECE scores those constants, not a model's
self-assessment), and the ensemble's vote share has two possible values at $k{=}3$, so its
ECE is a structurally coarser object than a single judge's.
\textbf{Cost} is wall-clock seconds per trajectory on one workstation, with token counts
from the serving layer in Appendix~\ref{app:full}.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} Every interval in this paper is a 95\% stratified paired percentile
bootstrap over trajectories \citep{efron1979bootstrap}: $B{=}10{,}000$ replicates, resampling
within the eight design cells (clean; each failure type split by outcome survival) so the
design margins are preserved, with one shared resample across all five judges per
replicate, which is what licenses the paired deltas quoted in \S\ref{sec:analysis}.
Proportions observed at $0$ or $1$ get exact Clopper--Pearson intervals instead (marked
$\dagger$). The rule engine behaves identically on every trajectory within a design cell, so
the design-conditioned bootstrap assigns its recalls no sampling variance; those entries are
shown without brackets, because its coverage is structural (pinned by tests), not estimated.
These intervals quantify resampling uncertainty over the fixed \tjNTraj{}-trajectory design,
conditional on the generation seed and greedy decoding; they are not variance over
environments, seeds, or model builds. The full procedure and all deltas are in
Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}.

Table~\ref{tab:main} is the comparison; Figure~\ref{fig:silentloud} is its picture. Before
interpreting either, one reading discipline: \emph{recall at or near a judge's false-alarm
rate is not detection; it measures the judge's baseline willingness to say ``faulty''}, and loud
recall plus a matching false-alarm rate is what an always-say-faulty judge gets for free.

The outcome judge catches \tjOutcomeLoud{} \ci{\tjOutcomeLoudCI} of loud faults but only
\tjOutcomeSilent{} \ci{\tjOutcomeSilentCI} of silent ones, while flagging \tjOutcomeFA{}
\ci{\tjOutcomeFACI} of clean trajectories. The step judge at the same model reaches
\tjStepQSilent{} \ci{\tjStepQSilentCI} silent recall with \emph{zero} false alarms across
\tjNClean{} clean trajectories ($\dagger$ 95\% upper bound \tjFAUpperBound): of the
\tjStepQFlags{} trajectories it flagged, all \tjStepQFlagsTrue{} were genuinely faulty. Its
localisation is nearly exact (\tjStepQStepExact{} over its \tjStepQStepN{} scored cases) and
its stated confidence is nearly honest (ECE \tjStepQECE{} against the outcome judge's
\tjOutcomeECE). The rule engine is free, perfectly localised, never false-alarms, and types
faults better than any model (\tjRulesTypeF{} macro-F1) because it never guesses. It
still misses more than half the silent faults (\tjRulesSilent), with no better version
possible: two of the six types are outside what rules can express. The 8B step judge flags
\tjStepLFlags{} of \tjNTraj{} trajectories; its numbers are read in
\S\ref{sec:capability-floor}. The ensemble triples cost (\tjSelfconsCost{} vs
\tjStepQCost{} s/traj) and lands within noise of one greedy pass on every quality axis
(\S\ref{sec:selfcons}).

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{The blind spot is structural, not a weak model}
\label{sec:blindspot}

The outcome and step judges are the same model, decoding, procedure, and taxonomy; the only
difference is the view. Changing $V_{\mathrm{out}}$ to $V_{\mathrm{step}}$ moves silent recall
from \tjOutcomeSilent{} to \tjStepQSilent{} (paired $\Delta = \tjDeltaStepOutcomeSilent$,
95\% CI \tjDeltaStepOutcomeSilentCI) and false alarms from \tjOutcomeFA{} to zero. Within the
outcome judge, the loud$-$silent gap is $\tjDeltaOutcomeLoudSilent$ \ci{\tjDeltaOutcomeLoudSilentCI}.
The judge is not weak. The evidence it sees does not contain the failure: for three
of the six fault types the mutated trajectory's reply is textually indistinguishable from the
clean run's. Pooled recall would read \tjOutcomePooledRecall{}; the stratification is the
measurement. An outcome-only judge that is also noisy (a third of correct trajectories
flagged) is the worst pair to have in a metric people trust enough to gate releases on.
\emph{Deployment consequence: report judge recall stratified by outcome survival, or the
number will hide exactly the faults process supervision exists to catch.}

\subsection{Case study: nobody reads the final answer}
\label{sec:unsupported}

\ftype{unsupported\_claim} follows the procedure perfectly and appends one invented sentence
to the reply. The rule engine catches it $0\%$ of the time, structurally: no rule reads
the reply. The step judge, which \emph{is} shown the reply and has the type in its taxonomy,
catches \tjStepQUnsupClaim{} \ci{\tjStepQUnsupClaimCI}; the ensemble
\tjSelfconsUnsupClaim{}. The outcome judge's \tjOutcomeUnsupClaim{} looks better until read
against its \tjOutcomeFA{} false-alarm rate: barely above its baseline willingness to flag.
One miss is diagnostic. On a trajectory whose reply ends with \emph{``I have also cancelled
the subscription linked to this order''}, a subscription no observation mentions, the step
judge walks all six steps, concludes \emph{``The agent's trajectory follows the procedure
correctly, step by step: 1.\ Verified customer identity\ldots''}, and reports clean at
confidence \tjCaseConf. Giving a judge the whole trajectory makes it better at everything except
the failure that lives in the answer, where it gets \emph{worse}, because its attention goes
to the steps. \emph{Deployment consequence: verifying the final reply against the evidence is
its own task; run it as a separate pass rather than assuming the trajectory judge covers it.}

\subsection{The capability floor}
\label{sec:capability-floor}

\judge{step (8B)} is the always-say-faulty baseline wearing a judge's clothes: it flags
\tjStepLFlags{} of \tjNTraj{} trajectories, so its recall is $1.00$ nearly everywhere, its
false-alarm rate is $1.00$, and its F1 of \tjStepLFone{} is the base rate of faults in the
set (precision \tjStepLPrecision{} $\approx \tjNFaulty/\tjNTraj$). Its rationales average
\tjStepLMeanTokens{} completion tokens against the 14B judge's \tjStepQMeanTokens{}: a
boilerplate sentence, then the verdict. Same prompt, same schema, same grammar: this is a
capability floor, not a prompting artefact. It stays in the table because a cheap local judge
that looks excellent on recall alone is a mistake worth being able to point at.
\emph{Deployment consequence: floor-check any candidate judge against the always-say-faulty
baseline before trusting its recall.}

\subsection{Self-consistency: three times the cost, nothing back}
\label{sec:selfcons}

The ensemble is the same step-rubric judge sampled three times with a majority vote. Against
one greedy pass, every quality delta is within resampling noise (F1
$\tjDeltaSelfconsFone$ \ci{\tjDeltaSelfconsFoneCI}, silent recall $\tjDeltaSelfconsSilent$
\ci{\tjDeltaSelfconsSilentCI}, type F1 $\tjDeltaSelfconsTypeF$ \ci{\tjDeltaSelfconsTypeFCI},
step localisation $\tjDeltaSelfconsStepExact$ \ci{\tjDeltaSelfconsStepExactCI}), while
calibration is measurably \emph{worse} (ECE $\tjDeltaSelfconsECE$
\ci{\tjDeltaSelfconsECECI}) and cost is deterministically $3\times$ (\tjSelfconsCost{} vs.\
\tjStepQCost{} s/traj). The calibration result is the instructive one: vote share was
supposed to be an \emph{estimate} where a stated confidence is not, but at $k{=}3$ it can
express only two values; we observed exactly \tjSelfconsTwoThirds{} verdicts at $2/3$ and
\tjSelfconsUnanimous{} at $1.0$. And voting sharpened nothing because the errors are not
sampling noise: \ftype{unsupported\_claim} goes \tjStepQUnsupClaim{} $\to$
\tjSelfconsUnsupClaim{}, meaning all three samples miss the same invented promises. This is a
negative result and we publish it as one. \emph{Deployment consequence: before paying
$k\times$ for an ensemble, check whether the judge's errors are stochastic; systematic misses
vote together.}

\subsection{Detecting a fault and naming it are different problems}
\label{sec:attribution}

The step judge detects nearly everything (F1 \tjStepQFone) and misattributes a third of what
it detects (type macro-F1 \tjStepQTypeF). From its confusion matrix
(Appendix~\ref{app:full}): it finds all \tjNPerType{} \ftype{hallucinated\_argument} cases
and calls \tjStepQHallucAsWrong{} of them \ftype{wrong\_tool}. Fetching a policy for an
invented SKU \emph{is} also a tool that does not serve the sub-goal, so part of this charge
belongs to the taxonomy's boundary rather than the judge. \ftype{premature\_stop} scatters
worse: of \tjNPerType{}, it names \tjStepQPremCorrect{} correctly, calls
\tjStepQPremAsUnsup{} \ftype{unsupported\_claim} and \tjStepQPremAsSkipped{}
\ftype{skipped\_precondition}, a defensible reading of a trajectory that stopped early and
then asserted things it had not established. The rule engine types better
(\tjRulesTypeF{}) because it never guesses. \emph{Deployment consequence: if verdicts route
tickets or fill dashboard categories, evaluate attribution separately from detection; the
headline detection number does not transfer.}

\section{What faults occur organically}
\label{sec:organic}

Injected faults are uniform over six types by design. To check what the distribution looks
like in the wild, we let \texttt{qwen2.5:14b} \emph{drive the agent} for \tjAgentN{} episodes
under the same procedure text the judges receive, labelled by the rule engine (which is blind
to two types, so these rates are lower bounds) and reported separately, never mixed into the
controlled comparison. \tjAgentFaulty{} of \tjAgentN{} episodes are flagged or reach a wrong
outcome; \tjAgentWrongOutcome{} break the customer-visible outcome and \tjAgentSilent{} are
silent. Every rule-visible fault is a \ftype{premature\_stop}, concentrated where escalation
is required (\tjAgentAlreadyRefundedFaulty{} of 10 \ftype{already\_refunded} episodes fail;
\tjAgentWrongCustomerFaulty{} of 10 \ftype{wrong\_customer}); the model also re-escalates
compulsively (\tjAgentEscalates{} \texttt{escalate} calls across \tjAgentN{} episodes) and
runs \texttt{check\_eligibility} in only \tjAgentEligibilityEpisodes{} of \tjAgentN{}
episodes. The organic distribution is nothing like uniform: the benchmark measures what a
judge is \emph{capable of catching}, not how often each fault occurs in deployment
\citep{just2014mutants}. Those are different questions, and only the first is answered here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{One environment, one domain.} A support desk with encoded preconditions is a friendly
case \emph{for} step-level judging; open-ended coding or browsing agents have no comparable
rule engine. If the blind spot is this large where rules can exist, domains without them are
worse, not better. We measure only this one.
\textbf{Injected faults are cleaner than real ones.} Each mutation breaks exactly one thing
at one step; real trajectories fail in cascades, and \S\ref{sec:organic} shows the organic
mix is a point mass, not uniform.
\textbf{Local models only.} Everything runs on \texttt{qwen2.5:14b} and
\texttt{llama3.1:8b}, so results reproduce without an API key. A frontier judge would very
likely close part of the \ftype{unsupported\_claim} gap; this paper does not claim otherwise
and does not measure it. The instrument is model-agnostic.
\textbf{Confidence is self-reported} for single-pass judges; only the ensemble's is an
estimate of anything, and that is visible in the ECE column.
\textbf{Two types share a blurry border.} A policy fetched for an invented SKU is
simultaneously an ungrounded argument and a wrong tool; charging the judge for the other
reading overstates its attribution error somewhat. Detection is unaffected.
\textbf{The judged set is not stratum-balanced} (\tjNRestocking{} of \tjNTraj{} trajectories
sit in the restocking stratum, because two fault types can only be hosted there); scores are
not stratum-marginalised.
\textbf{``Silent'' is defined by the outcome the environment can see.} A refund of the right
amount by the wrong route counts as outcome-correct here. A bank auditing the route would
disagree, which is the point of measuring the route separately.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Every number in this paper regenerates offline. The released artifact carries the
environment, oracle, injector, and judges; the \tjNTraj{} labelled trajectories and all
\tjNVerdicts{} raw verdicts; and the analysis pipeline (bootstrap, tables, figures) with
fixed seeds. Continuous integration rebuilds the published tables from the committed raw
verdicts and fails on any drift, so the numbers cannot quietly stop matching the data they
came from. Determinism is pinned by named tests: seeded instance generation, string-seeded
mutation, byte-identical report rebuilds, and a deterministic shuffle under which any prefix
of the dataset is a stratified sample. Code, data, and raw verdicts:
\texttt{https://github.com/mohammadi-hadi/trajectory-judge}.
