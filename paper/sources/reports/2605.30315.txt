## Research Report: Resolution Diagnostics for Paired LLM Evaluation

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The author of this research paper is Anany Kotawala, affiliated with Princeton University, Princeton, NJ, USA.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently relies on leaderboards that rank models based on performance metrics, often reported as percentage-point scores on shared-prompt benchmarks. While these leaderboards provide a readily accessible means of comparing models, the underlying statistical claims regarding the significance of observed performance differences are often not rigorously assessed or explicitly reported.

Previous work in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and LLM evaluation has highlighted issues related to statistical power and appropriate test selection. Card et al. (2020) demonstrated that NLP comparisons are frequently underpowered, while Dror et al. (2018) surveyed various statistical tests, including McNemar's test for paired binary outcomes, which is relevant for shared-prompt evaluations. Miller (2024) introduced a closed-form required-N formula for unpaired Gaussian-accuracy cases, representing a prevalent approach for estimating sample sizes in LLM accuracy comparisons. However, this approach is designed for independent samples and does not fully account for the paired nature of many LLM evaluations, where models are assessed on the identical set of prompts.

This paper addresses a gap in current LLM evaluation practices by integrating classical hypothesis testing machinery, such as McNemar's test for paired binary outcomes, with modern leaderboard dynamics. It moves beyond simple p-value reporting to provide a comprehensive framework for assessing the *resolution* of pairwise LLM comparisons. The work differentiates itself by quantifying the efficiency gains of paired tests over unpaired alternatives in shared-prompt settings and by applying rigorous statistical diagnostics, including multiplicity correction, cluster-aware adjustments, and anytime-valid sequential testing, to real-world LLM leaderboards. Furthermore, it identifies a specific calculator-misuse pattern in common power analysis tools that leads to underestimation of required sample sizes in paired settings. This study therefore aims to enhance the statistical rigor and transparency of LLM leaderboard claims by providing diagnostics that reflect the actual evaluation design and statistical demands.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and apply a comprehensive resolution-reporting framework for paired LLM evaluation, addressing the limitations of current leaderboard practices. The motivation stems from the observation that many displayed pairwise rankings on prominent LLM leaderboards (e.g., Open LLM Leaderboard v1, MMLU-Pro) do not meet conventional statistical resolution targets, even when reporting nominally significant p-values. A gap between two models, such as "model A scores 78.3%, model B scores 77.5%", often translates into headlines and product decisions, yet the assertion that model A is meaningfully superior to model B is a statistical claim that requires adequate evidentiary support.

The paper highlights specific instances where observed gaps, despite yielding asymptotic significance, fall short of the required sample size for a predefined Type-I (α) and Type-II (1-β) error rate. For example, a 0.46 percentage-point (pp) gap on HellaSwag between gemma-7B and Llama-3-8B was asymptotically significant (p=0.049) but not by an exact conditional binomial test (p=0.054), and its paired-bootstrap 95% confidence interval contained zero. This illustrates that a simple p-value may not sufficiently convey whether a benchmark has the necessary resolution to distinguish between models.

The key objectives are structured around three main contributions:

1.  **New Theory**: To provide a sharp characterization of a specific misuse pattern in power analysis. This includes developing a small-effect expansion (Lemma 1) for the commonly used unpaired Cohen-h-plus-(1-ρ) shortcut, quantifying its deviation from the correct required sample size (N*) by approximately a factor of two in close-comparison regimes. An explicit second-order constant (C(p, ρ)) is derived, allowing practitioners to compute the admissible effect size (δ*) at which the shortcut remains reliable.

2.  **New Empirical Findings**:
    *   To demonstrate, through empirical reanalysis of public LLM leaderboards (Open LLM Leaderboard v1 and MMLU-Pro), that numerous displayed pairwise rankings are statistically "unresolved" at conventional (α, 1-β) resolution levels (0.05, 0.8).
    *   To characterize how three widely used power analysis calculators (Cohen 1988, G*Power, R pwr) silently underestimate the required sample size by a factor of two when the user applies a common (1-ρ) adjustment to their per-arm output.
    *   To show that real subject-level clustering significantly impacts the required sample size, increasing the count of unresolved pairs in the MMLU-Pro dataset.
    *   To empirically validate that the proposed framework's N* prescription achieves target power on real LLM evaluation data.

3.  **Methodological Integration**: To package and integrate classical statistical building blocks (Wald inversion, McNemar-Connor required-N, multiplicity correction, design-effect cluster correction, anytime-valid e-processes) into a coherent, end-to-end resolution characterization pipeline for public LLM leaderboards. This includes developing a pip-installable software package, `llm-power`, to facilitate the adoption of the diagnostic protocol by benchmark designers and leaderboard maintainers.

Overall, the motivation is to move beyond mere point estimates and p-values in LLM evaluation, advocating for a more robust statistical assessment of leaderboard claims by explicitly reporting the resolution capability of benchmarks given their size and data structure.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research frames shared-prompt LLM benchmarks as paired hypothesis-testing problems and derives a resolution-reporting framework by inverting level-α, power-(1-β) tests.

**Paired Setup:**
Two models, A and B, are evaluated on the same *N* prompts, which are treated as an i.i.d. sample from a prompt superpopulation. Per-prompt scores (X_A_i, X_B_i) can be binary, graded, or real-valued. The core quantity is the paired difference D_i = X_A_i - X_B_i, with its paired-mean estimator δ̂ = N⁻¹ Σ D_i.

**Test and Inversion:**
Under a normal approximation, the test statistic T_N = δ̂ / cSE(δ̂), where cSE(δ̂) = σ_D / √N and σ²_D = Var(D_i). For two-sided level-α tests, the null hypothesis H₀: E[D_i] = 0 is rejected if |T_N| ≥ z_(1-α/2). Inverting the power function (1 - β) at a fixed alternative E[D_i] = δ yields three key quantities:
*   **Required paired sample size (N*)**: N*(δ; α, β) = [(z_(1-α/2) + z_(1-β)) σ_D / |δ|]². This is the sample size needed to detect a target effect δ with desired Type-I and Type-II error rates.
*   **Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)**: δ_MDE(N; α, β) = (z_(1-α/2) + z_(1-β)) σ_D / √N. This is the smallest effect detectable at the current sample size N.
*   **Resolution Ratio (q)**: q := N / N*(δ̂). The displayed gap is considered statistically resolvable if q ≥ 1.

**Paired Variance and Multiplicity:**
For shared prompts, the variance of the paired difference is σ²_D = Var(X_A) + Var(X_B) - 2 Cov(X_A, X_B). This formulation accounts for the typically large covariance between LLM performances on shared items. For leaderboards with K models, there are up to (K choose 2) pairwise tests. Multiplicity control (e.g., Bonferroni, Holm, or Benjamini-Hochberg) is applied by adjusting α to α' in the N* formula, which inflates the required N*.

**Sequential / Anytime-Valid Extension:**
Recognizing that public leaderboards update continuously, meaning N can be a stopping time chosen post-hoc, the framework extends to anytime-valid testing. This involves replacing the fixed z_(1-α/2) threshold with a time-uniform boundary u(n) derived from a paired-Bernoulli mixture e-process, ensuring Type-I control at any stopping time.

**Paired-Binary Instantiation and the Shortcut Lemma:**
For binary accuracy metrics, the paired-difference variance (Equation 5) is σ²_D = p_A q_A + p_B q_B - 2ρ√(p_A q_A p_B q_B), where ρ is the within-pair Bernoulli correlation. Substituting this into the N* formula yields the McNemar-Connor required paired count (Equation 6).
**Lemma 1** provides a sharp small-effect expansion for a common shortcut (n_h) used when adapting unpaired Cohen-h calculators for paired designs. This shortcut, which multiplies an unpaired per-arm sample size by (1-ρ), is shown to deviate from the correct N* by approximately a factor of two in the close-comparison limit. The lemma explicitly quantifies this underestimation with a second-order constant C(p, ρ), revealing that n_h ≈ ½ N* for small δ.

**Calculator-Misuse Characterization:**
The paper demonstrates this shortcut misuse across five power-analysis tools. It shows that three out of five commonly used tools (Cohen’s 1988 textbook formula, G*Power 3.1, R pwr) underestimate N* by half when their per-arm output is naïvely multiplied by (1-ρ) for paired designs. `statsmodels.NormalIndPower` and the newly developed `llm_power` package correctly compute N*.

**Finite-Sample Calibration:**
The normal approximation underlying Equation (3) has finite-sample remainders. The paper empirically calibrates five paired-binary test variants (McNemar χ²₁, exact conditional binomial, mid-p, continuity-corrected McNemar, and a paired bootstrap) on simulated paired-Bernoulli populations. This calibration confirms that, while all variants maintain Type-I error rates, the asymptotic variants (McNemar χ²₁, mid-p, paired bootstrap) achieve target power more closely than exact or continuity-corrected methods, which tend to be conservative. The paired bootstrap is recommended for graded metrics when closed-form σ_D is unavailable.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The methodology was applied to real-world LLM leaderboard data from the Open LLM Leaderboard v1 (OLL v1) and the MMLU-Pro top-10 from OLL v2. All N* calculations used (α, 1-β) = (0.05, 0.8).

**OLL v1: 40 Unique Pairwise Comparisons:**
*   **Unresolved Pairs:** Across 40 unique pairwise comparisons for five 7-8B parameter models on four tasks, 11 pairs (28%) were found to be unresolved (q < 1) at their actual benchmark size.
*   **Resolution Boundary:** A resolution boundary was observed around |δ| ≈ 5 percentage points (pp). All pairs with |δ| ≤ 2% were unresolved, and all pairs with |δ| > 5% were resolved. The 2-5% band showed mixed results.
*   **Bootstrap Uncertainty:** For close pairs (|δ| ≤ 2%), 4 out of 7 showed robustly unresolved verdicts (5th-percentile N* > N), while the remaining 3 had confidence intervals spanning the resolution diagonal due to poorly estimated δ near zero.
*   **Prospective Design Validation:** The framework's N* prescription was empirically validated. For three OLL v1 pairs, subsampling to the calculated N* yielded empirical McNemar power between 0.796 and 0.827, closely matching the 0.80 target.
*   **Multi-Arbiter Agreement:** For close pairs (|δ| ≤ 2%), four arbiters (asymptotic McNemar χ²₁, exact conditional binomial, mid-p, paired bootstrap) agreed on 6 of 7 pairs (all fail-to-reject). A single "leaderboard-displayed-significant" HellaSwag pair (δ̂=0.46 pp) yielded a split verdict (χ²₁: p=0.049 rejects; exact: p=0.054 does not reject), consistent with it being a boundary case.

**MMLU-Pro Paired Item-Level Tightening (OLL v2):**
*   **Unresolved Adjacent Pairs:** For the 9 adjacent-rank pairs among the top-10 MMLU-Pro models (N=12,032 items), 4 out of 9 were unresolved.
*   **Lemma 1 Replication:** The shortcut n_h consistently underestimated N* by the predicted factor of two on every MMLU-Pro pair (median ratio 0.500), despite ρ ranging from 0.45 to 0.99.
*   **Rho Sensitivity:** Perturbing empirical ρ by ±0.10 shifted the unresolved count to [9, 12]/40 for OLL v1 and [2, 4]/9 for MMLU-Pro, indicating that the qualitative message of under-resolution is robust.

**Stress-Test Sequence:**

*   **Leaderboard-Scale Multiplicity:**
    *   **Bonferroni/Holm:** Applying Bonferroni correction (for a family of 40 tests on OLL v1 or 9 on MMLU-Pro) inflated N* by approximately 2.11-2.14 times. This increased the unresolved count on OLL v1 from 11/40 to 14/40, while the MMLU-Pro count remained at 4/9 (as unresolved pairs were already far below resolution).
    *   **Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR control):** This less conservative method did not retract any rejections for large-gap pairs.

*   **Cluster-Aware Sensitivity (MMLU-Pro):**
    *   **Effect of Clustering:** Using MMLU-Pro's 14 subject categories as clusters, the unresolved adjacent-pair count rose from 4/9 (IID assumption) to 6/9. Two pairs, previously considered well-resolved, flipped to N* > 3N due to high intra-cluster correlation (ICC(D) up to 0.036, Design Effect up to 31.5).
    *   **Robustness:** A category-bootstrap analysis (resampling 14 categories) showed that the unresolved count remained at 5/9 (45% of resamples) or 6/9 (55% of resamples), with Pr(unresolved ≥ 5) = 99.9%. A Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) recomputation also supported this flip, with the count remaining at 6/9 for most drops. Sensitivity to alternative cluster definitions showed the unresolved count spanning [5, 9]/9, reinforcing the importance of clustering.

*   **Anytime-Valid Leaderboard Testing (MMLU-Pro):**
    *   **Inflation of N*:** Using an anytime-valid mixture e-process for continuous monitoring, the time-uniform threshold inflated N* by 2.15 times compared to fixed-n testing.
    *   **Additional Unresolved Pair:** This stricter criterion led to 5 out of 9 adjacent-rank pairs being unresolved, one more than under fixed-n or Bonferroni-9 corrections. This indicates that a pair nominally significant under one-shot testing might not be resolvable under continuous monitoring.

**Summary of Unresolved Counts (Table 5):**
The aggregate results across various testing paradigms demonstrate a consistent pattern of under-resolution in current LLM leaderboards:
*   OLL v1: 11/40 (Fixed-n) → 14/40 (Bonferroni/Holm, Anytime-valid)
*   MMLU-Pro: 4/9 (Fixed-n) → 4/9 (Bonferroni/Holm) → 5/9 (Anytime-valid) → 6/9 (Real-ICC clustered)

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications for the evaluation and reporting practices of Large Language Model leaderboards:

Firstly, it rigorously demonstrates that many published LLM leaderboard claims regarding model superiority are statistically *unresolved* when subjected to appropriate paired-test diagnostics, multiplicity correction, clustering adjustments, and anytime-valid testing. The reported percentage-point gaps, even if nominally significant by a p-value, often lack the statistical power required to be reliably detected at conventional Type-I and Type-II error rates under the actual evaluation design. This finding challenges the implicit confidence placed on current leaderboard rankings, particularly for models with closely matched performance.

Secondly, the paper identifies and quantifies a specific misuse pattern in power analysis. **Lemma 1** and its empirical validation (Table 1) show that common power calculators, when misapplied by using an unpaired sample size formula and then adjusting by (1-ρ) for paired designs, can silently underestimate the required sample size by approximately a factor of two in close-comparison regimes. This insight is crucial for practitioners, enabling them to correctly estimate sample sizes and avoid underpowered studies. The provision of the explicit second-order constant C(p, ρ) allows for a more precise assessment of when this shortcut is unreliable.

Thirdly, the methodological integration of classical statistical tools into a cohesive framework (the resolution ratio *q* and the `llm-power` package) provides benchmark designers and leaderboard maintainers with a practical, interpretable diagnostic. This moves beyond simply reporting a p-value to explicitly state *what gaps can be distinguished from sampling noise* at specified error tolerances, reflecting the true resolution capability of a benchmark. The framework's ability to incorporate real-world complexities like clustering (demonstrated by the MMLU-Pro reanalysis, where clustering raised unresolved pairs from 4/9 to 6/9) and the continuous nature of leaderboard updates (anytime-valid testing, which flagged an additional unresolved pair) enhances the robustness of LLM evaluation.

The potential impact is to foster a more statistically informed and transparent culture in LLM evaluation. By making resolution diagnostics explicit, the research encourages:
*   **Better Benchmark Design**: Designers can use N* to determine appropriate sample sizes *prospectively* to ensure their benchmarks have the desired resolution.
*   **Improved Leaderboard Interpretation**: Users can interpret leaderboard rankings with a clearer understanding of the statistical confidence (or lack thereof) supporting pairwise comparisons, especially for adjacent ranks.
*   **Reduced Misinformation**: Avoiding unsupported claims of superiority based on underpowered evaluations can lead to more accurate headlines and product decisions.

While the work primarily focuses on binary accuracy and is validated on specific public benchmarks, its methodological principles are generalizable. Future work aims to extend the framework to graded metrics, pairwise-preference leaderboards (e.g., Chatbot Arena), multi-arm power calculations, and more sophisticated clustering models, further strengthening the statistical foundation of LLM evaluation. The paper underscores that statistical resolvability is a prerequisite for meaningful comparisons, even for benchmarks with high construct validity.
