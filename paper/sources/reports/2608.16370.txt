## Research Paper Analysis: What Does Context Compression Cost an Agent? Interaction Costs Unrevealed by Task-Completion Metrics

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research paper was authored by Shuyu Liu, affiliated with Chongqing University of Science and Technology. The work was accepted at the COLM 2026 Workshop on Efficient Reasoning, indicating its anticipated publication in 2026.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid accumulation of trajectories by long-horizon agents often results in context windows exceeding their capacity. To address this, modern runtimes commonly employ context compression techniques, such as summarization, sliding windows, or retrieval filters. The prevailing paradigm for evaluating the success of such compression is whether it preserves task performance while reducing context cost. Existing evaluations predominantly focus on whether information survives compression, is recoverable, or is utilized by the agent, often reporting task completion alongside aggregate metrics like tokens or runtime. This is characterized as assessing what compression *preserves* or *achieves*.

This research identifies a significant gap in this landscape: the lack of measurement and understanding of the *runtime interaction cost* an agent incurs when it must actively *reacquire* state that was dropped or compressed away. Prior work has acknowledged the insufficiency of task completion alone as an evaluation metric, noting that it can obscure process-level variance, failure modes, or architectural costs under intact contexts. However, these earlier works do not specifically address the cost associated with compression-induced state loss or the agent's behavioral response to such loss.

This paper positions itself as a complement to existing evaluation methods by asking a fifth question: "what does it cost the agent to re-acquire what was dropped, and is that cost visible to standard metrics?" It distinguishes its contribution from "lost in compaction" scenarios, where information is present but ignored, by focusing on situations where state is genuinely absent. The study provides a controlled, empirical demonstration that task completion, while crucial, can be an incomplete measure of the actual runtime cost, potentially failing to reveal substantial interaction overheads. It introduces a diagnostic for this reacquisition cost, which has implications for the design and evaluation of LLM-based agents.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to comprehensively quantify the runtime interaction cost incurred by an agent due to context compression, specifically focusing on the effort expended to reacquire state that was dropped. A central motivation is to determine whether this reacquisition cost is adequately captured by, or even visible through, conventional task-completion metrics.

The authors detail several key motivations and objectives:

1.  **Expose Incompleteness of Task-Completion Metrics**: The core motivation is the assertion that task completion is an incomplete measure for evaluating context compression. It is argued that compression can substantially increase an agent's interaction cost—defined as the reacquisition of dropped state—while leaving task completion statistically unchanged. The research aims to formalize and empirically demonstrate this "non-identifiability," where completion-only evaluation acts as a lossy projection of the full evaluation outcome, missing significant interaction costs.
2.  **Quantify Reacquisition Cost**: The study seeks to introduce a controlled runtime measurement protocol designed specifically to isolate and measure the reacquisition cost of context compression within a bounded-horizon agent. This involves decomposing agent tool calls into "retrieval" (re-acquiring external state) versus "execution" (performing task operations) to directly observe where the agent's interaction budget is spent.
3.  **Investigate Mechanistic Decomposition of Cost**: The research aims to mechanistically decompose the hidden cost along a "recoverability axis." This involves differentiating between externally queryable task state (D) and history-dependent state (R), and exploring how the loss of R might inflate defensive re-querying of D. Through oracle interventions, the availability of dropped state is manipulated to causally link state absence to reacquisition behavior.
4.  **Analyze Behavioral Relevance of Retained State**: A specific objective is to use retention interventions as causal probes. This allows for separating how much state is retained from which state is retained, and whether the retained content is valid and task-relevant. This investigation aims to understand the granularity at which retained state impacts the agent's interaction cost, exploring the roles of content validity, selection policies (e.g., random vs. hindsight oracle), and budget constraints.
5.  **Characterize Cost Dependencies and Boundaries**: The work aims to characterize the boundary conditions under which this reacquisition cost manifests, including its dependence on the specific large language model (LLM) used, the task regime (how much execution-relevant state needs discovery), and the environment's recoverability structure. An external probe in a different environment (ALFWorld) is used to test if the reacquisition signature is an intrinsic consequence of context shortening or environment-dependent.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the true overheads of context compression, providing a diagnostic tool for runtime designers to identify and mitigate costs that go unrevealed by traditional success metrics.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a controlled, empirical approach to isolate and measure the reacquisition cost of context compression. The methodology is structured around a formal definition of evaluation outcomes, a specialized agent and task environment, various context compression conditions and oracle interventions, and a robust statistical analysis protocol.

**Formalization of Evaluation Outcome:**
The evaluation outcome for a context-management condition `m` is defined as a triple `Y(m) = (Q(m), C_R(m), C_E(m))`, where:
*   `Q`: Task completion rate.
*   `C_R`: Number of retrieval tool calls (reflecting state re-acquisition effort).
*   `C_E`: Number of execution tool calls (reflecting task work).
The sum `C = C_R + C_E` is defined as the tool-level interaction cost. The paper formalizes completion-centric evaluation as a projection `π_Q(Y) = Q`, arguing that this projection can lead to non-identifiability where distinct outcomes with different interaction costs map to the same completion value.

**Agent and Task Environment:**
*   **Agent**: A minimal tool-using agent is employed.
*   **Environment**: A deterministic project-planning environment named `IRBench`.
    *   **Tasks**: 10 tasks, each with hidden execution constraints (e.g., ordering rules, resource holds, fail-until-success counts) revealed only through interaction.
    *   **Resources**: A small set of single-capacity resources.
    *   **Tools**: Four tools are provided: `get task info`, `check dependency`, `query resource` (classified as **retrieval** tools, used for obtaining environment state), and `execute` (classified as an **execution** tool, for performing tasks and revealing constraints). This clear classification is central to measuring reacquisition cost.
    *   **Interaction Horizon**: Fixed at 24 turns; each turn comprises one model call and any tool results. Task completion is measured by tasks finished within this horizon.
    *   **Task Regimes**: Two regimes, varying the density of hidden, execution-relevant facts:
        *   **High-IR**: Constraints are revealed only during execution and are costly to lose.
        *   **Low-IR**: Multi-hop dependency chains exist, but state is largely re-derivable from the public task graph.
    *   **Determinism**: The environment is deterministic, allowing exact replay of conditions on identical task sets.

**Context Conditions and Oracle Interventions:**
All context conditions are applied online, with the context rebuilt at each turn.
*   **Full**: The complete trajectory is provided as the baseline.
*   **Sliding**: A compression operator that maintains a token budget (`r^-1` of the trajectory) by greedily keeping the most recent turns and dropping earlier turns outright. Compression ratios `r` of 1.7x, 2.5x, 5x, and 10x are used. This tests genuine state absence.
*   **Summary**: Uses the same token budget as `Sliding`, but dropped early turns are replaced by a deterministic, extractive "fact digest." This digest preserves observed state facts while discarding reasoning text, serving as a controlled comparison to `Sliding`.
*   **Oracle Intervention**: Involves injecting specific dropped state back into the `Sliding` context as a trailing message. This state is categorized into:
    *   `D*`: Externally queryable state (e.g., task graph), recoverable through more tool calls.
    *   `R*`: History-dependent state (e.g., revealed constraints, failure counts), not recoverable by a single public query. This intervention causally manipulates state availability.

**Retention Interventions (Causal Probes):**
These interventions investigate the impact of *which* and *how much* state is retained, and its *validity*. Applied at a fixed compaction point (turn 10), they extract state atoms and render a compressed prefix under varying digest budgets `B ∈ {265, 100, 50}` tokens.
*   **Selection Rules**: Various rules for selecting atoms for the digest, including `RAR-D` (D-type only), `RAR-R` (R-type only), `RAR-All`, `RAR-TypeAware` (R-first), `Random`, `Recent`, and `Hindsight` (an offline reference oracle).
*   **D-Irrelevant**: A critical control where D-type atoms are replaced with *fabricated out-of-universe entities* while R-content, format, and budget are held constant. This tests the behavioral relevance of valid content.

**Models Evaluated:**
Three large language models were evaluated:
*   DeepSeek (deepseek-v4-flash): Subjected to the full compression severity sweep.
*   Qwen (qwen3.7-plus): Evaluated at a fixed 5x compression ratio.
*   GPT-5.5: Evaluated at a fixed 5x compression ratio.
All models were used with a temperature of 0.3, without explicit extended-thinking modes, and accessed via an OpenAI-compatible interface.

**External Environment Probe (ALFWorld):**
To assess environment dependence, a comparative study was conducted in ALFWorld, a household instruction-following domain. The same `Full` vs. `Sliding` contrast was applied, with actions categorized into retrieval-like, execution-like, and navigation.

**Protocol and Analysis:**
*   **Runs**: Each condition typically involved 10 seeds (10 distinct task instances per seed), leading to 100 task-level observations per condition. Retention interventions used an extended 20 paired seeds.
*   **Statistical Analysis**: Per-seed paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used, along with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals on per-seed differences.
*   **Multiple Comparison Control**: For primary comparisons (Full vs. Sliding on completion, tools, retrieval calls), Holm–Bonferroni correction was applied at `α = 0.05` across the family of six retrieval comparisons (three models × two regimes).

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the interaction costs of context compression and the limitations of completion-centric evaluation.

**1. Compression Raises Reacquisition Cost Before Completion Degradation:**
*   **DeepSeek (High-IR severity sweep)**: Task completion remained relatively stable through moderate compression ratios, with a minor increase at 1.7x (87% vs. 83% Full). Significant completion degradation (10x: -17pp, p = .016) was observed only at the most aggressive ratio. In contrast, retrieval calls were significantly elevated at a milder 5x compression ratio (p = .002). Overall tool calls increased monotonically from 2.5x onward (+37.6 calls at 10x), with this increase almost entirely attributed to retrieval calls (+41.0 calls) while execution calls remained stable or decreased. This indicates that compression primarily drives increased reacquisition rather than increased task work.
*   **DeepSeek (Low-IR)**: Under this regime, completion remained near-lossless (100% until 10x), yet tool calls and retrieval increased substantially, mirroring the High-IR pattern. This suggests that even when state is re-derivable, the agent still incurs reacquisition costs, although these do not translate into completion loss within the given horizon.

**2. What is Retained Matters More Than the Ratio (Operator Contrast):**
*   At the same 5x compression ratio on DeepSeek (High-IR), the `Extractive Summary` operator (fact-preserving digest) achieved near-lossless completion (83% vs. 80% Full) with minimal increase in tool calls (39.0 vs. 37.4 Full).
*   Conversely, the `Sliding` operator (dropping old turns) degraded completion (72%) and tripled retrieval calls (55.1).
*   This demonstrates that the *content* of what is preserved is more critical than the compression ratio itself, confirming that operators preserving state facts can mitigate costs.

**3. Restoring Dropped State Reduces Reacquisition (Oracle Intervention):**
*   Injecting `D*` (externally queryable task graph state) into the `Sliding` context (DeepSeek High-IR, 5x) removed most of the compensatory retrieval (72.9 to 35.8 tool calls, -51%, p = .002) and recovered most of the completion gap (66% to 80%).
*   Injecting `R*` (history-dependent state) improved completion (+12pp) but left retrieval cost largely unchanged (72.9 to 69.0), as `R*` is not directly queryable and its absence still drives defensive re-querying of `D`.
*   This provides causal evidence that absence of execution-relevant state increases reacquisition, and its restoration reduces this behavior.

**4. The Cost Generalizes, but Completion Outcome Does Not (Three Models):**
*   Across all six model-regime comparisons at 5x compression, retrieval tool calls increased. Five of these six increases remained statistically significant after Holm–Bonferroni correction. Execution calls remained approximately stable in five of six comparisons.
*   In contrast, none of the completion changes at 5x reached statistical significance in any of the six cells (all p ≥ 0.125).
*   **GPT-5.5 (High-IR)** provided a sharp illustration: completion was statistically unchanged (80% → 85%, p = 1.0) while retrieval calls roughly tripled (+42.9 calls, p = .002).
*   This highlights that reacquisition cost consistently increases under compression, but its translation into a measurable completion loss varies significantly across models, demonstrating that interaction cost and task completion are distinct, non-interchangeable outcomes.

**5. Retention Interventions Reveal Content Validity and Budget Dependence:**
*   **Selection Null**: Within the pool of real, task-relevant D-state atoms, fine-grained selection policies (e.g., `RAR-TypeAware` vs. `Random` or `Recent`) showed little marginal effect on retrieval cost or completion. Random selection effectively matched the offline `Hindsight` oracle (ΔC_R = +0.10, 95% CI [−2.3, +2.3]).
*   **Content Intervention (D-Irrelevant)**: Replacing real D-type atoms with semantically irrelevant, fabricated state (DeepSeek High-IR, B=265) sharply increased retrieval by 57% (+11.5 calls, p < 0.001) while completion remained statistically unchanged (p = 0.41). This demonstrates that state *content validity* is behaviorally load-bearing for the agent.
*   **Budget Dependence**: This content effect was strongly budget-gated. It was prominent at loose budgets (B=265) where the digest dominated the context window, but became null at tighter budgets (B=100) where the agent relied more on recent turns.
*   **Cross-model Replication**: The content-relevance effect replicated on GPT-5.5 at the loose budget (B=265), confirming it is not model-specific, though the magnitude and specific behavioral expression (e.g., reduced execution calls for GPT-5.5) could be model-dependent.

**6. External Environment Boundary (ALFWorld):**
*   Applying the same `Sliding` compression operator in ALFWorld produced no retrieval surge (paired ∆(retrieval-like) ≈ 0). In this environment, relevant state could be re-observed directly via available interaction actions.
*   This finding indicates that the reacquisition signature is environment-dependent, emerging only when execution-relevant state becomes unavailable and cannot be easily reacquired through normal interaction.

**7. Interaction Budget:**
*   The fixed 24-turn horizon was a binding constraint in most High-IR conditions, indicating that compression reduces actual task progress within this fixed budget rather than solely introducing turn pressure.
*   Compressed agents frequently exceeded an auxiliary tool budget diagnostic, underscoring the substantial interaction overhead.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of LLM agent behavior and evaluation by introducing a nuanced perspective on the costs associated with context compression. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

1.  **Refined Agent Evaluation Paradigms**: The core finding—that task completion is an incomplete and potentially misleading metric for context compression—challenges conventional evaluation practices. By formalizing and empirically demonstrating the non-identifiability of interaction cost through completion, the paper advocates for a multi-dimensional evaluation approach that jointly considers task completion and granular interaction costs (specifically, retrieval vs. execution tool calls). This could lead to the development of more robust benchmarks and evaluation frameworks that provide a holistic view of agent performance.

2.  **Diagnostic for Runtime Designers**: The study offers a concrete diagnostic tool for runtime designers. Instead of merely asking "which compression ratio is safe," designers can now ask "which execution-relevant state, if dropped, would the agent spend its budget re-acquiring?" This shift enables targeted optimizations for context management strategies. The protocol is generalizable, offering a checklist for evaluating any context-management strategy by instrumenting tool calls and comparing against oracle interventions.

3.  **Mechanistic Insights into Agent Behavior**: The mechanistic decomposition of interaction cost reveals that compression primarily leads to increased reacquisition of externally queryable task state (D) rather than an increase in core task execution. This insight into the "re-query loop"—where agents expend budget to recover lost D-state—is crucial for understanding the internal dynamics and bottlenecks of LLM agents under memory constraints.

4.  **Guidance for Context Management Strategy Design**:
    *   **Prioritize State Preservation**: The findings suggest that preserving externally queryable task state (D) and compact, history-dependent state (R) is critical to reduce reacquisition overhead.
    *   **Content Validity Over Fine-Grained Selection**: For D-state, ensuring that retained content is *valid and task-relevant* is far more important than sophisticated, fine-grained selection among valid atoms. This simplifies design choices for compression algorithms by highlighting that coarse distinctions (real vs. irrelevant content) have greater behavioral impact.
    *   **Budget-Gated Effects**: The observation that content relevance is budget-gated—only becoming significant when the digest occupies a substantial portion of the context window—provides practical guidance for dynamic context management, suggesting that recency can be a sufficient strategy for very tight budgets.

5.  **Understanding Boundary Conditions**: The research emphasizes that the magnitude, and even the presence, of reacquisition cost is conditional on the specific LLM, task regime (e.g., recoverability of state), and the environment's interaction structure. This underscores that there is no universal "cost of compression," but rather a complex interplay of factors, providing a richer context for interpreting compression evaluation results across diverse domains.

6.  **Complementary to Existing Theories**: The work clearly delineates its contribution from other lines of research, such as attention-dilution effects ("lost in compaction"). By isolating a distinct failure mode where state is genuinely *absent* and must be re-fetched, it offers a complementary perspective, implying that runtime optimizations may need to address multiple, separable sources of degradation.

In summary, this paper provides a robust framework and empirical evidence for measuring and understanding the hidden interaction costs of context compression. It aims to equip researchers and developers with a more complete diagnostic for evaluating agent performance, thereby fostering the development of more efficient and robust long-horizon LLM agents.
