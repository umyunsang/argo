### Research Report: HarnessOpt-Bench: Evaluating LLMs at Harness Optimization

#### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this research paper are Varun Ursekar, Apaar Shanker, Yash Maurya, Shehab Yasser, Vijay S. Kalmath, Veronica Chatrath, and Yuan (Emily) Xue. All authors are affiliated with Scale AI.

#### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The increasing deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) within agentic systems necessitates effective methods for maximizing their capabilities. These capabilities depend not solely on the foundational model weights but significantly on the surrounding "harness," which encompasses prompts, tools, control flow, memory, and orchestration code. Prior research has demonstrated that identical LLMs can exhibit varying performance depending on their harness configuration, underscoring the importance of harness design.

This context has led to the emergence of "harness optimization," which involves the iterative and evaluative improvement of a harness within a defined budget. A growing area of inquiry explores whether AI systems themselves can automate this optimization process, contributing to the broader field of self-improving agents, meta-optimization, and automated research. Existing approaches to automated optimization vary. Some methods, such as DSPy, focus on prompt optimization while keeping the program fixed. Others, like ShinkaEvolve and GEPA, integrate LLMs as mutation operators within larger evolutionary algorithms. A distinct line of work, exemplified by VERO and MetaHarness, employs coding agents as end-to-end optimizers capable of directly editing the harness codebase.

A critical differentiation in this landscape is the cost and stochasticity of evaluation. While traditional code changes can be cheaply verified by test suites, assessing the impact of a harness change often requires running a stochastic agent across multiple test cases, incurring substantial cost and yielding noisy feedback. This scenario demands an optimizer capable of diagnosing failures from incomplete evidence, implementing system-level changes, managing limited budgets, distinguishing genuine improvement from noise, and making deployment decisions. The research paper argues that this "expensive and noisy evaluation" regime is increasingly relevant as AI systems grow in complexity.

Despite the recognized importance of automated harness optimization, the research community has lacked a standardized protocol for measuring the performance of frontier LLMs in this task. Existing evaluations often conflate the optimizer model, its coding harness, the target agent, and the specific scoring protocol, making direct comparisons difficult. HARNESSOPT-BENCH aims to address this gap by providing a controlled and reproducible benchmark, enabling a more granular understanding of LLM capabilities in this demanding, multi-faceted optimization problem that combines long-horizon reasoning, interaction with diverse tool ecosystems, and interpretation of stochastic system behavior.

#### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce HARNESSOPT-BENCH, a controlled benchmark designed to evaluate how effectively frontier LLMs perform at end-to-end harness optimization, particularly under conditions of expensive and stochastic evaluation.

The motivation behind developing HARNESSOPT-BENCH stems from several key observations and requirements:

*   **Crucial Role of Harnesses:** As LLMs become integral to agentic systems, their performance is increasingly dictated by the quality and design of their harnesses. Optimizing these harnesses is thus a critical pathway to enhancing the capabilities of AI systems.
*   **Demand for Automated Optimization:** The ability of AI systems to automate their own improvement through harness optimization represents a significant leap towards more autonomous and capable AI. This capability is complex, requiring advanced reasoning, debugging, and strategic decision-making.
*   **Lack of Standardized Evaluation:** Prior work on harness optimization has typically relied on diverse, method-specific evaluation protocols, including varying target agents, seed harnesses, budgets, disclosure policies, and scoring metrics. This inconsistency makes it challenging to compare the true underlying capabilities of different optimizer models or approaches.
*   **Disentangling Performance Factors:** To accurately assess LLM performance in harness optimization, it is necessary to separate the contributions of the optimizer model itself from its surrounding coding harness, the target agent being optimized, and the evaluation protocol. HARNESSOPT-BENCH seeks to isolate these factors through a controlled design.
*   **Addressing Expensive and Stochastic Evaluation:** The benchmark is specifically motivated by the need to evaluate optimizers in scenarios where reliable evaluation is costly and noisy. This simulates real-world challenges where optimizers must reason from incomplete evidence, manage limited budgets, and robustly identify genuine improvements amidst variability.
*   **Answering Key Research Questions (RQs):** The work is motivated by specific questions that a controlled benchmark can answer:
    *   **RQ1:** Can frontier models be distinguished based on their performance in harness optimization?
    *   **RQ2:** What are the specific limitations or shortcomings of current optimizers in this task?
    *   **RQ3:** How much does the optimizer’s own coding harness contribute to performance relative to the underlying LLM model?

By establishing a common protocol and trusted execution environment, HARNESSOPT-BENCH aims to transform harness engineering from an ad-hoc demonstration into a quantifiable and reproducible research target, fostering systematic development and improvement of AI systems that can optimize other AI agents.

#### 4. Methodology and Approach

The HARNESSOPT-BENCH methodology is centered around a controlled, reproducible protocol for evaluating harness optimization, summarized by Algorithm 1 in the paper. This protocol defines the interaction between an optimizer and a target agent's harness under specific constraints.

**4.1 The Optimization Problem Defined**

*   **Candidates and Invariants:** A candidate harness (H) is an executable codebase editable by the optimizer, comprising elements like prompts, tool definitions, memory, and control flow. The optimizer begins with a pinned seed harness (H0) and can modify, add, or delete files, adhering to a fixed execution interface and immutable paths. Each task specifies invariants (θ), which include the target models (M) available to the candidate, the environment (E(x)) for each test case (x), and the verifier (V) that assigns a score between 0 and 1 to a completed trajectory. The optimizer cannot modify these invariants.
*   **Evaluation and Disclosure:** Test cases are divided into disjoint development (Ddev), validation (Dval), and test (Dtest) sets. An evaluation of harness H on a case x yields a stochastic trajectory (τ), which is scored by the verifier V. The expected score on a partition D is defined as Eθ(H; D). During search, the optimizer can request an evaluation Fθ(H, Q) on a subset Q from Ddev or Dval, which returns an estimated aggregate performance (ŝ) and per-case outcomes and execution traces (φ). A partition-specific disclosure policy (πD) governs what information the optimizer observes: Ddev provides detailed traces for diagnosis, Dval provides only an aggregate score for selection, and Dtest remains entirely inaccessible during the search phase, being evaluated by a trusted server only after a final candidate is nominated.
*   **Budget:** Each evaluation request incurs a cost vector (cj), and the optimizer must operate within a fixed budget vector (B). In HARNESSOPT-BENCH, this budget includes caps of 100 evaluation calls per partition, four full case passes on both development and validation partitions, and a cap on total expendable target-model tokens. The optimizer's own inference is metered for observability but is not capped in the presented experiments.
*   **Optimizer:** Any program satisfying Algorithm 1's interface, which receives H0, interacts with Fθ under πD and B, produces candidate harnesses, and nominates a final candidate (H+). In this work, optimizers are LLMs operating through a coding harness.
*   **Objective:** The optimizer's goal is to maximize the expected improvement of its nominated candidate (H+) over the pinned seed (H0) on the held-out test partition. Since raw improvement scores may not be comparable across tasks, a normalized gain (g) is used: `g = (Eθ(H+) - Eθ(H0)) / (1 - Eθ(H0))`, where negative values indicate performance worse than the seed.

**4.2 The HARNESSOPT-BENCH Suite**

The benchmark suite comprises four downstream tasks: OfficeQA Pro, BrowseComp-Plus, Terminal-Bench 2.0, and GAIA.
*   **Seed Harnesses:** Each task has a pinned, small, and deliberately untuned Python seed harness (H0), designed to have clear headroom for improvement. For instance, OfficeQA's seed is a ~130-line agent, while GAIA's seed is a non-functional stub.
*   **Target Models:** Each task uses one pinned target model (e.g., deepseek-v4-flash for OfficeQA, gpt-5.4-mini for GAIA), chosen to ensure the seed's baseline performance falls into a measurable mid-range.
*   **Evaluation Noise:** The noise in repeated evaluations is quantified by a task-specific "resolution band" to distinguish meaningful performance differences from random variation.
*   **Infrastructure:** The evaluation leverages a trusted execution environment, building on VERO. Optimizers run in isolated sandboxes with restricted access to evaluation results and target harness files. Each target agent rollout occurs in an ephemeral sandbox to mitigate environmental drift and ensure consistent execution.

**4.3 Experimental Setup**

*   **Task Grid:** The core experiments evaluate five frontier LLMs (claude-opus-5, claude-sonnet-5, gpt-5.6-sol, gpt-5.6-terra, kimi-k3) from three developers across all four tasks.
*   **Coding Harnesses:** Each LLM acts through two types of coding harnesses: a shared "opencode" harness (fixed across all models) and its "native harness" (e.g., claude-code for Claude models, codex for GPT models, kimi-cli for Kimi). This yields 10 core optimizer configurations.
*   **Total Runs:** A total of 111 scored optimizer runs were conducted.
*   **Additional Harnesses:** For the GAIA task, two extra harnesses (goose and mini-swe-agent) were tested with all models to broaden the analysis of scaffold sensitivity.
*   **Model Progress Tracking:** Earlier releases of Claude Opus and GPT models were evaluated on OfficeQA to provide a "capability ladder" for tracking model advancements.
*   **Scoring:** Each held-out evaluation is the mean of three attempts per test case. Each optimizer configuration is run twice. The optimizer's inference token usage is metered but not capped to assess maximum achievable performance.
*   **Analysis Protocol:** Normalized gain (g) is the primary outcome. Task-specific resolution bands are used to interpret performance differences. A composite model score, LSS-λ, is derived by fitting an additive model over task and model effects, representing a model's task-adjusted mean performance relative to the grand mean of evaluated models under the shared harness.

#### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using HARNESSOPT-BENCH yielded several findings regarding the capabilities of frontier LLMs in harness optimization:

**5.1 Distinguishing Frontier Models**

*   **Model vs. Coding Harness Contribution:** The choice of optimizer model demonstrated a larger impact on normalized gain than the choice of coding harness. Changing the optimizer model, while holding task and harness constant, resulted in an average gain change of 0.142. In contrast, changing the coding harness, with task and model fixed, resulted in an average gain change of 0.079. Both magnitudes exceeded the respective task resolution bands, indicating discernible differences.
*   **Performance Spread:** The strongest optimizer configurations achieved substantial gains, capturing approximately two-thirds of the available headroom on OfficeQA and half on BrowseComp-Plus. Conversely, the weakest configurations performed close to the baseline, often unresolved from zero on tasks like BrowseComp-Plus and Terminal-Bench. While extremes separated clearly, differences among intermediate configurations frequently fell within their round-to-round variation, suggesting performance tiers rather than a strict, fine-grained ranking.

**5.2 Tracking Model Progress**

*   **Generational Improvement:** Across successive GPT model releases evaluated on OfficeQA, a monotonic increase in normalized gain was observed, rising from +0.03 to +0.49. Three out of four generational steps in this series exceeded the task's resolution band, indicating measurable progress. For Claude Opus releases on OfficeQA, gain ranged from +0.37 to +0.59. While not strictly monotonic, the overall spread from the earliest to the latest release also exceeded the task’s resolution band, demonstrating an evolution in capability.

**5.3 Where Current Optimizers Fall Short**

Analysis of optimizer search trajectories revealed insights into current limitations:

*   **Broader Search Associated with Gain:** A positive correlation was observed between the fraction of pre-registered harness "levers" (e.g., prompt, context management, tool schema) touched during search and the achieved normalized gain. Spearman ρ values ranged from +0.34 to +0.88 across tasks. This suggests that a more extensive exploration of the harness modification space is associated with better outcomes, though it does not distinguish between exploratory breadth and retained changes, or total modification volume.
*   **Limited Use of Trace Reading:** The proportion of optimizer actions dedicated to reading detailed evaluation traces was negatively associated with gain, with ρ ranging from −0.31 to −0.64 across the four tasks. Detailed trace spans were requested infrequently (16 times across 7 of 111 runs), indicating that optimizers predominantly rely on per-case score summaries. This suggests that for the benchmark's tasks, aggregate feedback may be sufficient, or current optimizers may not effectively leverage granular diagnostic information.
*   **Case-Pass Budgets Are Binding:** Optimizers were found to be constrained more by the budget for case passes than by the budget for evaluation calls. The median optimizer utilized only 8 evaluation calls (4% of the cap) but 82% of its case allowance. Furthermore, 55 out of 100 runs exhausted at least one partition’s case budget, whereas the call cap was rarely reached.
*   **Optimistic Validation Scores:** The best validation scores observed during search generally overstated the performance achieved on the held-out test partition. Most submitted candidates' test scores were lower than their best validation scores, highlighting the necessity of a held-out test set to measure actual generalization and realized gain, rather than selection-induced overfitting or validation-test mismatch.

**5.4 The Effect of the Optimizer’s Own Harness**

*   **No Consistent Advantage for Native Harnesses:** Across 20 model-task pairs where both shared (opencode) and native harnesses were evaluated, the shared harness outperformed the native harness in 11 cases, while the native harness won in 9 cases, with no ties. This indicates that native harnesses do not offer a consistent performance advantage for harness optimization.
*   **Heterogeneous Effects:** While the aggregate outcome was a near-tie, differences between shared and native harness performance exceeded the task's resolution band in 11 pairs, implying that the lack of overall advantage is due to varied effects rather than uniformly small ones. For example, on the GAIA task (where multiple harnesses were tested), both GPT models showed significantly better performance (four to five resolution bands) when using their native `codex` harness compared to `opencode`. In contrast, the Claude and Kimi models performed similarly with their native harnesses as with `opencode`.

#### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research, through the introduction of HARNESSOPT-BENCH, establishes harness optimization as a quantifiable and discriminative capability for Large Language Models. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

*   **Standardized Evaluation:** By providing a common, controlled, and reproducible benchmark, HARNESSOPT-BENCH enables systematic measurement and comparison of optimizer models, coding harnesses, and search algorithms. This addresses the prior limitation of inconsistent evaluation protocols, shifting harness engineering from ad-hoc demonstrations to an empirically verifiable scientific domain.
*   **Advancing AI Agent Development:** The benchmark directly supports research into building more capable AI agents. By identifying which LLMs excel at modifying and improving their own or other agents' harnesses, it provides a clear pathway for developing next-generation self-improving AI systems.
*   **Understanding LLM Meta-Capabilities:** The findings offer insights into the meta-capabilities of frontier LLMs, specifically their ability to reason about and modify codebases, diagnose performance issues, and make strategic decisions under budget constraints and stochastic feedback. This contributes to a deeper understanding of LLM intelligence beyond direct task completion.
*   **Guiding Optimizer Design:** The results highlight specific areas for improvement in optimizer design. For instance, the observed positive correlation between search breadth and gain suggests that future optimizers could benefit from more extensive or structured exploration strategies. The limited use and negative association of detailed trace reading with gain indicate a need for optimizers that can more effectively process and act upon complex diagnostic information.
*   **Separating Model and Scaffold Contributions:** The finding that the optimizer model generally contributes more to performance than its coding harness helps to disentangle these intertwined factors. This allows researchers to prioritize improvements in the core LLM's reasoning and coding abilities while also recognizing the specific contexts where native tooling might offer a distinct advantage (as seen with GPT models on GAIA).
*   **Robust Evaluation Principles:** The implementation of a trusted execution environment, held-out test partitions, and strict budget enforcement sets a precedent for robust evaluation in agentic AI research. This mitigates risks of overfitting to visible scores and gaming the evaluation process, ensuring that measured improvements reflect genuine generalization.
*   **Foundation for Future Research:** HARNESSOPT-BENCH serves as a foundation for future investigations. Its limitations (e.g., fixed seeds, single target models, Python-only candidates) naturally suggest avenues for extending the benchmark to cover a broader range of complexities, languages, and agent architectures.

In conclusion, HARNESSOPT-BENCH represents a step in defining and measuring the capability of LLMs to reliably improve AI agents, moving towards a future where AI systems can autonomously enhance their own operational components.
