## Report on "Randomness in large language models: What researchers need to know (and report)"

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical and emerging challenge in contemporary academic research: the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into empirical workflows. LLMs are increasingly being used to generate data for research tasks such as classification, annotation, information extraction, and numerical scoring. This trend has significant implications for research transparency and reproducibility, which are fundamental tenets of academic integrity.

Traditional research practices and existing data and code policies, particularly in social sciences like economics and management, were largely established before the widespread adoption of generative AI. These policies provide limited guidance on how to report and verify data generated by probabilistic, "black-box" models. Consequently, LLM outputs are often treated as fixed measurements, similar to conventional data, despite their inherent variability. This discrepancy creates a gap in current reproducibility standards and raises concerns about the reliability of research findings that rely on such data.

The paper complements a growing body of literature examining the reproducibility and reliability of LLM-generated data. It builds upon findings by Kim (2026), which highlights the instability of coefficients and potential for misleading significance when relying on single LLM runs, and Wang and Wang (2025), which documents task-dependent reproducibility and improvements from aggregation. It also aligns with practical guidance from de Kok (2025) regarding archiving prompts and raw LLM outputs. This work specifically identifies and categorizes the multiple sources of non-determinism in LLM outputs, distinguishing between user-controllable and provider-controlled variations, and proposes concrete reporting and verification standards to address these issues. By doing so, it contributes to establishing a foundation for robust research practices in an era where LLM-generated data is becoming prevalent.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to establish that data generated by an LLM should be conceptualized and treated as draws from a distribution, rather than as fixed, deterministic measurements. This fundamental shift in perspective is motivated by the documented non-deterministic nature of LLM outputs, which can vary across repeated requests even when prompts and apparent model settings remain identical. The paper aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of this variability and its consequences for research, particularly in empirical studies where LLM outputs serve as inputs for subsequent analyses.

The authors articulate four specific contributions to achieve this objective:

1.  **Identification and Explanation of Variation Sources:** To systematically identify the primary sources of variation across repeated LLM calls, providing accessible explanations of the underlying mechanisms for a broad audience of empirical researchers. This includes both overt (e.g., deliberate sampling) and subtle (e.g., numerical rounding, silent model updates) factors.
2.  **Distinction of Control Levels:** To differentiate between sources of variation that researchers can directly control or influence (e.g., temperature settings in some models, local execution of open-weight models) and those that remain under the exclusive control of model providers (e.g., server infrastructure, hidden updates of proprietary models).
3.  **Empirical Illustration of Consequences:** To conduct an experiment that quantifies the practical implications of these variation sources in a typical research task, specifically sentiment annotation of corporate filings. This illustration aims to demonstrate how LLM output variability can propagate to downstream analytical results, such as regression coefficients and statistical significance.
4.  **Development of Practical Recommendations:** To propose actionable recommendations for both authors and journal data editors. For authors, these recommendations focus on how to report LLM usage in papers and prepare replication packages. For data editors, they provide guidance on assessing papers that utilize LLMs and their accompanying materials, aiming to facilitate the development of new reporting and verification standards.

The motivation for this work is underscored by the rapid and pervasive adoption of LLMs in academic research, as evidenced by their increasing mention in top-tier journal articles and surveys of economists. Many studies relying on LLM-generated variables are already in advanced stages of publication or have been published without adequate reporting of the probabilistic nature of the data. The authors emphasize the urgency of establishing clear reporting norms *before* ad hoc practices become entrenched, thereby ensuring the transparency, reproducibility, and ultimately, the credibility of research in this evolving landscape. The focus is specifically on scenarios where LLM output directly constitutes data used in analysis, as this poses the most direct and significant risk to reproducibility.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach combining literature review, empirical investigation, and the development of normative guidelines.

#### 4.1 Documentation of LLM Adoption

The authors first established the increasing prevalence of LLM use in academic research. They surveyed articles published between January 2020 and July 2026 in 145 top-tier (ranked 4 or 4\* in the Academic Journal Guide) economics and management journals. They used OpenAlex to search abstracts for 212 specific LLM names (listed in Appendix A) and generic terms such as "LLM," "large language model," "Gen AI," and "generative artificial intelligence." This quantitative analysis was complemented by a reference to a July 2026 NBER survey indicating widespread LLM adoption in economists' workflows.

#### 4.2 Identification of Randomness Sources

The paper systematically identifies and explains six distinct sources of non-determinism in LLM outputs, categorizing them based on their underlying mechanisms and the degree of user control:

1.  **Deliberate Sampling:** This refers to the probabilistic selection of tokens from a distribution derived from logit scores, controlled by a "temperature" parameter. A temperature of zero typically enforces greedy decoding (highest score), but this parameter is not always exposed or available to users in commercial APIs. A related control, the "seed," influences pseudo-random draws but is only effective under otherwise identical conditions.
2.  **Silent Model Updates:** Proprietary model providers may alter the underlying weights, safety guardrails, or system messages of an LLM without changing its public name or API endpoint. This makes it impossible to archive the exact model version used for data generation.
3.  **Floating-Point Rounding:** Due to finite precision arithmetic in computers, numerical calculations can lead to subtle variations, especially when dealing with numbers of vastly different magnitudes or when operations are reordered. This can affect LLM behavior when leading candidate tokens have nearly tied scores. Sources of reordering include:
    *   **Server Load:** Batching of multiple user requests can alter the order of operations.
    *   **Hardware Differences:** Variations in processors, drivers, numerical libraries, or precision settings across different machines in a data center.
    *   **Model Splitting:** Large models distributed across multiple processors may combine partial calculations in varying orders.
4.  **Expert Routing:** In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, tokens are routed to specialized components (experts). The availability of experts can depend on other concurrent requests, potentially leading to different processing paths for identical inputs if the preferred expert is unavailable.

The authors use Table 1 to summarize which of these sources survive a temperature of zero setting and which are controllable under local execution versus API access.

#### 4.3 Experimental Illustration with Corporate Filings

To demonstrate the practical consequences of LLM randomness, the authors conducted an experiment involving sentiment classification:

*   **Task:** Classifying the overall tone (positive, neutral, or negative) of excerpts from Item 1 and Item 1A of S&P 500 firms' 2025 annual 10-K filings.
*   **Sample:** 100 firms with the shortest excerpts were selected to manage API costs.
*   **Prompt:** A standardized, verbatim prompt was used, requesting a single-word classification ("positive," "negative," or "neutral").
*   **Repetitions:** The procedure was repeated 200 times for each model with the exact same prompt.
*   **Models:** Three LLMs were used:
    *   OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna (proprietary API, reasoning level "none," default non-zero temperature).
    *   DeepSeek V4-Pro (open-weight, API access, tested with T=0 and T=1).
    *   Google's Gemma 4 26B A4B (open-weight, locally deployed, tested with T=0 and T=1.5).
*   **Output Processing:** Sentiment responses were digitized as 1 (positive), 0 (neutral), and -1 (negative).
*   **Downstream Analysis:** For each of the 200 runs, a simple regression was performed: `annual stock returns = α + β × sentiment score + e`.
*   **Metric:** The distribution of the t-statistics for the sentiment coefficient (`β`) across the 200 runs was analyzed to quantify the variability in statistical significance.

#### 4.4 Proposed Reporting and Verification Standards

Based on their analysis and experimental findings, the authors propose a structured reporting standard for articles and replication packages (Table 2). This standard specifies "must have" and "good to have" items for inclusion in both the main paper and its accompanying replication package, covering aspects like model identification, run settings (prompts, generation parameters), raw outputs, variability reports, and cost statements.

Finally, they provide guidance for journal data editors on how to verify results from LLM-based pipelines. This involves two types of checks: reproducing results from deposited raw LLM outputs (skipping model execution) and replicating data generation itself using deposited prompts and settings.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the randomness of LLM outputs and their implications for empirical research:

#### 5.1 Rapid Adoption of LLMs in Academia

The analysis of journal articles revealed a substantial and rapid increase in the mention of LLMs in the titles and abstracts of papers published in leading economics and management journals. By July 2026, nearly 6% of papers in the sample mentioned LLMs, indicating their widespread adoption in research. The analysis also showed a dominant market share for closed-weight models, particularly from OpenAI and Google, suggesting that many researchers are using models with limited user control over internal mechanisms.

#### 5.2 Sources of Randomness and User Control

The paper systematically documented six sources of randomness:

*   **Deliberate Sampling:** This is the most direct and often controllable source, managed by the `temperature` parameter. Setting temperature to zero (greedy decoding) eliminates this specific source of randomness. However, many contemporary proprietary models (e.g., OpenAI GPT-5.6, Anthropic's latest models) no longer expose this parameter directly, instead offering "reasoning intensity" settings that manage sampling internally.
*   **Silent Model Updates:** Proprietary models can undergo undocumented changes to their underlying weights, safety features, or infrastructure, even when accessed via the same API endpoint and model name. This makes exact reproduction impossible over time.
*   **Floating Point Rounding:** Even with identical model weights and zero temperature, small numerical differences arising from server load, varying hardware configurations, or model splitting across processors can alter the order of operations. When leading token candidates have very similar scores, these minor numerical variations can lead to different token selections, propagating to entirely different subsequent outputs.
*   **Expert Routing:** In Mixture-of-Experts architectures, the routing of tokens to specialized components can be influenced by other simultaneous user requests in the same batch, leading to non-deterministic processing paths.

A critical distinction was drawn regarding user control: local execution of open-weight models offers the greatest control, allowing researchers to archive weights, fix batch sizes, preserve software environments, and document hardware. In contrast, commercial APIs offer minimal control over hardware, batching behavior, and silent model updates.

#### 5.3 Empirical Demonstration of Variability

The sentiment classification experiment on corporate 10-K filings provided concrete evidence of LLM output variability and its impact on downstream analyses:

*   **OpenAI GPT-5.6 Luna (Proprietary API, Default Temperature):** The 200 repeated runs yielded a wide distribution of t-statistics for the sentiment coefficient in the stock return regression (Figure 2). Approximately 5% of the t-statistics fell outside the 90% confidence significance threshold, implying that a statistically significant result could be reported in some runs purely by chance, while other runs would show no significance. This demonstrates how even with a fixed prompt, identical model, and task, the probabilistic nature of the output can lead to inconsistent statistical conclusions.
*   **DeepSeek V4-Pro (Open-Weight API):**
    *   With a non-zero temperature (T=1), the distribution of t-statistics was comparable in dispersion to the OpenAI results.
    *   When temperature was set to zero (T=0), the distribution became significantly more concentrated but *did not collapse to a single value*. Instead, the t-statistics took four distinct values. This residual variability was attributed to two out of 100 firms receiving different sentiment classifications across runs, highlighting that `temperature=0` removes deliberate sampling but not all other sources of randomness inherent to API-served models.
*   **Google Gemma 4 26B A4B (Open-Weight, Locally Deployed):**
    *   With a non-zero temperature (T=1.5), the t-statistics showed dispersion.
    *   Crucially, when temperature was set to zero (T=0), the distribution of t-statistics was *entirely concentrated at a single value*. This indicates fully deterministic outputs under the controlled conditions of local execution, demonstrating that deterministic output is achievable when researchers have full control over the computational environment.

These results empirically validate the paper's central thesis: LLM outputs should be treated as draws from a distribution. The experiment clearly differentiates between deliberate sampling (controlled by temperature) and residual randomness stemming from infrastructure, hardware, and model serving mechanisms, particularly in proprietary or API-based services.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries substantial significance for the scientific community, particularly in fields rapidly integrating large language models into their methodologies. Its potential impact spans several critical areas:

1.  **Establishing a New Paradigm for LLM Outputs:** The most fundamental significance is the clear articulation and empirical demonstration that LLM outputs are draws from a distribution rather than fixed measurements. This conceptual shift is essential for robust research design, analysis, and interpretation in studies utilizing LLM-generated data. It fundamentally challenges the prevailing, often implicit, assumption of determinism in LLM-based data collection.

2.  **Enhancing Research Reproducibility and Transparency:** The paper directly addresses the urgent need for new standards to ensure the reproducibility and transparency of LLM-powered research. By identifying specific sources of randomness and differentiating between controllable and uncontrollable factors, it provides a roadmap for researchers to better understand, mitigate, and report variability. This is crucial for maintaining the credibility of academic findings in an era of rapid technological adoption.

3.  **Guiding Authors on Reporting Practices:** The proposed reporting standard (Table 2) offers concrete, actionable guidance for authors. By outlining "must have" and "good to have" items for both the main paper and replication packages, it aims to standardize documentation practices. This includes explicit requirements for model identification, verbatim prompts, generation parameters, raw outputs, and variability assessments. Adherence to these guidelines will enable readers and reviewers to critically evaluate the reliability of LLM-generated data and the robustness of subsequent analyses.

4.  **Informing Journal Data Editors and Publishers:** The paper provides a framework for journal data editors and publishers to adapt existing reproducibility policies to the context of LLM usage. It suggests specific checks for verification teams, distinguishing between reproducing results from archived raw outputs and replicating the data generation process itself. This will facilitate the development of new protocols for assessing research that relies on LLMs, ensuring that published work meets appropriate standards of rigor and verifiability.

5.  **Promoting Best Practices in LLM Application:** The practical recommendations, such as setting temperature to zero when possible, not equating zero temperature with full determinism, and documenting the complete computational environment for local model execution, will guide researchers toward more responsible and rigorous application of LLMs. The suggestion to explore containerization (e.g., Docker) for local setups highlights a forward-looking approach to ensure the long-term preservation and reproducibility of specific LLM execution environments.

6.  **Fostering Critical Discussion and Standard Development:** The authors explicitly state their hope that these contributions will stimulate discussion among researchers, journal editors, and model developers. This paper serves as a foundational piece for the ongoing development of community-wide reporting and verification standards, which are essential for building a stable scientific infrastructure that incorporates LLM capabilities.

In summary, this research is significant because it systematically dissects a critical methodological challenge posed by LLMs, provides empirical evidence of its impact, and offers practical, concrete solutions for addressing it. Its widespread adoption could significantly elevate the quality and trustworthiness of research conducted using large language models.
