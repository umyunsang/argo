## Report on "Can AI Agents Synthesize Scientific Conclusions?"

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research paper "Can AI Agents Synthesize Scientific Conclusions?" was authored by a team of researchers from multiple institutions:
*   Hayoung Jung, Aleksandra Korolova, and Manoel Horta Ribeiro are affiliated with Princeton University.
*   Pedro Viana Diniz, José Reinaldo Corrêa Roveda, and Abner Fernandes da Silva are affiliated with Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais.
*   Haeun Jung is affiliated with Stony Brook University.
*   Enoch Tsai is affiliated with Hackensack Meridian School of Medicine.
*   Aleksandra Korolova and Manoel Horta Ribeiro are jointly advised on this work.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) agents capable of interacting with the open web to retrieve, analyze, and synthesize information represents an evolving area in natural language processing and AI research. These agents are increasingly employed to generate scientific conclusions, impacting decision-making in critical sectors such as health, science, and policy. Traditional information retrieval systems, like standard search engines, primarily provide relevant documents, leaving the synthesis task to the user. In contrast, modern agentic systems are designed to perform complex, long-horizon tasks, including evidence retrieval, source filtering, reconciliation of conflicting findings, evidence quality assessment, and the production of long-form, expert-level conclusions.

Prior research in evaluating AI capabilities in scientific domains often focuses on isolated or intermediate tasks. These include retrieval and citation grounding, summarization, short-form factuality assessments, or multiple-choice question answering. While valuable, these approaches do not fully capture the complexity and real-world challenges associated with synthesizing comprehensive, long-form scientific conclusions from diverse, open-web evidence. Some recent benchmarks have moved closer to evaluating open-web synthesis using expert-curated datasets; however, these datasets are typically small due to the high cost of expert curation, can become outdated rapidly, and frequently do not adequately address benchmark leakage. Benchmark leakage occurs when models are pre-trained on, or retrieve directly from, ground-truth artifacts, leading to an inflated perception of genuine synthesis capabilities.

This work addresses these gaps by introducing a large-scale, live benchmark designed for open-domain scientific conclusion synthesis. It specifically tackles the challenges of evaluating long-horizon tasks, mitigating benchmark leakage through a controlled clean-room environment, and employing a fine-grained, expert-validated factual evaluation methodology. By focusing on the full task of synthesizing scientific conclusions from open-web evidence, the research aims to provide a more comprehensive and ecologically valid assessment of AI agents' capabilities in this high-stakes domain.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to rigorously evaluate the capability of AI agents to synthesize scientific conclusions, particularly in high-stakes domains such as health, where reliable information is critical for consequential decisions. The authors identify a significant gap in current evaluation methodologies: existing benchmarks often fail to assess the full, long-horizon task of generating expert-level, long-form scientific conclusions from a vast and noisy open-web environment.

The motivation behind this work stems from several key observations:
*   **Increasing Reliance on AI Agents:** Individuals and institutions are increasingly delegating scientific synthesis tasks to AI systems. In healthcare, for instance, OpenAI reports billions of weekly ChatGPT messages related to healthcare, and specialized platforms like OpenEvidence are used as clinical AI copilots for critical decision-making. This widespread adoption necessitates a robust understanding of these agents' reliability.
*   **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** As detailed in the previous section, current benchmarks are often insufficient. They either focus on intermediate components (e.g., retrieval, summarization) rather than comprehensive synthesis, are too small, become rapidly outdated, or fail to account for data leakage. This makes it difficult to ascertain the true capabilities of AI agents in real-world scenarios.
*   **Risk of Data Leakage:** A major concern is that AI models, especially those with web access, may retrieve pre-existing ground-truth conclusions directly from the web rather than genuinely synthesizing information. This benchmark leakage can artificially inflate performance metrics, misleading researchers and users about the agents' actual synthesis abilities.
*   **Importance of Factual Quality:** In domains like health, scientific conclusions must be accurate, comprehensive, and non-contradictory. Errors, such as factual inaccuracies or omissions, can have significant negative consequences for clinical and policy decisions. Therefore, a granular evaluation of factual quality is essential.

To address these motivations, the research establishes three key objectives:
1.  **Develop SCICONBENCH:** Create a large-scale, live benchmark of 9,110 questions and expert-written conclusions derived from systematic reviews, specifically the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR). This benchmark is designed to be continuously updated to mitigate leakage and reflect current scientific knowledge.
2.  **Introduce SCICONHARNESS:** Design a "clean-room" evaluation harness that provides controlled web interaction tools to AI agents, preventing access to ground-truth artifacts (e.g., original CDSR articles) and thus ensuring a valid measurement of genuine synthesis capabilities rather than mere retrieval.
3.  **Establish a Factual Evaluation Pipeline:** Implement an expert-validated pipeline that decomposes conclusions into atomic facts and quantitatively measures factual precision (correctness) and factual recall (comprehensiveness), along with an F1 score for overall quality, utilizing Large Language Model (LLM)-based judges for scalability.

These objectives collectively aim to provide a more rigorous, realistic, and reliable framework for evaluating the advanced scientific reasoning and synthesis capabilities of AI agents.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces three main components to evaluate AI agents' scientific conclusion synthesis capabilities: SCICONBENCH (a live benchmark dataset), SCICONHARNESS (a clean-room evaluation environment), and a factual evaluation pipeline.

#### 4.1 SCICONBENCH Dataset Construction

SCICONBENCH is a large-scale, live benchmark comprising 9,107 question-answer pairs derived from the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR) as of January 1st, 2026. The CDSR is recognized as a "gold-standard" for evidence-based synthesis in health.
*   **Data Source:** Systematic reviews from the CDSR are used. Each review synthesizes evidence to answer a defined clinical or public health question, appraises evidence quality, and provides a concise conclusion.
*   **Live Benchmark:** To mitigate benchmark leakage, SCICONBENCH is designed to be continuously updated with new CDSR reviews, contrasting with static benchmarks.
*   **Data Preprocessing:** Each systematic review is converted into a structured question-answer unit. The "Objectives" section of the review is used as the basis for the question, and the "Authors' Conclusions" serves as the ground-truth answer.
*   **Question Generation:** Since Objectives are declarative, GPT-5-chat is employed to transform them into clinically grounded, sentence-style questions following the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) framework. This process leverages a few-shot prompt with examples and incorporates the "Background" section for context. Rule-based checks ensure well-formed questions.
*   **Validation:** Two medical students with clinical research experience independently validated a sample of generated questions. They assessed questions for Faithfulness (92%), PICO Completeness (92%), and Clarity & Answerability (96%), showing high inter-annotator agreement (Gwet’s AC1: 0.756–1.00).

#### 4.2 SCICONHARNESS Design

SCICONHARNESS is an evaluation harness designed to enforce a "clean-room setting" for evaluating AI agents, preventing models from accessing ground-truth systematic reviews during synthesis.
*   **Orchestration Engine:** Built using Ai2’s open-source `dr-agent-lib`, SCICONHARNESS orchestrates iterative model-tool interactions, managing model context and appending tool outputs.
*   **Controlled Web Tools:** Agents are equipped with three controlled web tools:
    *   `google_search`: Retrieves top search results via Serper API.
    *   `web_browse`: Fetches page text from a URL via Jina Browsing API (summarized by GPT-5-mini for efficiency).
    *   `paper_search`: Retrieves relevant paragraphs from open-access papers via Semantic Scholar Full-Text Search API.
*   **Clean-Room Protocol:** A filtering middleware inspects all tool outputs before they are returned to the model. It blocks:
    *   URLs from CDSR domains (e.g., `cochrane.org`, `cochranelibrary.com`).
    *   Result titles containing "cochrane" or matching a CDSR review title.
    *   Results published after the ground-truth review's publication date to prevent indirect leakage from derivative content.
*   **Validation:** Manual annotation of 150 tool outputs confirmed high precision (0.88–1.00) and recall (0.957–1.00) for the clean-room filtering, with all direct ground-truth CDSR articles successfully removed.

#### 4.3 Factual Quality Measurement Pipeline

To quantitatively evaluate the factual quality of generated conclusions, a multi-step pipeline is developed, which relies on atomic fact decomposition and LLM-based judges.
*   **Atomic Fact Decomposition:** Both generated and reference conclusions are broken down into "atomic facts"—self-contained statements containing single pieces of information. This pipeline consists of six steps:
    1.  **Preprocessing:** Tokenizes conclusions into sentences and filters non-informative ones.
    2.  **Decomposition:** Uses GPT-5.1 to break each sentence into atomic facts.
    3.  **Decontextualization:** Uses GPT-5.1 to resolve implicit references (e.g., pronouns) in facts for self-containment.
    4.  **Incomplete Fact Rewriting:** Uses GPT-5-mini to identify and rewrite incomplete facts (ambiguous concepts, missing comparisons, lack of conditions).
    5.  **Relevance Filtering:** Uses GPT-5-mini to filter facts for direct relevance to the original question.
    6.  **Redundancy Filtering:** Uses GPT-5-mini to remove redundant facts within a sentence's set.
*   **Cost Optimization:** The pipeline assigns higher-capability models (GPT-5.1) to early, formative stages and smaller models (GPT-5-mini) to later, classification-style stages to balance quality and cost, reducing cost from $0.35 to $0.13 per instance.
*   **Validation of Atomic Fact Generation:** Two medical doctors validated the quality of generated facts, confirming high faithfulness (96.4%), completeness (96.0%), comprehensiveness (98.0%), and non-redundancy (90.5%) (Gwet’s AC1: 0.597–0.955).
*   **Factual Quality Metrics:**
    *   **Factual Precision:** Measures the extent to which facts from generated conclusions are supported and non-contradictory with respect to the CDSR review. It rewards supported facts and penalizes contradictions.
    *   **Factual Recall:** Measures the extent to which generated conclusions cover facts from the Authors' Conclusions of CDSR reviews (the authoritative set).
    *   **Factual F1:** The harmonic mean of factual precision and recall, representing overall factual quality.
*   **LLM-based Judges:** To scale evaluation, GPT-5.4-mini is employed as an LLM judge for factual precision and recall tasks. The judges are provided with the atomic fact and source text (CDSR abstract/summary for precision, generated conclusion for recall).
*   **Validation of LLM Judges:** An expert-annotated gold-standard dataset (N=129 facts for precision, N=119 for recall) was created by three medical doctors (two annotating, one adjudicating disagreements). GPT-5.4-mini achieved a macro F1 of 0.837 for precision and 0.868 for recall. Agreement between the LLM judge and individual experts was comparable to, and in some cases exceeded, the agreement between the experts themselves (average Expert–LLM Gwet’s AC1: 0.560 for precision, 0.723 for recall). Error analysis revealed challenges in contextual equivalence/clinical synonymy and differing interpretations of source text.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved 8 frontier models and deep research agents benchmarked on 268 samples from SCICONBENCH, under three settings: base (parametric-only), SCICONHARNESS tools (unconstrained retrieval), and SCICONHARNESS tools + clean-room (controlled retrieval).

#### 5.1 Performance of Models and Deep Research Agents

*   **Overall Low Factual Quality:** Across all systems and settings, factual F1 scores remained below what would be considered reliable for scientific conclusion synthesis. In the clean-room setting, the best-performing agent, `o3-deep-research`, achieved a factual F1 of 0.337. Even in the unconstrained setting, `o3-deep-research` achieved the highest F1 of 0.508.
*   **Pervasive Quality Issues:** A substantial proportion of generated conclusions contained factual errors. Between 44.8% and 84.0% of generated conclusions included at least one fact contradicting the reference CDSR review. Furthermore, nearly all generated conclusions (94.0% to 100.0%) contained at least one fact not supported by the reference review.
*   **Incompleteness:** Generated conclusions frequently failed to cover the necessary information from the ground-truth. Between 55.4% and 84.7% of reference facts from CDSR reviews were not supported or covered by the generated conclusions.

#### 5.2 Impact of Tool Augmentations

*   **Uneven Gains from Tools:** The addition of SCICONHARNESS tools (without clean-room constraints) led to performance improvements in most cases, but these gains were inconsistent. `claude-sonnet-4.5` showed substantial gains in recall (+0.139) and F1 (+0.091), despite a slight drop in precision. In contrast, `gpt-5.1` showed only marginal F1 gain (+0.012) despite heavier tool usage, and `gemini-3-pro`'s performance degraded with tools. This suggests that access to tools alone is insufficient; effective integration and precise use of evidence are critical.

#### 5.3 Impact of Clean-Room Evaluation

*   **Consistent Performance Attenuation:** The clean-room protocol consistently reduced factual F1 scores across all systems, ranging from 0.02 to 0.172. For instance, `claude-sonnet-4.5` saw an F1 drop of 0.085, and `o3-deep-research` experienced a 0.17 F1 decrease. This reduction indicates that a significant portion of the observed performance in unconstrained settings is attributable to the retrieval of ground-truth artifacts rather than genuine synthesis by the agents.
*   **High Leakage Mitigation:** The `google_search` tool exhibited high clean-room filtering rates, ranging from 49.6% to 81.8% of calls, highlighting the extent to which agents actively sought and retrieved ground-truth or derivative artifacts when unconstrained. This underscores the necessity of clean-room evaluation for valid capability assessment.

#### 5.4 Auditing Consumer-Facing Agents

*   **Unreliable Conclusions:** An audit of widely-deployed consumer-facing agents (`Google AI Overview`, `Google AI Mode`, `OpenEvidence`) on the same benchmark samples (without clean-room protocol, as these operate on the open web) revealed significant reliability issues.
    *   `Google AI Mode` (F1: 0.361) and `Google AI Overview` (F1: 0.384) performed poorly, exhibiting weak recall and high rates of contradictory facts (56.3% to 59% of conclusions).
    *   `OpenEvidence` performed comparatively better (F1: 0.522), achieving higher precision (0.580) and recall (0.541). However, it still produced contradictory facts in 50.8% of its conclusions and covered only approximately half (51.7%) of the reference facts.
*   **Implications for High-Stakes Decisions:** These findings suggest that consumer-facing AI agents, despite having access to ground-truth information, frequently generate incomplete or contradictory scientific conclusions. This poses risks for high-stakes decision-making in health contexts where these systems are increasingly utilized.

#### 5.5 Failure Mode Analysis (Summary from Appendix)

Manual analysis identified common failure modes:
*   **Incorrect Direction-of-Effect:** Agents sometimes inverted the core conclusion of evidence, presenting null findings as beneficial or harmful, or reversing positive/negative effects.
*   **Evidence Quality Mischaracterization:** Agents frequently distorted the confidence level of evidence, misrepresenting high-certainty findings as low-certainty, or overstating weak evidence.
*   **Lack of Specificity:** Generated conclusions often provided overly general summaries, lacking the nuanced, outcome-level details crucial for comprehensive scientific interpretation.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a rigorous assessment of the current capabilities of AI agents in synthesizing scientific conclusions, yielding several significant implications:

*   **Highlighting an Unresolved Challenge:** The consistently low factual F1 scores across frontier models and deep research agents, particularly under clean-room evaluation, demonstrate that reliable scientific conclusion synthesis remains an open and complex challenge. Current systems frequently produce incomplete, unsupported, or contradictory information, even for well-defined scientific questions.
*   **Validation of Clean-Room Evaluation:** The observed and consistent performance degradation when AI agents operate under clean-room conditions strongly suggests that benchmark leakage significantly inflates performance estimates in unconstrained settings. This emphasizes the critical importance of controlled evaluation environments, like SCICONHARNESS, to ensure valid and accurate measurement of genuine synthesis capabilities, rather than simple retrieval or memorization. This methodology helps prevent overestimation of AI capabilities and maintains construct validity in agentic evaluations.
*   **Implications for High-Stakes Domains:** The audit of consumer-facing AI agents reveals their current unreliability in synthesizing scientific conclusions, with frequent omissions and contradictions. Given the increasing reliance on these systems in health, science, and policy contexts, these findings raise concerns about potential misinformation, skewed interpretations of evidence, and adverse impacts on decision-making. The research underscores the need for caution and further development before these agents can be widely trusted in high-stakes applications.
*   **Guidance for Future AI Development:** SCICONBENCH, as a large-scale, live benchmark, and the expert-validated factual evaluation pipeline provide a robust framework for guiding the development of future AI agents. By offering fine-grained metrics for factual precision and recall, the work enables researchers to identify specific weaknesses (e.g., in reasoning, evidence integration, or uncertainty communication) and iterate on agent designs that are more reliable, transparent, and grounded in scientific evidence.
*   **Promoting Trustworthy AI:** By systematically evaluating the limitations of current AI agents, this work contributes to the broader goal of developing trustworthy AI. It encourages a focus on improving the foundational capabilities of scientific synthesis, evidence appraisal, and robust information integration, which are essential for AI to truly augment human decision-making in scientific and medical fields. The open-source nature of SCICONBENCH and SCICONHARNESS aims to foster community-wide efforts in addressing these challenges.
