## Research Report: When Does LLM Orchestration Pay Off? A Controlled Evaluation of Accuracy, Cost, and Task Difficulty

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)
*   **Nicolas Leins:** Zuse Institute Berlin & TU Berlin, Berlin, Germany
*   **Nico Pelleriti:** Zuse Institute Berlin & TU Berlin, Berlin, Germany
*   **Jana Gonnermann-Müller:** Zuse Institute Berlin & Weizenbaum Institute Berlin, Berlin, Germany
*   **Sebastian Pokutta:** Zuse Institute Berlin & TU Berlin, Berlin, Germany

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated increasing capabilities across various reasoning-intensive tasks, including mathematical problem-solving and code generation. Beyond their pre-trained weights, the allocation of computational resources during inference (often referred to as orchestration) is recognized as a factor influencing their performance. This includes methods such as generating multiple candidate solutions, iteratively refining an initial response, or enabling interaction among multiple model instances. These strategies aim to enhance a model's output without modifying its underlying weights.

However, the practical application of these inference-time orchestration methods introduces complexities. Increased computation translates to more API calls, higher token consumption, elevated latency, and additional potential points of failure. The fundamental question within the research community is whether the accuracy improvements yielded by these orchestrations consistently justify their associated resource costs. Existing literature presents mixed evidence regarding the efficacy and efficiency of multi-agent debate or self-correction, particularly when considering protocol configuration and inference resources.

A significant gap identified in previous evaluations is the lack of controlled experimental designs. Comparisons often involve variations in LLM backbones, prompting strategies, stopping criteria, benchmarks, and the amount of optimization effort, alongside differences in workflow structure. This makes it challenging to isolate the specific value attributable to the orchestration methodology itself. Furthermore, evaluations that focus solely on accuracy may inadvertently favor more complex workflows that have received disproportionately greater optimization during their development.

While difficulty-aware systems exist to dynamically select model capacity, allocate test-time computation, or construct query-specific workflows based on estimated task complexity, these systems do not inherently establish whether the relative benefit of a *fixed* orchestration increases monotonically with human-derived task difficulty. This research addresses these limitations by conducting a controlled, within-study evaluation to systematically quantify the accuracy-resource trade-offs of common LLM orchestrations, while also investigating the influence of task difficulty and the underlying LLM backbone.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation
The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while LLM orchestration is frequently assumed to enhance reasoning capabilities through additional inference-time computation, the actual gains may not always be commensurate with the incurred costs. Prior comparisons often fail to adequately control for varying optimization efforts, making it difficult to discern the intrinsic value of orchestration structures.

The key objectives of this study were to:
1.  **Evaluate Accuracy and Resource Use under Controlled Optimization:** Conduct a controlled comparison of three common LLM orchestrations (Self-Refine, Best-of-N, Debate) against two single-call baselines (task-only and Chain-of-Thought) across multiple LLM backbones and benchmark domains. A central aim was to quantify both accuracy improvements and the corresponding inference resource consumption (token usage) while standardizing optimization effort using an automated prompt evolution system (GEPA).
2.  **Investigate the Relationship between Orchestration Effectiveness and Task Difficulty:** Determine whether the relative benefits of fixed orchestration methods systematically increase with human-derived task difficulty. This objective addresses the hypothesis that harder tasks might inherently benefit more from additional reasoning steps.
3.  **Assess Backbone Model Dependence:** Examine the extent to which the effectiveness of different orchestration methods varies across different underlying LLM backbones. This aims to understand if orchestration gains are generalizable or model-specific.
4.  **Inform Practical Decision-Making:** Provide empirical evidence to guide decisions on when LLM orchestration is justified, considering the trade-offs between moderate accuracy gains and significantly increased inference costs, and to suggest improvements for future evaluation methodologies.

By addressing these objectives, the researchers aimed to provide a more nuanced understanding of LLM orchestration's utility, moving beyond assumptions of uniform benefit to data-driven insights on its conditional value.

### 4. Methodology and Approach
The study employed a controlled experimental design to evaluate LLM orchestration methods. This involved a fully paired comparison of three multi-call orchestrations and two single-call baselines across five LLM backbones and three distinct benchmark domains, with item-level difficulty estimates. Critical elements held constant included LLM backbones, decoding settings, benchmarks, and grading rules.

**Benchmarks:**
Three benchmarks from the Easy2Hard-Bench suite were selected for their item-level difficulty estimates, dedicated training data for prompt optimization, and automatic outcome verification:
*   **Lichess:** Chess puzzles with difficulty derived from player ratings.
*   **Codeforces:** Competitive programming problems with difficulty derived from player ratings.
*   **AMC:** Mathematics problems (American Mathematics Competitions) with difficulty estimated using Item Response Theory.
For evaluation, 200-item subsets of disjoint evaluation splits were used for each benchmark, stratified by normalized difficulty quantile and rating uncertainty to maintain the original difficulty distribution.

**Models:**
Five locally hosted, open-weight LLMs were evaluated:
*   DeepSeek V4 Flash (284B)
*   Gemma 4 (25.2B)
*   GLM 4.7 Flash (30B)
*   Qwen 3.6 (35B)
*   GPT-OSS Puzzle 88B
All models shared a 120,000-token output limit and decoding settings (temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95), served with vLLM. Specific hardware configurations were used for each model.

**Orchestrations:**
Five methods were compared:
*   **LLM (Task-only):** A single-call baseline using only the benchmark task prompt.
*   **CoT LLM (Chain-of-Thought):** A single-call baseline incorporating a GEPA-optimized CoT system prompt.
*   **Self-Refine:** A multi-call method involving an initial solution generation followed by at most two feedback-refinement rounds.
*   **Best-of-N (BoN):** Generates three independent candidate solutions, from which a selector model chooses the best one.
*   **Debate:** A multi-call method where two agents independently answer, revise after observing each other, and then a judge synthesizes a final answer (always five calls).

**Budget-Controlled Prompt Optimization:**
To standardize prompt development and prevent confounding orchestration effectiveness with unequal tuning effort, GEPA (Generative Prompt Evolution with Agents) was used to optimize textual prompt components. Each method, for each benchmark, received a common maximum optimization budget: 750 metric calls and a cap on weighted token consumption (40M for Codeforces, 75M for Lichess). DeepSeek V4 Flash served as the task, profile, and reflection model during GEPA runs. The candidate prompt with the highest validation accuracy was selected. For AMC, a hand-crafted prompt was used as GEPA did not improve the seed configuration.

**Grading and Failure Handling:**
All tasks were graded as pass@1 (correct/incorrect) based on benchmark-specific rules (exact match for Lichess/AMC, passing tests for Codeforces). Transient technical or malformed-generation failures were retried. Maximum-output-limit failures, where models consumed the budget without a valid answer, were retained as score-zero outcomes and contributed to resource totals.

**Measurements:**
*   **Accuracy:** The fraction of correctly solved tasks.
*   **Resource Use:** Input tokens, output tokens, and reasoning tokens were logged. A weighted token (t_w) metric was used as a proxy for inference cost, defined as input tokens + 4 × (output + reasoning tokens) to account for differential pricing.
*   **Token Efficiency:** Calculated as t_w / Accuracy.
Metrics were reported overall and stratified by within-benchmark difficulty quartile.

**Statistical Analysis:**
The study used mixed-effects logistic regression models, fitted separately for each benchmark, to account for item-level dependence and quantify uncertainty. CoT was set as the primary inferential reference.
*   Model 0 (M0): `correct ~ difficulty + LLM + (1 | item) + (1 | item:LLM)`
*   Model 1 (M1): `correct ~ method + difficulty + LLM + (1 | item) + (1 | item:LLM)` (tested average method effect).
*   Model 2 (M2): `correct ~ method * difficulty + LLM + (1 | item) + (1 | item:LLM)` (tested method-by-difficulty interaction).
*   Model 3 (M3, exploratory): `correct ~ method * LLM + difficulty + (1 | item) + (1 | item:LLM)` (tested method-by-LLM interaction).
Likelihood-ratio tests assessed model fit improvements, with Holm correction applied for multiple comparisons. Spearman correlations assessed the relationship between difficulty and accuracy.

### 5. Main Findings and Results
The controlled evaluation yielded several findings regarding the accuracy, cost, and contextual factors influencing LLM orchestration performance.

**GEPA Optimization and Single-Call Baselines:**
GEPA optimization significantly improved prompt performance on the validation sets for Codeforces and Lichess. For example, on Codeforces, CoT accuracy increased from 20% (seed) to 82% (selected candidate). On Lichess, CoT improved from 42% to 56%. For AMC, GEPA did not produce notable improvements, and a hand-crafted prompt was utilized. The comparison between the optimized CoT and the task-only single-call baseline revealed only marginal, non-significant differences in benchmark-level accuracy. However, model-specific differences between CoT and task-only varied substantially, ranging from -10.5 to +10.0 percentage points on Codeforces, and similarly diverse ranges for Lichess and AMC.

**Average Effects of Orchestration:**
*   **Accuracy Gains:** Orchestration methods yielded moderate, benchmark-dependent accuracy gains.
    *   On **Codeforces**, Self-Refine (+4.6 percentage points over CoT) and BoN (+4.2 percentage points over CoT) showed statistically significant improvements, with odds ratios of 1.90 and 1.79, respectively. Debate did not significantly outperform CoT.
    *   On **AMC**, Self-Refine (+1.9 percentage points over CoT) and BoN (+2.6 percentage points over CoT) also showed statistically significant improvements, with odds ratios of 1.99 and 2.68, respectively. Debate was not significant.
    *   On **Lichess**, no orchestration method significantly improved accuracy over CoT.
    *   Averaged across backbones within each benchmark, the largest improvement observed was 4.6 percentage points over optimized CoT inference and 4.5 points over task-only inference.
*   **Mixed-model fit:** Adding method as a predictor improved mixed-model fit for Codeforces (p = .0003) and AMC (p = .0032), but not for Lichess (p = .227).

**Effects Across Task Difficulty:**
*   **Absolute Accuracy:** Human-derived difficulty measures (player ratings, IRT) were strongly and negatively correlated with item-level absolute accuracy across all three benchmarks (Spearman’s ρ from -.485 to -.660, all p < .001). This confirms that higher difficulty was generally associated with lower accuracy.
*   **Relative Benefit:** The mixed-model analysis (M2) found no statistically significant interaction between orchestration method and task difficulty for any benchmark (all p > .68). This indicates that the relative benefit of the evaluated orchestrations did not systematically increase with task difficulty. Descriptive analyses across difficulty quartiles showed varying, non-monotonic patterns, rather than a consistent increase in orchestration benefit for harder problems.

**Variation Across LLMs:**
Exploratory mixed-effects analyses (M3) revealed strong and statistically significant method-by-LLM interactions across all three benchmarks (p < .0001 for Codeforces and AMC, p = .0022 for Lichess). This finding indicates that the effectiveness of a specific orchestration method varied substantially depending on the underlying LLM backbone. For instance, model-specific paired differences from optimized CoT ranged from -4.0 to +18.0 percentage points on Codeforces, from -6.5 to +12.0 on Lichess, and from -9.5 to +11.0 on AMC.

**Inference-Resource Trade-offs:**
*   **Token Consumption:** All orchestration methods consistently required substantially more inference resources compared to single-call baselines.
    *   CoT inference consumed 0.92 to 1.24 times the weighted tokens of task-only inference.
    *   Self-Refine, BoN, and Debate consumed approximately 2.1 to 3.8 times the weighted tokens of CoT inference, and roughly 2 to 4 times the mean total tokens of task-only inference.
*   **Absolute Costs:** Mean weighted tokens per evaluation for orchestrations ranged from approximately 127,000 to 360,000 across benchmarks.
*   **Efficiency:** The ratio of weighted tokens per correct answer for orchestrations relative to task-only inference ranged from 1.41 to 5.88 across model-benchmark cells, consistently showing higher resource use per successful task.
*   **Output-Limit Failures:** Instances of models reaching maximum output limits without providing a valid answer were observed, particularly for Gemma on Lichess. These cases contributed to the high token consumption without yielding correct answers, highlighting limitations under common output budgets.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact
This research provides a controlled empirical analysis contributing to the understanding of LLM orchestration's efficacy and efficiency. It challenges the assumption that additional inference-time structure uniformly leads to substantial performance gains, establishing a clearer accuracy-resource trade-off.

**Key areas of significance include:**
*   **Cost-Effectiveness Evaluation:** The study demonstrates that while Self-Refine and Best-of-N can offer moderate accuracy gains in certain domains (Codeforces and AMC), these gains come at a significantly increased cost in terms of inference tokens. In other domains (Lichess), no significant improvement was observed. This implies that a well-optimized single-call method often serves as a more efficiency-oriented default, and the decision to use orchestration should be contingent on the application's specific value proposition for incremental accuracy versus the added expense (tokens, latency, serving costs).
*   **Revisiting the Role of Task Difficulty:** A significant finding is that while human-derived task difficulty strongly predicts a model's absolute accuracy, it does not systematically predict the *relative benefit* that a fixed orchestration method will provide. This indicates that identifying tasks likely to be missed by a single call differs from identifying tasks that a particular multi-call workflow is likely to correct. This insight is relevant for the design of adaptive LLM systems, suggesting they should focus on learned resource allocation policies rather than a simple heuristic of applying more computation to harder problems.
*   **Emphasis on Model-Specificity:** The observed strong method-by-backbone interactions are a critical contribution. They demonstrate that an orchestration effective for one LLM backbone may be neutral or even detrimental for another. This suggests that the underlying capabilities of different models (e.g., in revision, selection, critique, or synthesis) interact uniquely with orchestration structures. Consequently, workflow selection and prompt optimization should be considered an integral part of model selection and validated specifically for the intended model-workflow pair.
*   **Advancing Evaluation Standards:** The methodology of controlling optimization effort through GEPA under a common budget sets a standard for more rigorous evaluation. The findings underscore the importance of reporting both the method of workflow acquisition (e.g., optimization protocol) and the full inference resources consumed, moving beyond accuracy-only comparisons. This approach provides a more transparent and complete picture of an LLM system's performance characteristics.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted. It offers practical guidance for developers and researchers, recommending careful consideration of the specific LLM, task domain, desired accuracy, and acceptable inference costs when designing and deploying LLM-powered applications. Furthermore, it suggests that future research on LLM orchestration should prioritize model-specific evaluations and focus on conditional resource allocation strategies that are dynamically tailored to the specific context, rather than relying on generalized assumptions of benefit.
