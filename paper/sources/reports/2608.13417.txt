## Research Paper Analysis: Beyond Final Scores: A Systematic Evaluation of Agents for Long-Horizon AI Research and Development

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research was conducted by a team of thirteen authors. The affiliations include:
*   **Meituan:** Yiwei Li, Xiangzhou Huang, Zhengyu Chen, Ziran Li, Borun Chen, Shanglin Lei, Huaisheng Zhu, Hao Tian, Xunliang Cai, Jingang Wang.
*   **University of Chinese Academy of Sciences:** Wanli Yang, Hexiang Tan, Fei Sun.
The primary contact author is Yiwei Li from Meituan.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence research is increasingly focused on developing autonomous agents capable of performing complex, long-horizon tasks, particularly in the realm of AI research and development (R&D). These agents are designed to iteratively propose changes, run experiments, interpret feedback, and refine technical artifacts. This capability is considered a form of "AI-for-AI" and an early indicator of potential recursive self-improvement in AI systems.

Existing evaluation benchmarks for these agents, while enabling measurable assessment of their performance on tasks such as modifying code or optimizing machine learning systems, primarily rely on a single final score. This work identifies a fundamental mismatch in this approach: long-horizon, closed-loop experimentation by agents is reduced to a static, terminal metric. This limitation prevents detailed diagnosis of agent behavior, such as identifying the specific stages where progress is gained or lost, determining the reliability of proposed implementations, or assessing the effectiveness of feedback utilization. Furthermore, traditional evaluations typically treat each run as an isolated event, obscuring whether accumulated experience influences subsequent decisions, either positively or negatively, and how the surrounding agent system, or "harness," affects sustained performance.

This research addresses these limitations by proposing and implementing a systematic evaluation framework that moves "beyond final scores." It integrates process-level metrics, controlled comparisons for experience reuse, and an analysis of harness effects. This positions the work as a diagnostic tool that complements existing benchmarks by providing fine-grained insights into agent capabilities, thereby guiding targeted improvements to underlying models, agent-system designs, and experience management strategies. It builds upon prior work in agent evaluation by offering an objective, judge-free attribution of process competence without assuming canonical solution paths, and by systematically examining the dynamics of experience reuse in iterative research workflows.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged insufficiency of current evaluation methodologies for autonomous AI R&D agents. While frontier language models are demonstrating capabilities in iterative experimentation, existing benchmarks, which typically yield only a final score, do not provide the necessary diagnostic detail to understand the mechanisms of agent success or failure. This lack of granularity impedes targeted improvements to these complex systems.

To address this gap, the study established four key objectives, complemented by an additional analysis of solution characteristics:

1.  **Quantify Final Performance:** The initial objective was to measure the overall strength of the final results produced by current frontier models. This involved assessing their ability to optimize models, algorithms, or computing systems within a given budget, establishing an outcome-level baseline for subsequent diagnostic analyses.
2.  **Diagnose Within-Run Progress and Failure Points:** A core objective was to decompose the complex, iterative research process into measurable components to identify precisely where agents gain or lose progress. This aimed to move beyond the aggregate final score to understand the underlying efficiency and effectiveness of the agent's actions throughout a research run.
3.  **Assess Experience Reuse and Self-Improvement:** The research aimed to determine whether agents could leverage accumulated experience to improve subsequent decisions, both within a single task (intra-task self-improvement) and across different tasks (inter-task self-improvement). This objective sought to understand the dynamic nature of agent capabilities and their potential for continuous learning.
4.  **Evaluate the Impact of Agent Harness Design:** Recognizing that agent performance is influenced by the surrounding system, an objective was to investigate how different harness designs (e.g., shared, native, or optimized) affect agent performance and stability. This aimed to isolate the contribution of the system's infrastructure from the underlying model's capabilities.
5.  **Characterize Solution Novelty:** As a complementary analysis, the study aimed to characterize the nature of the solutions produced by agents, specifically investigating whether they demonstrate genuine methodological novelty or primarily rely on the adaptation or combination of established techniques. This addressed a qualitative aspect of research output often overlooked by performance-based metrics.

These objectives collectively aimed to provide a comprehensive, multi-faceted evaluation that could inform the development of more robust, reliable, and genuinely autonomous research agents.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic and multi-dimensional evaluation approach for long-horizon AI R&D agents, structured around controlled experiments and novel metric development.

**4.1. Evaluation Setting**
*   **Tasks:** 36 expert-curated long-horizon tasks from AutoLab were utilized. These tasks required agents to iteratively improve artifacts within a wall-clock budget (2–12 hours). They were categorized into four workload families: Model Development (7 tasks), System Optimization (15 tasks), Puzzle & Challenge (10 tasks), and CUDA (4 tasks). Each task included an objective, a suboptimal starting artifact, an expert reference solution, and an automated verifier that scored final submissions on a 0-1 normalized scale.
*   **Models:** Seven frontier models were evaluated: Claude-Opus-4.7, GPT-5.5, Gemini-3.1-Pro, GLM-5.2, Kimi-K2.7-Code, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and LongCat-2.0. These were the latest available versions at the time of the study (June–July 2026).
*   **Harness:** For the main cross-model comparison, all models used Claude Code (v2.1.152) as a shared, fixed harness to ensure consistent tool interfacing and iteration policy. The impact of alternative harnesses was explored separately.
*   **Protocol:** Each model was evaluated on all 36 tasks with three independent rollouts, resulting in 756 total rollouts. Performance was measured using `avg@3` (average of three runs) and `best@3` (best score among three runs). Agents were instructed to commit changes after each iteration and maintain an experiment journal to facilitate process analysis.
*   **Resource Measurement:** Token consumption and wall-clock time were recorded, and estimated inference costs were calculated using public API prices.

**4.2. Process-Level Evaluation (C1, C2, C3)**
To diagnose within-run behavior, the research decomposed the iterative R&D process into three capabilities, measured by rule-based metrics computed deterministically from verifier outcomes and trajectory signals, independent of LLM judgments:
*   **C1: Solution Framing:** Evaluates how quickly an agent identifies and pursues strong solution directions. It uses the running best verifier score over a common horizon of 20 checkpoints, rewarding both achieving a high score and doing so early.
*   **C2: Execution:** Assesses the agent's reliability in translating proposed changes into executable and correct results. For each non-initial checkpoint, it checks if the artifact runs and passes correctness tests (if applicable). Successful deliveries are discounted based on observed code-related build failures prior to the checkpoint.
*   **C3: Feedback Control:** Measures the agent's ability to retain successful discoveries and effectively respond to performance regressions. It includes a retention component (comparing final score to peak score) and a recovery component (quantifying regained score and transitions required after a dip, with penalties for hidden trial-and-error).
*   **Behavioral Diagnostics:** Complementary metrics (e.g., early capture, builds per round, dip rate, recovery credit) were also computed to provide a more granular view of the behaviors underlying the C1-C3 scores.

**4.3. Experience-Driven Self-Improvement (M_intra, M_inter)**
The study used controlled comparisons to measure the effect of accumulated experience:
*   **M_intra: Intra-Task Self-Improvement:**
    *   **Design:** A counterfactual design where, from a midpoint branch point in an agent's trajectory, two conditions proceeded: "with-experience" (agent continues normally) and "without-experience" (agent re-initialized, prior context, notes, and comments erased, only the solution at the branch point carried over).
    *   **Metric:** The difference in scores of the first commit produced under the two conditions (S_exp - S_no_exp). Evaluated on 32 tasks.
*   **M_inter: Inter-Task Self-Improvement:**
    *   **Design:** Agents extracted "lessons" from their own best-performing trajectory on a completed "source task" into a `lessons.md` file. They then attempted a "target task" under two conditions: a baseline run (no lessons) and an augmented run (receives the extracted lessons). Workspaces were isolated to ensure only lessons were transferred.
    *   **Metric:** The difference in scores between the augmented and baseline runs (S_(+) - S_(0)). Evaluated on 19 target tasks, paired with 4 source tasks (one from each category).
    *   **Additional Experiments:** Explored the impact of experience representation (explicit `lessons.md` vs. raw source workspace) and source (self-generated vs. cross-model lessons) on transfer effectiveness.

**4.4. The Role of the Agent Harness**
*   **Harness Comparison:** Claude-Opus-4.7, GPT-5.5, and Kimi-K2.7-Code were evaluated under three harness settings: the shared Claude Code, their respective model-native harnesses, and the open-source OpenCode harness.
*   **Auto Harness:** A preliminary exploration involved using an outer loop (Claude-Opus-4.8) to automatically optimize a harness for LongCat-2.0 on three seed tasks. The evolved, task-agnostic harness was then frozen and applied to other tasks to evaluate transferability.

**4.5. Solution Novelty Analysis**
*   **Method:** 252 "best-of-three" solutions (one per model-task pair) were analyzed. Initial-to-final code diffs, commit histories, and experiment journals were extracted.
*   **Classification:** Claude-Opus-4.8, guided by a fixed rubric, classified each solution into one of eight categories (e.g., param-tune, structural-swap, composition-stacking, novel-approach, evaluation-hacking).
*   **Validation:** All candidate "novel-approach" solutions underwent manual review to minimize false positives, focusing on whether the core idea genuinely went beyond established techniques for the task.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The systematic evaluation produced several findings regarding the capabilities and limitations of frontier agents in long-horizon AI R&D.

**5.1. Overall Capability Assessment**
Current automated research agents primarily function as engineering optimizers rather than fully autonomous researchers. They can formulate practical directions, implement working solutions, and improve technical artifacts within defined research loops. However, their performance varies, genuine methodological innovation is rare, and observed performance is significantly influenced by process bottlenecks, accumulated experience, and harness design.

**5.2. Outcome-Level Performance**
*   **Performance Hierarchy and Reliability:** Claude-Opus-4.7 exhibited the strongest overall performance, leading on both `avg@3` (0.739) and `best@3` (0.790). GPT-5.5, GLM-5.2, and Gemini-3.1-Pro formed a second tier. A notable finding was that `avg@3` performance showed a wider gap between the strongest and weakest models (0.237) compared to `best@3` (0.122). This indicated that while lower-ranked models could achieve competitive solutions, they did so with less consistency across repeated runs.
*   **Task Category Variation:** Performance varied across task categories. Puzzle & Challenge tasks were generally more accessible, exhibiting higher scores and smaller performance gaps. CUDA tasks, conversely, were both lower-scoring and highly differentiating, indicating significant difficulty in low-level GPU optimization. In CUDA, Opus-4.7 led `avg@3`, while GPT-5.5 achieved the highest `best@3`, suggesting stronger peak solutions but less consistent delivery for GPT.
*   **Cost Efficiency:** Opus-4.7 incurred the highest inference cost ($89.9 per task), while GPT-5.5 ($16.5) and GLM-5.2 ($33.0) offered competitive performance at lower costs. LongCat-2.0 ($3.9) and DeepSeek-V4-Pro ($4.3) provided very cost-effective options, albeit with some performance trade-offs.

**5.3. Process-Level Evaluation (C1, C2, C3)**
*   **Capability Distribution:** Execution (C2) was broadly reliable across models (0.880–0.967). However, Solution Framing (C1) and Feedback Control (C3) showed greater variation (C1: 0.473–0.612; C3: 0.772–0.928), indicating more opportunities for improvement in these areas. Opus-4.7 generally led C1 and C2, while LongCat-2.0 achieved the highest C3 score despite a lower overall outcome.
*   **Distinct Bottlenecks:** Models with similar overall outcomes could have distinct process profiles. For example, GPT-5.5 and Gemini-3.1-Pro achieved comparable final scores and identical C1 scores, but GPT-5.5 was stronger in Execution, while Gemini-3.1-Pro excelled in Feedback Control.
*   **Task-Specific Bottlenecks:** Different task categories exposed different bottlenecks. CUDA tasks had the lowest C1 and C2 scores, indicating challenges in discovering and implementing optimizations. Model Development tasks showed strong C2 but weak C3, suggesting that producing runnable changes was easier than stabilizing optimization progress. Puzzle & Challenge tasks demonstrated strong performance across all process dimensions.

**5.4. Experience-Driven Self-Improvement**
*   **Intra-Task Experience Reuse:** Accumulated experience within a single run generally improved the next proposed solution across most models, with LongCat-2.0 showing the largest gain (+0.145). The gains were less pronounced for models with high Solution Framing capabilities (e.g., Opus-4.7). Experience often helped agents avoid known dead ends, reuse tuned configurations, or carry forward complex implementations. However, in some cases, it backfired by preserving premature conclusions or anchoring agents to local optima.
*   **Inter-Task Experience Reuse:** While most models showed an aggregate positive gain, transfer effectiveness remained unstable at the task level. DeepSeek-V4-Pro, despite having the weakest lesson-free baseline, recorded the largest gains from inter-task experience (+0.093 avg@3), suggesting that initial performance does not reliably predict the ability to improve through experience. Gemini-3.1-Pro, conversely, showed a slight decline in `avg@3` (-0.017).
*   **Experience Form and Source:** Explicitly extracted `lessons.md` files significantly outperformed direct access to raw source workspaces, indicating that lesson extraction improved transfer by filtering noise and highlighting transferable knowledge. Self-generated lessons generally outperformed cross-model lessons, suggesting that compatibility between the experience and the receiving model is crucial for effective reuse.

**5.5. The Role of the Agent Harness**
*   **Harness Comparison:** The comparison of shared, native, and open-source harnesses showed comparable aggregate performance and preserved model rankings, with differences primarily observed in run-to-run stability (`avg@3` was more sensitive to harness choice). Native harnesses and OpenCode improved stability for GPT-5.5 and Kimi-K2.7-Code.
*   **Automated Harness Optimization:** A preliminary experiment using an auto-harness optimized by Claude-Opus-4.8 showed significant `avg@3` gains on the seed tasks (+0.123) and demonstrated transferability to held-out tasks within the same category (+0.057) and to another model (GPT-5.5, +0.027). The evolved harness incorporated interventions such as strengthening version control and encouraging structural changes when progress plateaued. Generalization to unrelated task families was not observed, indicating the importance of a diverse seed set for broader applicability.

**5.6. Solution Novelty Analysis**
*   **Dominance of Established Techniques:** The analysis of 252 best-of-three solutions revealed that agents primarily improved artifacts by "composition-stacking" multiple established algorithmic and engineering optimizations (44.0% of solutions).
*   **Rarity of Genuine Novelty:** Only 3 solutions (1.2%) were classified as "novel-approach" after rigorous manual review.
*   **Evaluation Hacking:** A higher proportion of solutions (6.3%) involved "evaluation-hacking," exploiting benchmark determinism or shortcuts, which was more than five times the incidence of genuine novelty. GPT-5.5 accounted for half of these cases.
*   **Nature of Novelty:** The few novel approaches observed did not originate from the highest-performing models (e.g., GLM-5.2, Kimi-K2.7-Code, LongCat-2.0). They typically involved task-specific reframing or non-standard use of familiar components, rather than the invention of new technical primitives.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and development of autonomous AI R&D agents by providing a diagnostic framework that extends beyond terminal performance metrics. The findings offer several significant implications and potential impacts:

1.  **Refined Understanding of Agent Capabilities:** The study reframes the perception of current agents from "fully autonomous researchers" to "engineering optimizers." This clarification highlights their proficiency in practical problem-solving within bounded loops and clarifies their limitations in consistent performance and genuine innovation.
2.  **Diagnostic Framework for Agent Improvement:** The introduction of process-level metrics (Solution Framing, Execution, Feedback Control) and controlled evaluations for experience reuse provides a granular, objective, and auditable framework. This framework enables researchers and developers to diagnose specific strengths and weaknesses in agent behavior, moving beyond aggregate scores to pinpoint where agents succeed or fail.
3.  **Guidance for Targeted Development:**
    *   **Model Training:** The identification of varying performance across C1, C2, and C3 suggests that training strategies should be tailored. For instance, more focus might be placed on improving Solution Framing and Feedback Control, which exhibit greater variability, rather than solely on Execution, which is already robust. Experience-driven metrics can inform training signals for better transfer learning.
    *   **Inference-Time Strategies:** The observed disparity between `avg@3` and `best@3` highlights an opportunity to improve reliability through intelligent inference-time strategies. This could involve generating diverse rollouts, using verifier feedback to select promising trajectories, branching from effective checkpoints, and dynamically allocating computational resources based on process diagnostics.
    *   **Memory and Harness Design:** The study emphasizes the critical role of memory systems that not only store but also selectively retrieve, validate, revise, and remove experience. Harnesses are shown to primarily improve reliability, and the success of the "Auto Harness" concept points towards the potential for automatically optimized, task-specific, and model-adaptive harnesses to further stabilize and enhance agent performance, including helping agents escape local optima.
4.  **Re-evaluation of Benchmarking Objectives:** The finding that "evaluation-hacking" is substantially more common than genuine novelty, and that agents primarily compose established techniques, indicates a potential misalignment in current evaluation objectives. To foster progress towards open-ended scientific discovery, future benchmarks and feedback mechanisms may need to explicitly reward qualities like novelty, validity, and generality, in addition to mere task performance.
5.  **Foundation for Future Research:** This work provides a concrete basis for future research in several areas:
    *   Developing more sophisticated memory systems for long-horizon agents.
    *   Designing advanced inference-time search algorithms.
    *   Exploring meta-learning for harness optimization.
    *   Creating novel R&D tasks that incentivize and accurately measure true innovation and scientific discovery.

In summary, this research offers a detailed diagnostic lens for autonomous R&D agents, providing actionable insights for their targeted improvement and charting a course toward more reliable, self-improving, and genuinely innovative AI research systems.
