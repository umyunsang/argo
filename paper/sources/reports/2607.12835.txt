## Research Paper Analysis: Can LLMs Write Reliable Rubrics? A Meta-Evaluation for Experiment Reproduction

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research paper "Can LLMs Write Reliable Rubrics? A Meta-Evaluation for Experiment Reproduction" was authored by Hanhua Hong, Yizhi Li, Jiaoyan Chen, Luu Gia Huy, Sophia Ananiadou, Jung-jae Kim, and Chenghua Lin.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has led to significant progress in capabilities such as code generation, reasoning, and tool use. These developments are increasingly enabling LLM-based agents to engage in various stages of scientific research, including interpreting papers, designing experiments, and executing computational studies. A critical and challenging aspect within this workflow is the reproduction of experiments described in research papers into executable code repositories. This challenge is particularly acute in machine learning (ML), where the high pace of publication often outstrips the release of reproducible artifacts, with a low percentage of papers at top conferences providing complete codebases.

To address this, a growing body of research focuses on automated paper-to-code generation systems, such as PaperCoder, AutoP2C, AutoReproduce, and HiRAS. However, the reliable evaluation of outputs from these open-ended LLM systems presents its own set of difficulties. Traditional evaluation methods, such as direct comparison between a paper and a generated repository (e.g., in the Paper2Code benchmark), are susceptible to "hallucination," where evaluators may incorrectly infer that described methods have been implemented.

In response to these evaluation challenges, rubric-based evaluation has emerged as a promising alternative, notably exemplified by benchmarks like PaperBench. This approach involves defining fine-grained, weighted pass/fail criteria for distinct reproduction stages, against which candidate repositories are independently assessed. Rubric-based evaluation is increasingly adopted for assessing open-ended LLM outputs across various domains (e.g., HealthBench, ResearchRubrics, ProfBench) due to its capacity to yield more reliable judgments compared to holistic evaluations.

Despite its benefits, rubric construction itself has become a major bottleneck. Manually designing paper-specific rubrics, often in collaboration with original authors, demands substantial expert effort. For instance, PaperBench covers only 20 papers, with criteria counts ranging from 94 to 2,551, underscoring the difficulty and cost of standardization. The high annotation costs observed in other domains further highlight this scalability issue. Simultaneously, LLMs are being increasingly utilized as judges operating over fixed rubrics and as generators of rubrics for tasks like Reinforcement Learning reward modeling. This context sets the stage for the central question of this research: whether LLMs can reliably generate rubrics for the high-stakes domain of paper reproduction, while maintaining alignment with human expert judgments. This work aims to investigate this specific application, addressing a critical scalability limitation within the broader effort to automate and evaluate scientific research.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate the capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) to reliably generate rubrics for the evaluation of paper reproduction, specifically within the machine learning domain. This investigation aims to determine whether LLM-authored rubrics can achieve alignment with human expert judgments, thereby offering a scalable solution to a significant bottleneck in scientific research evaluation.

The motivation for this work stems from several interconnected challenges and opportunities in the current landscape of LLM-based scientific agents and their evaluation:

*   **Scalability of Evaluation for LLM-based Agents:** LLMs are increasingly being developed to perform complex scientific tasks, including automated experiment reproduction. As these agents become more sophisticated and prevalent, the need for robust, scalable, and reliable evaluation methods becomes paramount. Current benchmarks often rely on labor-intensive, human-expert-driven evaluation processes, which are not sustainable for the rapid pace of LLM development and the vast number of research papers.
*   **Limitations of Existing Evaluation Methods:** Direct comparison between a paper and a generated code repository, while seemingly straightforward, has proven prone to LLM "hallucination" in the evaluation process. This unreliability necessitates more structured and objective assessment tools.
*   **High Cost of Human-Authored Rubrics:** Rubric-based evaluation, exemplified by benchmarks like PaperBench, has demonstrated higher reliability by breaking down complex tasks into fine-grained, weighted criteria. However, the manual creation of these rubrics demands significant expert time and effort. For instance, PaperBench's 20 papers required extensive collaboration with original authors, and other benchmarks like HealthBench and ResearchRubrics similarly consumed thousands of expert hours. This high cost limits the scope and scalability of such rigorous benchmarks.
*   **Emerging Role of LLMs in Evaluation:** LLMs are already being explored for various evaluative roles, including acting as judges against predefined rubrics and even generating rubrics for simpler tasks. This raises the question of whether their capabilities extend to generating high-quality, domain-specific rubrics for complex scientific reproduction tasks. If LLMs can reliably automate rubric generation, it could substantially reduce human effort and accelerate the development of comprehensive evaluation benchmarks.
*   **Understanding LLM Affordances and Limitations:** By rigorously meta-evaluating LLM-generated rubrics, the research aims to not only assess their utility but also to characterize their inherent strengths and weaknesses. This includes understanding their tendencies regarding criterion granularity, bias in score assignment, and adaptability to diverse scientific domains, thereby providing insights for future improvements in LLM design for structured knowledge extraction and generation.

To address these motivations, the study articulates specific objectives:
1.  To reformulate rubrics into a standardized, checklist-style format suitable for LLM generation.
2.  To systematically evaluate four progressively augmented rubric-generation settings, ranging from basic prompting to agentic scaffolding and distilled procedural skills, across different LLM backbone models.
3.  To conduct a comprehensive meta-evaluation of generated rubrics using both intrinsic metrics (lexical and semantic similarity to human-authored rubrics) and extrinsic metrics (correlation of repository-level scores induced by generated rubrics with ground-truth evaluations).
4.  To analyze the characteristics of LLM-generated rubrics, including their statistics, score distributions, and domain adaptability, through qualitative and quantitative comparisons with human-authored rubrics.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a structured methodology centered on rubric reformulation, augmented generation settings, and a two-pronged meta-evaluation approach (extrinsic and intrinsic).

**4.1 Problem Definition and Rubric Representation**
The study defines rubrics in a checklist-style format, where each rubric item \(r_i\) consists of a criterion \(c_i\) and an associated importance score \(s_i\) ranging from 1 to 5. A complete rubric for a paper P is a set of such items \(R = \{r_1, r_2, ..., r_n\}\). For evaluating a repository's reproduction, an evaluator assigns a binary judgment (Pass/Fail) to each criterion. The final reproduction score is calculated as the weighted sum of passed criteria, normalized by the total possible score from all criteria.

**4.2 Rubric Generation Settings**
The research investigates four incrementally augmented rubric-generation settings, each building on the previous one by adding a new form of assistance to the LLM:

1.  **Direct Prompting:** The base setting where an LLM is given the target paper (P) and a set of manual instructions (I) specifying the desired checklist-style format and scoring standards. The model generates the complete rubric in a single output.
2.  **In-Context Examples:** This setting enhances Direct Prompting by providing human-authored rubric examples (E) from other papers as in-context demonstrations. These examples are intended to guide the LLM on desired structure, criterion granularity, and score allocation patterns.
3.  **Agentic Scaffold:** Building on the previous settings, this approach introduces an iterative, agent-based process (following the ReAct paradigm). Instead of a single pass, the LLM iteratively reasons, inspects its intermediate outputs, and refines criteria. The agent uses an initial prompt and operates within a workspace containing the paper and in-context examples, equipped with system tools like "Read" and "Write" for accessing and saving information.
4.  **Distilled Skill:** The most augmented setting. It leverages a stronger LLM (Claude-Opus) to generate rubrics for a single paper under the Agentic Scaffold. The stronger model then summarizes its rubric-construction procedure, heuristics, and decision patterns into a general-purpose skill document (S). This distilled skill is subsequently incorporated into the prompt of the weaker LLM (Claude-Sonnet or GPT-5.4) within the agentic procedure to guide rubric generation for all other papers, aiming to transfer high-quality procedural knowledge efficiently.

**4.3 Meta-Evaluation Metrics**
Meta-evaluation is conducted from two complementary perspectives:

1.  **Extrinsic Meta-Evaluation:**
    *   **Objective:** To assess how well LLM-generated rubrics align with ground-truth human-authored rubrics in terms of their induced repository-level evaluation outcomes.
    *   **Procedure:** For each paper, multiple reproduction repositories (generated by existing agent-based systems like PaperCoder and HiRAS) are evaluated by an LLM-based evaluator (ChatGPT-4o-mini). This evaluator, given a rubric criterion, retrieves the top-K (K=10) most relevant files from the repository and assigns a binary pass/fail judgment.
    *   **Quantification:** The alignment is measured using Spearman and Pearson correlation coefficients between the score vectors derived from the generated rubric and the ground-truth rubric for a set of repositories. These correlations are then averaged across all evaluated papers.

2.  **Intrinsic Meta-Evaluation:**
    *   **Objective:** To measure the lexical and semantic similarity between model-generated and human-authored rubrics.
    *   **Lexical Level:** For each generated rubric item, its ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L scores (maximum) are computed against all ground-truth rubric items for the same paper, and then averaged.
    *   **Semantic Level:** Semantic matching precision, recall, and F1 scores are calculated between the sets of generated and ground-truth rubric criteria. Unlike some prior work, this study uses embedding cosine similarity (from Qwen3-Embedding-8B) with a threshold (\(\tau=0.7\)) to identify criterion matches, aiming to reduce hallucination risk. A generated criterion is "matched" if it aligns with at least one ground-truth criterion, and a ground-truth criterion is "covered" if matched by at least one generated criterion.

**4.4 Experimental Setup**
*   **Models:** Claude-Sonnet and GPT-5.4 were used for rubric generation. Claude-Opus was used for skill distillation. ChatGPT-4o-mini served as the LLM-based evaluator. Qwen3-Embedding-8B was used for cosine similarity calculations.
*   **Baselines:** A human baseline was established by manually re-annotating PaperBench papers into the checklist-style format, allowing for comparison of how human-authored rubrics align with PaperBench's ground-truth.
*   **Datasets:** Experiments were conducted on the PaperBench dataset, utilizing five reproduction repositories generated by various configurations of PaperCoder and HiRAS for extrinsic evaluation.
*   **Hardware:** All experiments were run on eight NVIDIA L40S GPUs.
*   **Parameter Sensitivity:** The study reports sensitivity analyses for the cosine similarity threshold (\(\tau\)) used in intrinsic evaluation.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a systematic meta-evaluation of LLM-generated rubrics, revealing both the advancements achieved through augmented generation settings and persistent limitations compared to human-authored rubrics.

**5.1 Extrinsic Meta-Evaluation Results**
*   **Improved Alignment with Augmentations:** The study found that each incremental augmentation step (Direct Prompting, In-Context Examples, Agentic Scaffold, Distilled Skill) consistently improved the alignment between model-generated and human-authored rubrics. This improvement was observed across both Claude-Sonnet and GPT-5.4 backbone models, validating the effectiveness of the proposed augmentation hierarchy.
*   **Near Human-Level Performance in Strongest Setting:** The Distilled Skill setting achieved the strongest model-generated performance, significantly closing the gap to the human annotation baseline. For Claude-Sonnet, the Spearman correlation increased from 0.58 (Direct Prompting) to 0.70 (Distilled Skill), approaching the human baseline of 0.83. Pearson correlation also improved from 0.62 to 0.78. GPT-5.4 showed even more pronounced relative gains, improving Spearman correlation by approximately 35% and Pearson correlation by 27% compared to Direct Prompting, also reaching near-human performance.
*   **Increased Rubric Quantity:** Augmented settings generally produced more comprehensive rubric sets, with the average number of criteria increasing substantially (e.g., from 35.95 to 77.95 for Claude-Sonnet), approaching or slightly exceeding the human average of 83.20.
*   **Longer Generated Criteria:** Despite increased quantity, model-generated criteria remained generally longer than human-authored ones. This suggests a tendency for LLMs to decompose implementation details into fine-grained operational steps, whereas human annotations often aggregate details into concise, high-level criteria.

**5.2 Intrinsic Meta-Evaluation Results**
*   **Modest Intrinsic Improvements:** While augmentations improved intrinsic alignment, the gains were less pronounced compared to the extrinsic improvements. Both ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L scores, which measure lexical overlap, consistently increased from Direct Prompting to Distilled Skill for both models.
*   **Recall-Driven Improvements:** The cosine-similarity F1 score, measuring semantic matching, generally improved across augmentation levels, primarily driven by increased recall rather than precision. This indicates that stronger augmentation settings mainly improved the coverage of ground-truth rubric criteria. Precision fluctuated across settings but remained relatively close to the human baseline.
*   **Implication for Granularity:** The pattern of improved recall with less fluctuating precision, combined with longer generated rubrics, suggests that LLMs can generate semantically valid criteria, but often struggle with holistic coverage due to overly fine-grained output. Augmentations helped mitigate this granularity issue by enhancing the coverage of reproduction criteria.

**5.3 Further Analysis**
*   **Paper Category Analysis:** A systematic "code-centric bias" was observed in model-generated rubrics. While human-authored rubrics showed clear category-dependent score distributions (e.g., 48.8% for "Result Match" in Reinforcement Learning papers), model-generated rubrics were much more uniform. They consistently allocated over 60% of the total score to "Code Development" criteria across all categories, with "Result Match" remaining below 21%. This indicates that LLM-generated rubrics were structurally consistent but less adaptive to topic-specific reproduction priorities and often over-weighted implementation details.
*   **Score Distribution Analysis:** Direct Prompting produced a skewed importance-score distribution, concentrating scores at higher importance levels. Incremental augmentations (In-Context Examples, Agentic Scaffold, Distilled Skill) led to more bell-shaped and balanced distributions, indicating improved score calibration. However, a mild bias toward higher scores persisted across all model-generated settings, suggesting an ongoing limitation in score overestimation.
*   **Qualitative Analysis:** A comparison between human-authored and model-generated rubrics for the same DPO un-alignment experiment revealed that model-generated rubrics were typically more fine-grained, decomposing single reproduction components into multiple operational checks. They also explicitly specified granular implementation details (e.g., number of samples, specific hyperparameter values) and assigned relatively higher scores to auxiliary details. In contrast, human-authored rubrics used more concise, higher-level criteria focusing on core reproduction milestones, with peripheral details aggregated into lower-weighted criteria and a more discriminative score allocation. This reinforces the findings of verbosity, fine-granularity, and code-centric bias in LLM-generated rubrics.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research carry several significant implications for the development and evaluation of LLM-based scientific agents and the broader research ecosystem.

**6.1 Scalability and Efficiency in Research Evaluation:**
The most immediate impact is on addressing the scalability bottleneck in the evaluation of open-ended LLM outputs, particularly for complex tasks like paper reproduction. By demonstrating that LLM-generated rubrics, especially with augmented generation settings, can approach human-level evaluation alignment, this work offers a pathway to drastically reduce the substantial expert effort and cost associated with manual rubric construction. This increased efficiency could enable the creation of larger, more diverse, and frequently updated evaluation benchmarks (such as PaperBench), which are critical for robustly assessing and driving progress in automated scientific discovery and experiment reproduction.

**6.2 Enhanced Reliability and Consistency of Evaluation:**
The adoption of rubric-based evaluation is already recognized for its ability to provide more reliable and consistent judgments than holistic evaluations, mitigating issues like hallucination. This research indicates that LLMs can contribute to this robust evaluation paradigm by generating the rubrics themselves. While human oversight remains important, LLM-generated rubrics can serve as a foundation for a more standardized and objective evaluation process, fostering greater trust in the assessment of LLM-generated scientific artifacts.

**6.3 Advancing LLM Capabilities for Structured Knowledge Generation:**
The study provides valuable insights into the current capabilities and limitations of LLMs in extracting, structuring, and generating complex, domain-specific knowledge in a prescriptive format. The success of augmented settings, particularly Agentic Scaffold and Distilled Skill, highlights the importance of iterative reasoning, in-context learning, and distilled procedural knowledge for improving LLM performance on high-stakes tasks. The observed improvements, especially in extrinsic alignment and coverage (recall), suggest that LLMs can be engineered to produce functionally effective structured outputs, even if their intrinsic textual similarity to human outputs is more modest.

**6.4 Guiding Future LLM Development:**
The identified limitations of LLM-generated rubrics—such as overly fine-grained criteria, code-centric bias, and less adaptive score weighting across paper domains—provide clear directions for future research. Developers can focus on refining LLM architectures and prompting strategies to:
*   Control granularity more effectively, encouraging the generation of higher-level, more concise criteria where appropriate.
*   Mitigate inherent biases (e.g., code-centricity) by improving models' understanding of domain-specific priorities and the relative importance of different reproduction stages (e.g., theoretical consistency vs. empirical result matching).
*   Improve score calibration, reducing the tendency to over-assign high importance scores.
*   Enhance adaptability to diverse scientific disciplines beyond machine learning, which may have different reproduction requirements.

**6.5 Assistive Tool for Researchers:**
Ultimately, the framework proposed can function as an assistive tool for researchers and benchmark developers. Rather than replacing human expertise, LLM-generated rubrics can provide a strong initial draft or a comprehensive set of criteria that can then be refined, prioritized, and validated by human experts. This synergistic approach leverages the LLM's capacity for rapid, exhaustive generation while retaining critical human oversight for accuracy, nuance, and contextual understanding in scientific assessment. This can accelerate the feedback loop for developing new scientific agents and fostering a more reproducible research culture.
