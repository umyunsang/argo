## Detailed Research Report: Prompt-Induced Waste in Coding Agents

This report provides a detailed analysis of the research paper "Prompt-Induced Waste in Coding Agents: Reasoning, Effort, Harness Design, and End-to-End Cost," focusing on its contributions to understanding efficiency in AI coding agents.

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research was conducted by Sarel Weinberger and Amir Hozez, both affiliated with PointFive.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The prevailing approach to analyzing the efficiency of coding agents often focuses on quantifiable metrics such as input tokens, output tokens, context size, cache utilization, or the direct cost of the underlying language model. While these metrics are relevant for billing and resource allocation, this paper posits that they are insufficient for characterizing the true efficiency of an agent in performing a task. The authors argue that the same task can result in vastly different computational trajectories, encompassing varying numbers of reasoning steps, model turns, tool calls, repository interactions, edits, tests, and retries.

This work integrates into a broader field of research exploring various facets of AI agent efficiency and behavior. Existing studies have investigated aspects such as prompt-induced agent behavior, the phenomenon of "overthinking" in models, the fidelity of visible chain-of-thought reasoning, strategies for efficient tool selection, cost-aware planning in multi-turn interactions, and context optimization techniques for coding agents. Systems-level contributions, such as the DeepSeek Harness, provide architectural solutions for orchestrating agent capabilities.

This paper distinguishes itself by conducting controlled experiments that isolate and examine the *interactive* effects of prompt semantics, inference effort, and agent harness policies on an agent's end-to-end cost and task success. It departs from analyses that treat these factors as independent controls, instead demonstrating their joint influence on the agent's behavioral trajectory and the ultimate cost of a successful task. By systematically varying these elements and measuring their downstream impact, the research aims to provide a more holistic and actionable understanding of coding agent efficiency, moving beyond purely token-based assessments.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to move beyond token counts and model prices to develop a comprehensive understanding of what determines the *trajectory* an AI coding agent undertakes to solve a problem, and how this trajectory influences end-to-end cost and task success. The paper is motivated by the recognition that efficiency in coding agents is a complex, multi-faceted phenomenon that cannot be reduced to simplistic metrics.

The specific motivations and objectives include:

*   **Deconstructing Efficiency Beyond Token Counts:** The authors contend that current efficiency analyses overlook the dynamic sequence of reasoning steps, model interactions, and tool actions an agent performs. A primary objective is to investigate how these underlying behavioral trajectories are shaped and how they contribute to overall cost.
*   **Investigating Prompt Semantics:** To determine how subtle variations in prompt wording, even when preserving the core task, can alter an agent's internal reasoning, verification strategies, search patterns, branching behavior, and decision to stop. The motivation is to identify specific prompt structures that induce unnecessary computation or "waste" without improving outcomes.
*   **Analyzing Inference Effort:** To assess the trade-off between increased computational effort (e.g., deeper reasoning, more model turns) and task success, particularly on challenging problems. The paper aims to establish whether additional effort consistently yields benefits or can merely escalate costs without commensurate improvements in solution quality or reliability.
*   **Evaluating Agent Harness Policies:** To quantify the impact of different agent runtime environments (harnesses) on baseline computational cost and on the efficacy of prompt and effort interventions. This objective is driven by the observation that harnesses define system prompts, tool schemas, context management, and control flow, which collectively modulate an agent's behavior.
*   **Developing a Holistic Efficiency Metric:** To propose and demonstrate "cost per successful task" as a more appropriate and terminal metric for agent efficiency, which integrates both the economic cost of computation and the utility of task completion. This metric is intended to supersede metrics focused solely on intermediate resource consumption.
*   **Informing Adaptive Control Strategies:** To lay the theoretical and empirical groundwork for designing adaptive controllers that can dynamically manage agent behavior (e.g., adjusting effort, modifying prompts) based on the current state and task characteristics, with the goal of minimizing cost while maintaining a high probability of success.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to provide actionable insights for practitioners and developers designing, evaluating, and deploying AI coding agents, shifting the focus towards optimizing the entire agent trajectory for cost-effectiveness and reliability.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a preregistered, controlled experimental program designed to systematically investigate the impact of prompt wording, inference effort, and agent harness configurations on coding agent performance and cost.

**Models and Serving:**
The study evaluated six open-weight reasoning models (≥500B-class, including DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi-K2.6, Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B, Inkling, and GLM-5.2), primarily served by Together AI. Additionally, Claude Sonnet 5 was evaluated through a protocol-translation gateway to the Anthropic Messages API and directly via its native API.

**Harnesses:**
Three distinct agent harnesses were utilized:
*   **PI.DEV (0.82.1):** Directly interfaced via OpenAI chat completions.
*   **Claude Code (2.1.220):** Accessed via a pinned LiteLLM 1.93.0 gateway that translated requests to OpenAI chat completions, and natively for Claude Sonnet 5.
*   **DeepSeek Harness (dsh 0.1.0rc7):** Used in a post-registration extension, with Claude Sonnet 5, served locally through an OpenAI-compatible chain.

**Tasks:**
The core benchmark comprised 24 deterministic coding tasks (8 low, 8 medium, 8 high complexity) across Python, JavaScript, and Go. Each task included a frozen fixture, visible tests, hidden deterministic tests, and an evaluator. A separate hard-task campaign (SWE-bench Verified slice) used 25 repository-scale instances for effort-level testing.

**Prompt Variants:**
Eighteen prompt variants were generated from frozen templates, manipulating only the user instruction. Nine primary variants preserved the objective, acceptance criteria, and test command verbatim, designed to test specific behavioral hypotheses (e.g., "baseline," "verbose repetition," "deep thinking," "multiple approaches," "max certainty," "bounded efficiency"). Seven "stress" variants intentionally introduced semantic ambiguities or conflicts (e.g., "misleading architecture," "ambiguous scope").

**Experimental Campaigns:**
*   **Core Prompt/Harness Program:** This involved 4,644 valid runs, focusing on prompt wording and harness effects. Experiments were paired within task–model–harness blocks, with results evaluated on an 8-task holdout set.
*   **Hard-Task Effort Campaign (SWE-bench):** This separate preregistered campaign involved 150 paired model–task–effort cells on challenging, repository-scale tasks to assess the cost-success trade-off of explicit inference effort.
*   **DeepSeek Harness Extension:** A post-registration experiment with 75 runs using Claude Sonnet 5 and DeepSeek Harness to study how harness changes influence the value of effort control.
*   **Other Post-Registration Studies:** Included replications with Kimi-K3, a cross-provider study with native Claude Sonnet 5, and robustness checks on paraphrases and repetition stability.

**Metrics:**
A comprehensive set of metrics was recorded:
*   **Behavioral:** Reasoning tokens (provider-reported), visible output tokens, tool calls (by type), turns, files inspected, repeated actions (searches, test runs), time to first edit, wall time, out-of-scope changes, and task success (on visible + hidden tests).
*   **Billing:** Uncached/cached input tokens, logical input, actual cost (at pinned catalog prices), and estimated no-cache cost.
*   **Primary Outcome Metric:** The paired reasoning ratio, calculated as a run's reasoning tokens divided by the median baseline reasoning of the same model–harness–task block. Task-clustered bootstrap intervals were used for significance testing. A variant was classified as "wasteful" if its median ratio exceeded 1.5, with a confidence interval lower bound above 1.1, no material success gain, and the effect appearing on multiple tasks.

**Data Annotation and Trace Analysis:**
For models with exposed reasoning text, 2,801 traces were semantically annotated by a condition-blind judge using a frozen rubric to categorize mechanisms such as candidate strategies, discarded branches, redundant verification, and ungrounded hypotheses. A deterministic tool taxonomy was also applied to analyze tool-layer activity. Claude Sonnet 5 was excluded from trace-level claims due to lack of recoverable reasoning text.

**Cost Model:**
The research defined agent efficiency as cost per successful task (CPS), modeled as a function of task, model, prompt, explicit effort, harness, execution environment, and provider pricing. This model emphasizes that token and cache quantities are consequences of the agent's trajectory, not independent optimization targets.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments identified distinct mechanisms through which prompt semantics, inference effort, and harness design influence agent behavior, cost, and success.

**Prompt Wording and Agent Work:**
Prompt wording significantly altered reasoning volume and end-to-end cost without necessarily changing task success.
*   **"Multiple approaches"** instructions increased reasoning tokens by 2.4–7.4× across six open models, yet produced no statistically credible success benefit. Trace analysis showed these prompts induced approximately 3.5 candidate strategies per run, with around 3 being elaborated and then discarded.
*   **"Deep-thinking"** instructions led to a 1.6–2.2× increase in reasoning text but no median increase in observable functional units (hypotheses, evidence, verification).
*   **"Certainty-oriented"** wording increased reasoning by 1.3–1.9× and introduced redundant re-verification of already-settled facts.
*   **"Scope-expanding"** language (e.g., "adjacent cleanup") was the only prompt family that consistently led to widened code diffs.
*   **"Bounded-efficiency"** templates, specifying scope, smallest-sufficient-change criteria, and a stopping rule, showed no measurable cost penalty and reduced reasoning for one model, with preserved diagnosis and validation on well-specified tasks. However, on ambiguous specifications, this framing reduced evidence seeking, leading to hidden-test failures.
*   Verbatim restatement of the task, by contrast, had approximately unit cost, indicating that prompt *content* rather than mere *length* drives the observed effects.

**Composition of Additional Reasoning:**
Trace annotations revealed the nature of increased reasoning:
*   **Multiple-approach prompts:** Primarily led to the elaboration and discarding of multiple candidate strategies in reasoning text, with only one approach ultimately implemented.
*   **Deep-thinking prompts:** Resulted in more recorded reasoning text but did not increase the number of observable functional units (e.g., new hypotheses or verifications).
*   **Certainty-oriented prompts:** Prompted redundant re-verification of established facts, including re-running already-passing tests.
*   **Misleading architectural hints:** Led to an increase in unsupported assumptions and pre-edit deliberation, and were negatively associated with task success.

**Mechanisms of Excess Cost:**
The dominant waste mechanisms propagated cost through different channels:
*   **Discarded Branches:** Primarily token-borne. Runs with one unused branch incurred approximately 1.9× the median cost of clean runs, while median tool calls remained largely unchanged (7 to 8). This suggests additional exploration occurred within recorded reasoning rather than through repository interactions.
*   **Redundant Verification:** Primarily tool-borne. Certainty-oriented wording led to 1.75 additional post-success tool calls (e.g., extra test executions) and increased latency. Higher levels of redundant verification correlated with up to 18× the raw median cost and 2.5× the tool calls, without a success gradient. Local tools, even without direct monetary charge, created model cost by introducing results into context.

**Harness Effects:**
Harness design exhibited the largest cost differences, often outweighing prompt effects.
*   **Cost Discrepancy:** Claude Code demonstrated 5–30× higher cost per success compared to PI.DEV on matched model–task–prompt triples, despite comparable success rates. This was attributed to Claude Code's 12–15× larger fixed prefix and 2–7× more turns.
*   **Tool Profile Differences:** Claude Code's tool profile was verification-heavy (approximately 50% of calls were test executions) compared to PI.DEV (22% tests, more inspection-heavy).
*   **Interaction with Prompts:** Prompt effects were not invariant across harnesses. For example, "goal-only" prompts reduced reasoning under Claude Code (which supplied methodology via its system prompt) but reduced verifiability under PI.DEV.
*   **DeepSeek Harness Extension:** This extension demonstrated a significant harness–effort interaction. The same effort-control logic, which reduced cost by approximately 19% under Claude Code, achieved a 75% cost reduction under DeepSeek Harness. This indicated that the benefit of effort control is conditional on the harness's default settings and request policy, with dsh exhibiting an aggressive reasoning default that benefited significantly from explicit effort control.

**SWE-bench Effort Campaign (Hard Tasks):**
On difficult repository-scale tasks, increasing explicit inference effort consistently increased compute (2.4–4.5× billed cost).
*   **Opus 5:** Low effort was on the observed cost–success frontier, with higher effort levels not improving success and sometimes leading to regressions (e.g., timeouts, scope-expanding edits).
*   **Sonnet 5:** High effort improved success (7 rescues, 0 regressions) over low effort, but extreme high effort was dominated by high effort.
*   Simulated adaptive escalation, based on evidence triggers, did not improve the cost–success frontier, suggesting that for these tasks, additional compute at baseline was already substantial, and further escalation was not consistently beneficial.

**Other Findings:**
*   **Cache Behavior:** Provider-side prefix caching significantly reduced billed cost (e.g., 61% reduction) but did not indicate behavioral efficiency gains, as agent trajectories remained unchanged.
*   **Kimi-K3 Replication:** Kimi-K3 exhibited a substantially lower baseline deliberation floor, leading to larger relative reasoning ratios for waste-inducing prompts (e.g., 15×) compared to previous generations, yet its absolute waste tokens were often lower or comparable.
*   **Claude Sonnet 5 Cross-Provider Study:** Confirmed that primary prompt effects transferred to the native Anthropic API. "Max certainty" had a particularly large effect on Sonnet 5 under native Claude Code, suggesting harness interaction with this type of prompt.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a system-level account of coding-agent efficiency, emphasizing that true efficiency is a function of interacting factors rather than isolated metrics. Its findings have several significant implications and potential impacts:

**Redefining Agent Efficiency:** The paper establishes "cost per successful task" as the appropriate terminal metric for evaluating coding agents. This shifts the focus from optimizing intermediate resources (e.g., tokens, cache use) to optimizing the entire agent trajectory to achieve successful outcomes economically. This redefinition is crucial for developing benchmarks and evaluation methodologies that accurately reflect real-world value.

**Informed Prompt Engineering:** The detailed analysis of prompt semantics reveals specific instructions that induce "waste" (unnecessary reasoning or verification) without corresponding gains in task success.
*   **Impact:** This provides actionable guidance for prompt designers, suggesting the avoidance of language that demands exhaustive exploration, multiple comparative approaches unless explicitly required, or excessively high certainty. Instead, precise instructions, explicit verification steps, and clear stopping rules are recommended. The finding that "bounded efficiency" language can reduce waste but also risk failures on ambiguous tasks highlights the need for context-aware prompt design.

**Harness Design and Optimization:** The identification of agent harness configuration as the largest measured cost factor underscores its critical role in agent efficiency.
*   **Impact:** This emphasizes that harness-level choices—such as fixed-prefix size, tool-schema exposure, reasoning defaults, turn management, and context compaction policies—have substantial economic consequences. It encourages developers to design harnesses that are lean, minimize unnecessary context, and optimize tool interaction for efficiency. The demonstrated interaction between harness design and the effectiveness of effort control highlights that interventions are not universally beneficial and must be evaluated within specific runtime environments.

**Advanced Agent Control Systems:** The proposed model of agent cost and success, which incorporates the interaction between system variables, directly informs the design of adaptive efficiency controllers.
*   **Impact:** This provides a concrete framework for building intelligent agents that can dynamically adjust their behavior. Instead of fixed global rules, an adaptive controller can learn to condition on the current agent state (task difficulty, observed trajectory prefix, resource state) and select from a small set of safe interventions (e.g., escalating/de-escalating effort, modifying prompt policy, managing tool use) to minimize expected remaining cost while ensuring success non-inferiority. This paves the way for more robust and cost-effective autonomous agents.

**Comprehensive Benchmarking Standards:** The limitations section and the "What an efficiency benchmark must measure" subsection provide a detailed checklist of variables that should be controlled and reported in agent evaluations.
*   **Impact:** This advocates for more rigorous and transparent benchmarking practices. By explicitly tracking factors like task difficulty, model, prompt variant, harness configuration, model round trips, tool calls, and various token counts, benchmarks can distinguish true efficiency gains from artifacts like cache price effects or shifted work.

**Future Research Directions:** The paper concludes by outlining clear next steps, such as a crossed replication of the hard SWE-bench slice under DeepSeek Harness, which will further refine the understanding of harness, effort, and task-difficulty interactions.
*   **Impact:** This provides a roadmap for the research community to build upon these findings, particularly in the development and evaluation of adaptive, context-aware agent behaviors.

In summary, this research moves beyond superficial measures of efficiency to offer a deeper, system-level understanding of how various factors interact to determine the end-to-end cost and success of coding agents. Its implications are fundamental for the design, evaluation, and deployment of more intelligent and economically viable AI agents.
