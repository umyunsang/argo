## Detailed Research Report: Agent-Orchestrated Adaptive RAG

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this paper are Anuj Maharjan, Devinder Kaur, and Richard G. Molyet. All authors are affiliated with the Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science at the University of Toledo, Toledo, OH, USA.

### 2. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advanced capabilities in natural language understanding and generation; however, they possess inherent limitations, notably factual hallucination and restricted access to knowledge beyond their training data. These limitations are particularly critical in applications requiring high factual accuracy and real-time information access, such as DevOps and Site Reliability Engineering (SRE), where precise interpretation of technical documentation is essential.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) emerged as a solution to ground LLM responses in external, verifiable knowledge bases. Conventional RAG pipelines, often termed "Naive RAG," typically involve a static, single-pass procedure: a user query is embedded, a fixed number of top-k similar document chunks are retrieved, and these chunks are appended to the LLM's prompt as context. While effective for simple factual lookups, this approach struggles with complex queries demanding reasoning across multiple documents, chaining intermediate results, or requiring dynamic adaptation based on query characteristics.

To address these limitations, the research landscape has seen the rise of Agentic RAG. This paradigm extends conventional RAG by incorporating autonomous agents that can execute dynamic control flows, including planning, iterative retrieval, and self-critique. Agentic systems can decompose complex queries into sub-questions, gather evidence for each sub-question, and refine their responses through self-reflection. Frameworks such as ReAct, Toolformer, and Self-RAG have contributed to this evolution by formalizing the interleaving of reasoning and actions, enabling tool use, and introducing learned reflection signals.

Despite the growing interest and advancements in Agentic RAG, existing studies frequently evaluate these systems on a single domain or benchmark. This focus leaves a gap in understanding how agentic enhancements perform across diverse domains that exhibit differing structural properties and reasoning demands. This paper contributes to the broader research landscape by specifically addressing this gap through a comparative evaluation of an agent-orchestrated adaptive RAG framework across two distinct types of datasets, thereby characterizing the generalizability and specific tradeoffs of agentic components. The work distinguishes itself by offering empirical insights into the domain-dependent utility of agentic features, advocating for adaptive rather than uniformly aggressive reasoning pipelines.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of conventional RAG systems when confronted with complex, multi-faceted queries, particularly in high-stakes operational environments. Naive RAG's static, single-step retrieval process is insufficient for scenarios requiring iterative information gathering, query decomposition, or self-correction, which are common in real-world knowledge-intensive tasks like those found in DevOps. The authors highlight that while agentic RAG offers promising solutions by introducing dynamic control and iterative reasoning, a comprehensive understanding of its behavior and generalizability across different types of knowledge domains is currently lacking. Most prior work has concentrated on single-domain evaluations, making it difficult to discern when and where agentic enhancements provide genuine benefits versus incurring unnecessary computational overhead.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this work are articulated through three main contributions:

1.  **Implementation of an Agent-Orchestrated Adaptive RAG System:** The first objective was to design and implement a comprehensive RAG framework. This system integrates a Query Classifier, a Query Decomposer, an Answer Evaluator, and a central Orchestrator. A notable aspect of this implementation is its reliance on a fully local, privacy-first inference stack, which is critical for sensitive operational data often encountered in domains such as DevOps.
2.  **Dual-Dataset Evaluation:** The second objective involved conducting an evaluation of the implemented system across two contrasting datasets. These were a custom-developed, domain-specific DevOps knowledge base, characterized by structured information and specific terminology, and MuSiQue, a benchmark designed for compositional multi-hop reasoning in an open-domain setting. This dual-dataset approach was strategically chosen to allow for the observation and analysis of agentic behaviors under divergent conditions of data structure and reasoning complexity.
3.  **Characterization of Tradeoffs:** The third objective was to empirically characterize the performance tradeoffs associated with key agentic components, specifically query decomposition and self-reflection. By comparing performance across the two distinct datasets and analyzing metrics including quality, ranking precision, and latency, the researchers aimed to elucidate the conditions under which these advanced features are beneficial, detrimental, or cost-prohibitive. This characterization contributes to understanding when and how to apply agentic enhancements effectively.

In essence, the research aims to move beyond simply demonstrating the capabilities of agentic RAG to providing empirical evidence on its practical applicability and cost-benefit analysis across different problem types, thereby informing the design of more adaptive and efficient RAG systems.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Agent-Orchestrated Adaptive RAG system extends conventional RAG pipelines by introducing a dynamic, agent-based control layer capable of adaptive query routing, decomposition, and bounded self-correction.

**System Architecture:**
The architecture, depicted in Figure 2 of the paper, contrasts with a standard Naive RAG pipeline (Figure 1). A central **Orchestrator** manages query flow using rule-based logic:
*   Simple queries are routed for direct retrieval.
*   Complex queries trigger a decomposition pipeline.
*   Evaluation failures initiate a reflection mechanism, capped at two retries to control cost.
This adaptive design ensures that resource-intensive agentic processes are invoked only when warranted.

Specialized agents facilitate the adaptive behaviors:
*   **Query Classifier Agent:** Categorizes incoming queries, enabling metadata-aware filtering prior to similarity ranking. This agent leverages document metadata stored in the vector index (e.g., document type) to refine the retrieval candidate space, enhancing semantic precision beyond pure vector similarity.
*   **Query Decomposer Agent:** Addresses multi-hop reasoning requirements by splitting complex queries into ordered sub-queries. Its workflow includes detecting multi-hop structures, generating sequential sub-queries, performing independent retrieval for each sub-query, and finally aggregating the intermediate answers into a coherent final response.
*   **Answer Evaluator Agent:** Implements a self-reflective layer that critiques generated responses across four dimensions: relevance, citation accuracy, grounding in retrieved context (support), and hallucination detection. If issues are identified, the Orchestrator triggers reprocessing, potentially by refining the prompt or adjusting the retrieved context (e.g., selecting different chunks, re-ranking).

**Data Preprocessing and Indexing:**
A robust document ingestion pipeline is crucial for RAG performance. The approach includes:
*   **Structural Conversion:** Raw documents (PDF, DOCX, YAML) are converted to structured Markdown using IBM Docling, preserving visual hierarchy and decomposing content into semantic blocks. This contrasts with standard text extraction that may lose critical structural cues.
*   **Metadata Enrichment:** A custom preprocessing layer injects YAML frontmatter into each document, encoding attributes like document type and service. This metadata supports the Query Classifier's attribute-based filtering.
*   **Semantic Chunking:** Documents are segmented into 600-token chunks with a 100-token overlap. This chunk size was empirically selected to balance LLM context window constraints, retrieval discrimination capacity, and the preservation of technical coherence. The overlap maintains contextual continuity.
*   **Embedding and Indexing:** Each chunk is embedded using the BAAI BGE-base-en-v1.5 model and indexed in a FAISS vector store. The FAISS index, combined with metadata, enables hybrid filtering, where attribute constraints are applied before similarity ranking. In the agentic context, this index is interactive, allowing agents to issue multiple retrieval calls and adjust filters iteratively.

**Implementation Details:**
*   **Generative Core:** Meta's Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct LLM, quantized to 4-bit GGUF, is used for local inference via `llama.cpp`. This choice prioritizes privacy, particularly relevant for sensitive DevOps documentation.
*   **Embedding Model:** The BAAI BGE-base-en-v1.5 embedding model, known for its strong performance in semantic similarity tasks, is utilized.
*   **Vector Search:** FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) is employed for efficient approximate nearest-neighbor search.
*   **Similarity Metric:** Cosine distance is used to compute similarity between query and document embeddings.

**Experimental Setup:**
*   **Datasets:**
    *   **DevOps Knowledge Base:** A custom corpus of 80 documents (approx. 10,000 words) spanning six documentation types (Standard, Architecture, Runbooks, Incidents, Postmortem, Onboarding). This dataset was LLM-assisted in generation and manually validated to reflect realistic, structured, domain-specific knowledge with implicit cross-document relationships.
    *   **MuSiQue [15]:** An open-domain benchmark dataset for compositional multi-hop question answering, specifically designed to require evidence integration across multiple independent documents.
*   **Evaluation Metrics:** A comprehensive set of metrics was used:
    *   **Overall Score:** An aggregate quality measure.
    *   **Critical Source Recall:** Measures retrieval of essential supporting documents.
    *   **Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR):** Assesses the ranking quality of retrieved results.
    *   **Success@5:** Indicates whether relevant results appear within the top 5 retrieved items.
    *   **Citation Accuracy:** Evaluates the correctness of cited sources supporting claims.
    *   **Topic Coverage:** Measures the breadth of information provided in the response.
    *   **Latency:** Quantifies the end-to-end response time.

The experimental design involved comparing the performance of a Naive RAG baseline against configurations employing query decomposition and the full agent-orchestrated system with reflection, across both the DevOps and MuSiQue datasets.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded distinct results regarding the impact of query decomposition and reflection mechanisms, highlighting their domain-dependent utility and associated tradeoffs.

**Impact of Query Decomposition:**
*   **DevOps Corpus:** For the structured, domain-specific DevOps knowledge base, query decomposition consistently improved retrieval metrics. The overall score increased from 0.814 to 0.855, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) rose from 0.556 to 0.722, and Success@5 reached a perfect 1.0. Citation accuracy also improved significantly to 0.917. This indicates that in structured domains, decomposition aids in extracting relevant information and improves the quality of grounded responses. However, this came at a cost, with average latency more than doubling from 21 seconds to 47.66 seconds.
*   **MuSiQue Benchmark:** In contrast, on the multi-hop MuSiQue dataset, query decomposition presented a mixed outcome. While citation accuracy improved to 1.0 and topic coverage increased substantially (from 0.609 to 0.859), ranking-based metrics experienced significant degradation. MRR dropped from 0.469 to 0.102, and Success@5 plummeted from 1.0 to 0.063. This suggests that decomposition, while broadening coverage, can fragment the contextual signal crucial for precise ranking in genuinely multi-hop scenarios, where interdependencies between sub-questions might be complex or less structured. Latency also increased substantially, from 21.84 seconds to 74.87 seconds.

**Impact of Reflection:**
*   The self-reflection mechanism, as implemented, demonstrated limited and inconsistent quality gains, often coupled with substantial latency increases.
*   **DevOps Corpus:** On the DevOps dataset, incorporating reflection actually led to a decrease in overall score (from 0.870 to 0.781) with no improvement in recall or ranking metrics. Latency approximately doubled from 10.57 seconds to 21.78 seconds.
*   **MuSiQue Benchmark:** For MuSiQue, the overall score also decreased (from 0.721 to 0.666), and both recall and MRR declined. While citation accuracy showed a slight improvement, rising from 0.882 to 0.941, this was accompanied by a nearly sixfold increase in latency, from 17.21 seconds to 103.53 seconds.
These findings indicate that the current reflection mechanism imposes a considerable computational burden without consistently delivering commensurate quality enhancements, rendering it inefficient in its present form.

**Strategy Distribution:**
The Orchestrator's routing strategy varied based on the dataset's complexity:
*   **DevOps:** The system primarily routed queries through standard retrieval (69.2%), with only about 5% of queries undergoing decomposition. This aligns with the structured, often single-hop nature of the domain.
*   **MuSiQue:** Complex queries were more prevalent, with approximately 16% being routed for decomposition. This reduced reliance on undifferentiated complex retrieval. The distribution of strategies remained identical between the decomposition-enabled and full-agentic configurations, confirming that reflection acts as a post-hoc corrective step rather than influencing initial routing decisions.
The selective invocation of decomposition, even on MuSiQue where many complex queries were not decomposed, suggests that the current triggering policy is conservative and could be further optimized.

**Tradeoff Analysis:**
The study consistently identified three key tradeoffs:
1.  **Accuracy vs. Latency:** All agentic additions, particularly reflection, significantly increased response time, with reflection on MuSiQue causing an approximately sixfold slowdown.
2.  **Precision vs. Coverage:** Query decomposition broadened retrieval coverage but often diluted the contextual signal, leading to reduced ranking precision (MRR and Success@5) in multi-hop scenarios.
3.  **Control vs. Efficiency:** Modular agentic components enhance interpretability and enable adaptive behavior but introduce additional LLM calls and retrieval operations, which can limit scalability.

**Summary of Findings:**
The core finding is that agentic enhancements are not universally beneficial. Query decomposition is effective in structured domains with well-defined relationships but can degrade ranking precision in more complex multi-hop data. Reflection, in its current design, offers marginal and inconsistent quality improvements at a substantial computational cost. The prevalence of standard RAG in handling a significant portion of queries underscores the necessity of adaptive, cost-aware orchestration, applying expensive strategies only when empirically warranted.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations: the modest scale (80 documents, ~10,000 words) and LLM-assisted generation of the DevOps knowledge base, the small evaluation query sets affecting confidence intervals, the use of a single generator (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and embedding model, rule-based routing heuristics, and environment-dependent latency measurements. These factors qualify the generalizability of absolute metric values but do not undermine the central conclusion regarding the domain-dependent and cost-laden nature of agentic benefits.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides empirical evidence that significantly contributes to the understanding and practical application of Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems. The study's deliberately dual-dataset evaluation approach, contrasting a structured domain-specific knowledge base with a multi-hop reasoning benchmark, addresses a notable gap in existing literature, which often focuses on single-domain evaluations.

**Significance:**
1.  **Empirical Validation of Domain-Dependency:** The work demonstrates that agentic enhancements, specifically query decomposition, are not universally beneficial. Instead, their effectiveness is highly dependent on the characteristics of the knowledge domain and the nature of the queries. This finding challenges the assumption that more complex reasoning pipelines automatically yield superior results across all contexts.
2.  **Highlighting Critical Tradeoffs:** The study systematically characterizes the inherent tradeoffs between retrieval quality (accuracy, precision, coverage) and computational cost (latency). It empirically shows that increased agentic reasoning often comes with a substantial increase in response time, which is a critical consideration for real-time, operational environments.
3.  **Advocacy for Adaptive Orchestration:** By demonstrating that a significant portion of queries can be effectively handled by simpler, less computationally intensive RAG strategies, the research strongly advocates for adaptive, cost-aware orchestration. This paradigm shifts the focus from building uniformly aggressive reasoning pipelines to intelligently selecting strategies based on query complexity and domain requirements.

**Potential Impact:**
1.  **Informed System Design:** The findings can directly guide developers and researchers in designing more efficient and effective RAG systems. Rather than blindly integrating every available agentic feature, system architects can make informed decisions about when to apply complex strategies like query decomposition or self-reflection, optimizing for both performance and resource utilization. This is particularly relevant for latency-sensitive applications like DevOps, where a sixfold increase in latency, as observed for reflection on MuSiQue, would be unacceptable.
2.  **Future Research Directions:** The identified limitations and challenges pave the way for several critical future research avenues. These include developing learning-based, rather than rule-based, adaptive strategy selection mechanisms, incorporating explicit cost models into orchestration decisions, refining query decomposition techniques to better preserve contextual dependencies in multi-hop scenarios, and designing more targeted and efficient reflection mechanisms with fine-grained error detection.
3.  **Development of Robust and Scalable RAG:** By underscoring the importance of efficiency and context-awareness, the research contributes to the development of RAG systems that are not only accurate but also robust, scalable, and economically viable for real-world deployments. The emphasis on a local, privacy-first inference stack also has implications for sensitive data environments, where external API calls might be prohibitive.
4.  **Integration with External Tools:** The discussion on integrating with external tools (knowledge graphs, APIs, symbolic reasoning engines) points towards the evolution of RAG into more comprehensive AI assistants, capable of dynamic interaction with live systems, which could further enhance their utility in domains like DevOps.

In summary, this work provides crucial empirical insights into the practical utility and limitations of agentic RAG, advocating for a nuanced, adaptive approach to its implementation. Its findings are poised to influence the design philosophy and research agenda for the next generation of knowledge-intensive AI systems.
