## Research Report: Reliability without Validity: A Systematic, Large-Scale Evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge Models Across Agreement, Consistency, and Bias

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this research paper are Justin D. Norman, Michael U. Rivera, and D. Alex Hughes, all affiliated with the UC Berkeley School of Information.

### 2. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The evaluation of large language models (LLMs) has increasingly adopted the "LLM-as-a-Judge" (LLMaJ) paradigm, where LLMs are used to mimic human evaluators for tasks requiring analytical assessment. This approach has gained widespread use in both academic benchmarks, such as MT-Bench, Chatbot Arena, G-Eval, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard, and WildBench, and in industrial evaluation pipelines. Despite its proliferation, the LLMaJ paradigm has been associated with documented reliability issues, including inconsistencies across prompts and runs, systematic scoring biases, challenges in adapting evaluation criteria across domains, and a lack of standardized meta-evaluation metrics for comparing judges.

Existing judge validation practices predominantly rely on exact-match agreement. However, prior research has indicated that exact match metrics do not account for chance agreement, which can lead to an overestimation of an LLM judge's discriminative ability. Studies have highlighted various failure modes, such as biases inherited from pretraining data, temperature sensitivity, and inconsistencies under prompt variations. While some efforts have been made to improve reliability through dedicated judge architectures or by introducing reliability coefficients, a comprehensive, large-scale evaluation across multiple model families, generations, benchmarks, and evaluation protocols has not been previously conducted.

This work addresses this gap by presenting a systematic and extensive evaluation of LLMaJ reliability. It builds upon the understanding of individual reliability challenges by quantitatively assessing these issues across a diverse cohort of LLM judges using a multi-faceted protocol. The study aims to provide empirical evidence for the limitations of current validation metrics and to propose a more robust framework for evaluating LLM judges.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct the largest systematic evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge models to date, assessing their reliability, consistency, and bias across a broad spectrum of models, providers, and benchmarks. This comprehensive evaluation is motivated by several critical factors:

*   **Dominance of LLMaJ:** LLM-as-a-Judge has become the prevalent evaluation method for LLMs, extensively deployed in both research and production environments. This widespread adoption necessitates a thorough understanding of its underlying reliability.
*   **Documented Reliability Issues:** Despite its popularity, previous work has identified persistent reliability concerns with LLM judges, including inconsistency, systematic biases (e.g., position, verbosity), and weak domain-specific calibration. These issues pose risks to the validity of LLM evaluations.
*   **Limitations of Current Validation Metrics:** A significant motivation is the over-reliance on exact-match agreement for judge validation. This metric fails to correct for agreement that could occur by chance, thereby systematically overstating a judge's actual discriminative ability. The paper highlights that established benchmarks and newer successors often privilege raw agreement, contributing to a potentially misleading perception of judge performance.
*   **Absence of Large-Scale, Multi-Protocol Evaluation:** Prior research lacked a large-scale, cross-benchmark, multi-protocol evaluation that spans diverse model families and generations. Such an evaluation is crucial to identify consistent reliability patterns and diagnose systemic limitations inherent in the LLMaJ paradigm.
*   **Need for Improved Validation Standards:** The cumulative effect of these issues underscores the need for more robust and comprehensive validation protocols. The research aims to distill its findings into actionable recommendations, ultimately proposing a "Minimum Viable Validation Protocol" to enhance the trustworthiness and scientific rigor of LLM judge evaluations.

By systematically quantifying agreement (chance-corrected), consistency (test-retest stability), and various biases, the study seeks to expose the nuances of LLMaJ reliability beyond superficial metrics and provide a foundation for more valid and equitable model evaluations.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic, large-scale evaluation across 21 LLM judges, nine providers, three benchmarks, and three distinct evaluation protocols, generating approximately 541,000 individual judgments over 118 runs. All evaluations were conducted at a temperature of 0 for deterministic generation.

**Model Selection:**
Twenty-one general-purpose LLM judges were selected from nine providers. These models were categorized into three tiers:
*   **Tier 1:** Widely-deployed production judges.
*   **Tier 2:** Cost-conscious models.
*   **Tier 3:** April 2026 frontier models and open-source systems.
The selected models encompassed a range of parameter counts, from 8B to over 100B, and included models with "thinking-architecture" capabilities where reasoning channels were suppressed for consistent evaluation.

**Benchmarks:**
Three benchmarks were used, representing different types of evaluation tasks:
*   **MT-Bench:** Consists of 2,391 pairwise comparisons with expert human judgments, primarily focusing on general conversational quality and aesthetic preference. It is widely used for judge evaluation.
*   **JudgeBench:** Comprises 350 items across mathematics, coding, creative writing, and analysis, labeled for objective correctness.
*   **RewardBench:** Includes 2,981 chosen-versus-rejected pairs. For this study, per-item position randomization was applied to ensure that the chosen response was equally likely to occupy either position, addressing a known issue where human labels were consistently in position A.

**Evaluation Protocols:**
Three protocols were implemented to measure different aspects of judge behavior:
*   **Agreement Protocol:** This protocol produced a single judgment per item and compared it against the human label. Metrics included:
    *   **Exact match (EM):** The proportion of items where the judge's verdict matched the human label, using a tie-excluded variant for pairwise comparisons.
    *   **Cohen’s κ:** A chance-corrected measure of agreement between two raters on categorical labels. This was the primary agreement metric.
    *   **Krippendorff’s α:** A reliability coefficient accommodating more than two raters and various scale types, coinciding with κ for the two-rater nominal case.
*   **Consistency Protocol:** This protocol involved N ∈ [3, 5] independent evaluations per item with response caching disabled. Each pair was presented in both AB and BA orderings. Metrics included:
    *   **Test-retest reliability:** The agreement of a judge with itself across independent re-evaluations, capturing stability of verdicts.
    *   **Self-consistency:** The proportion of items where the majority verdict across runs agreed with each individual run.
    *   **Position flip rate:** The fraction of items where the judge's verdict changed when response order was swapped.
*   **Bias-Audit Protocol:** This protocol utilized AB+BA orderings along with response-length analysis. Metrics included:
    *   **Position bias:** The tendency of a judge to favor a response in a particular position, operationalized as |P(A wins) - 0.5|.
    *   **Verbosity bias:** The tendency of a judge to prefer longer responses regardless of quality, operationalized as the Pearson correlation between response-length differential and the judge's verdict.

**Diagnostic Concepts:**
The study introduced two diagnostic concepts:
*   **Kappa deflation (∆κ):** Defined as the difference between exact match and Cohen’s κ (EM - κ), quantifying how much raw agreement overstates chance-corrected discriminative ability.
*   **Consistency-bias paradox:** An empirical observation where high test-retest reliability (α > 0.95) coexists with severe position bias (> 0.10), indicating a judge that is highly reproducible but not valid.

**Experimental Procedure:**
The evaluation was executed in seven phases over a five-week period (March-April 2026). This included initial smoke tests, sequential evaluation of Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 models across the various protocols, ensuring consistency and controlled conditions (e.g., temperature 0, disabled response caching for consistency, and position-swap debiasing). A bespoke research evaluation library, intended for open-source release, was used for all computations. Seven hypotheses (H1-H7) were formulated *a priori* based on early phase observations, before the evaluation of frontier models, to guide the analysis.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The large-scale evaluation yielded five principal findings and confirmed the utility of the introduced diagnostic concepts, consistently observed across the entire cohort of 21 judges, including the frontier models released up to April 2026.

1.  **Universal Kappa Deflation:** Every judge in the study exhibited substantial kappa deflation on MT-Bench. Exact match scores systematically overstated chance-corrected agreement (Cohen’s κ) by 33.8 to 41.3 percentage points (pp), with a cohort mean of 38.6 pp. This deflation was universal across all capability tiers, supporting hypothesis H1. The magnitude of deflation varied with the benchmark's label distribution: MT-Bench (balanced ternary) showed the largest deflation, followed by JudgeBench (pairwise correctness, mean 23.7 pp), and RewardBench (binary chosen-vs-rejected, mean 10.4 pp). For instance, an LLM judge reporting 85% exact match on MT-Bench typically corresponded to a κ of approximately 0.48.

2.  **Heterogeneity in Position Bias:** Position bias varied significantly among models, spanning nearly two orders of magnitude from 0.002 (Gemini 2.5 Pro) to 0.192 (Qwen 3 8B). While reasoning and frontier models generally exhibited lower position bias, it was not eliminated; only one of the three "thinking-architecture" models (Gemini 3.1 Pro) met the a priori prediction of position bias below 0.05 (H2 partially refuted). Notable within-family heterogeneity was observed, such as a 70-fold difference between Gemini 2.5 Pro and Gemini 2.5 Flash.

3.  **Cross-Benchmark Rank Instability:** Judge rankings demonstrated considerable instability across different benchmarks, supporting hypothesis H3. Over half (11 of 21) of the models shifted by four or more positions between benchmarks. The largest shift was observed in Llama 3.3 70B, which dropped 15 positions from MT-Bench to JudgeBench. Only two judges (Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6) maintained a top-three position across all three benchmarks. This instability was amplified by differences in benchmark discriminability: MT-Bench compressed all 21 judges into a narrow 13.5 pp κ band, whereas JudgeBench spread them over a 60.4 pp range (4.5 times wider).

4.  **MT-Bench Ceiling Effect and RewardBench Validity:**
    *   **MT-Bench:** The κ spread on MT-Bench for the full cohort was 13.5 pp (0.376 to 0.511), which was wider than the a priori prediction of at most 5 pp (H4 partially confirmed). However, the spread narrowed to 6.5 pp within the top ten models, approaching the hypothesized ceiling. This indicated that MT-Bench's preference-style label set compressed meaningful quality differences, especially among strong judges.
    *   **RewardBench:** Contrary to hypothesis H5, per-item position-randomized evaluation revealed that RewardBench generated valid κ values ranging from 0.616 to 0.898 across all 20 evaluated judges. This demonstrated that, with appropriate randomization, RewardBench could provide a discriminative agreement signal.

5.  **Consistency Degradation and the Consistency-Bias Paradox:**
    *   **Consistency Degradation:** Consistent with hypothesis H6, seven of the sixteen judges evaluated under the consistency protocol showed a position flip rate increase of at least 1.5 times when moving from MT-Bench to the harder JudgeBench. For example, Llama 3.3 70B's flip rate increased by 3.3 times. The mean test-retest reliability across the cohort also decreased from 0.943 on MT-Bench to 0.911 on JudgeBench.
    *   **Consistency-Bias Paradox:** Hypothesis H7 was confirmed, as two judges exhibited the consistency-bias paradox: Qwen 3 8B (test-retest 0.992, position bias 0.192) and Gemini 2.5 Flash (test-retest 0.988, position bias 0.125). These judges demonstrated high reproducibility in their outputs but also severe systematic position bias, indicating that high test-retest reliability alone does not guarantee validity.

6.  **Reduced Verbosity Bias:** All 21 judges registered a verbosity bias below 0.011 on MT-Bench, with 17 judges falling below 0.005. These magnitudes are an order of magnitude smaller than previously reported in 2023-era studies, suggesting that verbosity sensitivity has substantially decreased across recent model generations under the specific pairwise rubric used.

7.  **Model Family Analysis:** Anthropic judges showed strong joint performance on hard items and the lowest cohort-level bias. Generational progression was more discernible on JudgeBench than on MT-Bench. Mid-tier judges like Kimi K2.5 demonstrated competitive performance in specific dimensions (e.g., low position bias) at a lower cost than frontier models.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic and large-scale empirical foundation for understanding the reliability and limitations of LLM-as-a-Judge models, with significant implications for both research practices and industrial deployment.

**Methodological Advancements:**
The study introduces and quantifies "kappa deflation" and the "consistency-bias paradox," which are critical diagnostic concepts for robust judge validation. By demonstrating the systematic overstatement of discriminative ability by exact-match metrics and the misleading nature of high test-retest scores in the presence of bias, the paper contributes to a more nuanced understanding of judge reliability. It establishes the largest dataset and evaluation framework for LLMaJ to date, which will be released as a community resource for future meta-analysis, development of calibration techniques, and establishing baselines.

**Practical Implications and Recommendations:**
The findings translate into direct, actionable recommendations for practitioners and researchers:
*   **Chance-Corrected Evaluation:** The universal kappa deflation underscores that raw exact match figures are insufficient and misleading. The research advocates for the mandatory reporting of chance-corrected metrics, such as Cohen’s κ or Krippendorff’s α, as the primary indicators of judge reliability.
*   **Multi-Benchmark Validation:** The observed cross-benchmark rank instability demonstrates that no single benchmark provides a reliable measure of a judge's general performance. It recommends evaluating judges on at least two benchmarks with contrasting label structures (e.g., preference-style vs. correctness-style) to achieve a more comprehensive assessment.
*   **Joint Assessment of Consistency and Bias:** The consistency-bias paradox highlights that high test-retest reliability can mask severe systematic biases. This necessitates a joint measurement of consistency and bias to avoid deploying judges that are reproducibly flawed.
*   **Re-evaluation of Verbosity Bias:** The significantly reduced verbosity bias (under the specified pairwise rubric) suggests that this particular bias may be less critical in current-generation LLMs compared to earlier models, allowing evaluation efforts to focus on other persistent biases.

**Minimum Viable Validation Protocol (MVVP):**
The study distills these recommendations into a "Minimum Viable Validation Protocol," providing a concrete set of steps for validating LLM judges before deployment:
1.  Report Cohen’s κ (or Krippendorff’s α) as the headline reliability number.
2.  Measure position bias via paired AB+BA evaluations.
3.  Measure test-retest reliability over multiple independent runs.
4.  Cross-validate on at least two benchmarks.
5.  Audit for the consistency-bias paradox, ensuring low position bias when test-retest is high.

**Future Research Directions:**
The paper outlines several avenues for future research, including investigating multilingual and multimodal judging, studying the temporal stability of hosted models, exploring judge-confidence calibration, and conducting formal variance-component decompositions to better inform sample size and replicate count decisions in future validation efforts.

In summary, this work provides a robust critique of existing LLMaJ validation practices and offers a scientifically grounded, multi-faceted approach to assessing their true reliability. Its impact lies in improving the rigor of LLM evaluation, promoting more transparent reporting, and guiding the development of more trustworthy LLM-as-a-Judge systems.
