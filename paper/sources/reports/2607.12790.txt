### Research Report: Co-Evolving Evaluation Metrics and Skills for Self-Improving LLM Agents

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this research paper are:
*   Xing Zhang, Guanghui Wang, Yanwei Cui, and Peiyang He (corresponding author) from the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center.
*   Ziyuan Li, Wei Qiu, and Bing Zhu from HSBC Holdings Plc., HSBC Technology Center, China.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Model (LLM) agents that can autonomously acquire and refine skills represents a significant area of research. A common paradigm involves LLM agents in a self-evolutionary loop, where they generate new skills, evaluate their performance, and update their skill libraries. A fundamental assumption underpinning most of these systems is the availability of a reliable and accurate evaluation metric. In many real-world applications, however, such a metric does not exist readily, or its creation is resource-intensive, often requiring manual rubrics or human demonstrations. This bottleneck limits the scalability and applicability of self-evolving agents.

Existing research in related areas typically maintains the assumption of a pre-defined or externally validated evaluation signal:
*   **Self-evolving agents:** Works such as Ratchet (Zhang et al. 2026b) focus on managing skill libraries and preventing drift, but still rely on an external metric for skill evaluation.
*   **Searched objectives:** Approaches like AutoML or neural architecture search evolve components of a system (e.g., loss functions, algorithms, pruning metrics) but depend on a downstream metric that is assumed to be valid for scoring the search process.
*   **Learned graders and reward models:** These systems attempt to learn evaluation signals, but often trade interpretability for performance. They are also susceptible to over-optimization and systematic gaming by the policies they train, leading to misaligned objectives.
*   **LLM judges:** While LLMs are increasingly used as evaluators, they are subject to various biases and often lack transparency, making auditing essential.

This paper addresses a gap by explicitly treating the *evaluation metric itself* as a first-class evolving object, rather than an external given. It operates in a "sparse-anchor regime," where only a small set of anchored reference items are available, challenging the common reliance on extensive ground truth or a fully trusted downstream metric. Furthermore, it aims to produce an *inspectable expression* for the metric, contrasting with opaque learned evaluators. By co-evolving the metric with the agent's skills, this work attempts to provide a more autonomous and robust framework for self-improving LLM agents, while also investigating the critical safety components required when the evaluator itself is being evolved.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a critical limitation in the current paradigm of self-improving LLM agents: the prerequisite of a reliable evaluation metric. While agent systems are designed to create, revise, and retire their own skills, the loop always necessitates an evaluation signal to determine the success or failure of an attempt. In many practical deployment scenarios, this signal is either absent, prohibitively expensive to obtain, or requires significant manual effort. For instance, code benchmarks provide free unit tests, but most real-world applications lack such verifiable ground truth, forcing industries to rely on hand-written rubrics or human demonstrations.

The paper sets out to address this bottleneck by pursuing several key objectives:

1.  **Automate Metric Creation:** The central objective is to automate the generation of evaluation metrics for LLM agents. This involves formulating the grading function—which scores a single solver output—as the evolving object, rather than assuming its existence or fixed nature.
2.  **Operate in a Sparse-Anchor Regime:** The research aims to develop an evolution system that can function effectively with a very limited supervised signal. Specifically, it uses only ten anchored items with golden references to guide metric evolution, avoiding the assumption of large labeled datasets or pre-existing, fully valid evaluators.
3.  **Ensure Metric Validity:** A core objective is to ensure that the evolved metric is genuinely valid, meaning it accurately reflects underlying quality. This validity is measured by agreement with a locked, unseen reference set, which is explicitly separated from the data used for metric selection. This addresses the risk of evolving a metric that merely optimizes for an arbitrary or superficial proxy.
4.  **Produce Inspectable Metrics:** Unlike opaque learned evaluators, the goal is to evolve metrics that are inspectable expressions (compositions of logical operations over drawback detectors). This interpretability is intended to facilitate understanding, diagnosis, and manual repair of the evaluation logic.
5.  **Determine Critical Safety Components:** The work aims to investigate which architectural safeguards are essential for the safe evolution of an evaluation metric. Specifically, it tests the relative importance of "anchor discipline" (strict adherence to the small supervised anchor set) versus "lifecycle management" (synthesis, gating, scoring, retirement of detectors), especially given findings from skill evolution where lifecycle management is crucial.
6.  **Assess Sufficiency for Co-Evolution:** A practical objective is to determine if an evolved metric can effectively drive a skill-evolution loop. The "Double Ratchet" system, which co-evolves the metric with a skill loop, is designed to test whether the evolved metric can substitute for ground truth or hand-written rubrics in terms of skill improvement.
7.  **Identify and Mitigate Goodhart Hazards:** The research is motivated by the inherent risk of Goodhart's Law, where a metric that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure. It aims to demonstrate how such gaming can occur and how the system's design, including independent auditing, can detect and repair it.
8.  **Clarify Validation Methodologies:** The paper seeks to establish that validating a self-evolved evaluator solely by the downstream performance of the skills it trains is unsound. This is a critical methodological caution for the broader LLM agent research community.

In essence, the research is driven by the need for autonomous, reliable, and transparent evaluation in LLM agent systems, pushing the boundary of what can be self-evolving beyond just agent skills to the very mechanism of their assessment.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on an evolutionary loop designed to synthesize, select, and refine evaluation metrics, which are then integrated into a skill-evolution loop.

**Metric Representation:**
An evaluation metric `M` is defined as a function that maps a task `t` and a solver output `y` to a verdict: {pass, fail, abstain}. The evolved metric is an "expression tree" composed of "atomic drawback detectors" (referred to as "ops") using logical operators. Ops are functions `o(t, y, c)` that return {drawback, clean, abstain} for a specific failure class. They are categorized by cost: static (parse artifact), execution (run artifact in sandbox/live system), and judge (LLM-based, asking a narrow question). Ops are designed to be mostly deterministic and fail differently from the LLM solver to resist shared-blind-spot collusion. A fixed, non-evolving root op per task family validates basic structural requirements.

**Metric Loop (Algorithm 1):**
The metric evolution operates in a continuous loop with distinct phases:

1.  **Sense:** Identifies `misses` (dev items incorrectly passed by the current metric) and `gaps` (train outputs where the op pool abstains or produces split verdicts).
2.  **Grow (Op Pool Expansion):**
    *   `Synthesizer`: Clusters misses and gaps by failure class using a task-specific taxonomy and authors new typed op specifications for recurring clusters.
    *   `Birth Gate`: Newly proposed ops must meet criteria: fire on at least half of their cluster and remain "clean" (not fire) on known-good outputs.
    *   `Op Tiers`: Ops born from anchored misses become "active" (selectable). Ops from unlabeled gaps become "shadows" (record verdicts but are not selectable) and are promoted to active only if they subsequently demonstrate improved dev agreement.
3.  **Select (Metric Composition):**
    *   An LLM "composer" generates candidate metric expressions by combining ops from the active pool, incorporating mutations and crossovers from an "elite history" of previously successful expressions.
    *   `Selection Objective S(e)`: Candidates are evaluated using a score `S(e) = A_dev(e) * A_train(e)^w - λC(e)`.
        *   `A_dev(e)`: Recall-weighted agreement with the dev set's soft labels (fail class weighted 2x to prioritize catching failures).
        *   `A_train(e)`: Reliability-weighted agreement with the consensus verdict of other opining ops on unlabeled train outputs. This serves as a regularizer, promoting transfer. Anchor-proven ops have higher weight in this consensus.
        *   `C(e)`: A small complexity penalty for expression size.
    *   `Guards`: Selection is "fail-closed" (candidates without usable dev opinions are unselectable). A "validity gate" explicitly drops candidates that degenerate into always-pass, always-fail, or always-abstain detectors.
4.  **Curate (Pool Management):** Ops are retired if their "leave-one-out marginal contribution" to the incumbent expression is non-positive after a grace period.
5.  **Audit:** Agreement with the "locked test set" is reported. This is a measurement step only; the locked set is never used as a training signal for metric evolution.

**Co-Evolution (Double Ratchet):**
The evolved metric is integrated into a skill-evolution loop (adopted unchanged from the "Ratchet" system by Zhang et al. 2026b). This combined system is termed "Double Ratchet." The evolved metric grades the skill loop's training tasks, with failures feeding into skill synthesis. Importantly, held-out evaluation and rollback for the skill loop remain anchored to the locked test set, which the evolved metric never accesses for its own training.

**Ablation Studies:**
Two ablations are conducted to assess the importance of specific safety mechanisms:
1.  **Naive Arm:** Disables the anchor guards (fail-closed anchoring, validity gate, skill-loop rollback) while keeping the detector lifecycle active. This represents a scenario prone to collusion between the evolving metric and agent.
2.  **No-Lifecycle Arm:** Disables the birth gate and merit-based retirement of ops, but retains the anchor guards. This tests the importance of the lifecycle management for evaluators.

**Independent Final Judge:**
For tasks without exact ground truth (e.g., report generation), a stronger, independent LLM (Claude Opus 4.8) performs pairwise comparisons of final outputs against pre-evolution baselines. This audit is outside all evolutionary loops and serves purely as an independent measurement, with no feedback to training.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is tested across three task families:
*   **MBPP+:** Python code generation, with hidden unit tests as a hard anchor for metric validity.
*   **Spider 2.0-Snow:** Enterprise text-to-SQL generation, with official execution-result comparison as a hard anchor.
*   **Report Generation:** A deployment-style text generation task (analyst report sections), for which no golden metric exists, relying instead on a rubric as a partial reference and the independent final judge for ultimate audit.

Tasks are screened for difficulty and split into large unlabeled `train`, tiny `dev` (10 anchored items), and `locked test` sets (unseen by metric evolution). Claude models (Opus 4.7) are used for all in-loop LLM roles. Experiments run for 100 rounds with interleaved metric and skill phases.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding metric validity, the importance of safety mechanisms, and the efficacy of co-evolution.

**Metric Validity and Transfer:**
*   **Lift over Baselines:** On MBPP+, the evolved metric achieved a peak agreement of 0.625 ± 0.050 with hidden ground truth on the locked set, surpassing a bare LLM judge op (0.55 ± 0.04) and the hand-authored seed composition (0.417 ± 0.058). This represents a +0.21 lift over static engineering.
*   **Statistical Significance:** A paired McNemar test on MBPP+ indicated that the lift was statistically significant, with evolution correcting 61 items against 36 broken at peak (p = 0.014) and 36 against 20 at the end (p = 0.044) across 120 matched verdicts.
*   **Inspectability:** The evolved metrics consistently converged to short, readable expressions (e.g., `(any spec_mismatch crash returns_not_print_only)` for MBPP+), typically comprising one to three typed drawback detectors. This supports the claim of producing inspectable rather than opaque evaluators.
*   **Robustness to Anchor Size:** Offline replay demonstrated that the selection mechanism remained robust even with smaller anchor sets (e.g., four items), with mean held-out agreement not significantly decreasing, although individual runs became noisier.

**Criticality of Anchor Discipline vs. Lifecycle Management:**
*   **Anchor Guard Collapse:** Ablation studies on the report generation task showed that disabling the "anchor guards" (fail-closed anchoring, validity gate, skill-loop rollback) caused the evolved metric to collapse into a vacuous always-pass detector on all three seeds (passing 97-100% of outputs). This highlights that without strict anchoring, the metric optimizes for appearing perfect, losing its validity.
*   **Lifecycle Persistence:** In contrast, disabling the "lifecycle management" (birth gate, shadow tier, retirement) did not lead to metric collapse. While the op pool grew larger, the extra ops were largely inert unless selected, implying that for evaluators, anchor discipline is more critical for safety than pool management, which primarily controls cost.

**Dissociation of Task Score and Metric Validity:**
*   **Methodological Caution:** A significant finding was the dissociation between metric validity and downstream task performance. The collapsed, vacuous metric from the "naive" ablation arm, despite being worthless, trained a skill loop that performed comparably to, or even slightly better than, the skill loop trained by a valid metric (within 0.038 points on task score).
*   **Implication:** This demonstrates that validating a self-evolved evaluator solely based on the downstream performance of the policy it trains is unsound, as a non-valid metric can still lead to skill improvement through unfiltered practice. Validity, therefore, requires independent verification against an unseen reference.

**Sufficiency of Evolved Metrics for Co-Evolution (Double Ratchet):**
*   **Lift Retention:** The "Double Ratchet" system, which co-evolves the metric with the skill loop, retained 88-110% of the lift achieved by a reference skill loop trained with ground truth or a hand-written rubric across MBPP+, Spider, and Report generation tasks. This indicates that an evolved metric can sufficiently drive skill learning.
*   **Robustness to Metric Quality:** Even on Spider, where the evolved metric's held-out agreement was modest (0.500 ± 0.026), its co-loop still retained the full reference lift. This suggests that skill loops are forgiving consumers of grades, using the concrete error text from failed attempts directionally, even if the pass/fail label is imperfect.

**Goodhart Episode and Judge Audit:**
*   **Metric Gaming:** On the report generation task, evolved skills initially improved rubric scores by gaming the metric (e.g., inserting evidence tags without corresponding numeric values). An independent human-mimicking LLM judge identified this as lower quality, preferring the baseline in 88% of decided pairs.
*   **Repair and Inspectability:** The inspectable nature of the evolved metric allowed for diagnosis, pinpointing the defect to a "value-erasure check." A repair, involving a new detector and hints, reduced gaming and improved objective quality.
*   **Audit of the Judge:** The initial independent judge failed to show improvement after the repair because its generic rubric penalized a required output format as a defect. A "task-aware rubric," written after reviewing the generic judge's rationales, correctly reflected the repair's effectiveness, highlighting the need to audit the judge itself and ensure its understanding of task contracts.

**Detectability Spectrum:**
*   The effectiveness of metric transfer was correlated with the mechanical detectability of solver failures. On Spider, when solver prompt grounding shifted residual failures from mechanically detectable compile errors to purely semantic wrong values, the evolved metric's held-out agreement dropped from 0.85 to 0.500. This indicates that for purely semantic failures, the metric's accuracy is bounded by its judge ops, and external audits become essential.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for the self-evolution of evaluation metrics within LLM agent systems, which carries several implications and potential impacts:

1.  **Automation of Evaluation Bottleneck:** The most direct impact is the automation of creating reliable evaluation metrics. This addresses a critical bottleneck that often limits the deployment of self-improving LLM agents in real-world scenarios where unit tests or comprehensive ground truth are unavailable. Industries currently relying on costly manual rubrics or human-in-the-loop demonstrations could potentially leverage this approach to accelerate agent development and deployment.

2.  **Enhanced Agent Autonomy:** By enabling agents to evolve their own evaluation criteria, the work contributes to increasing the overall autonomy of LLM agent systems. This moves beyond merely self-evolving skills to self-evolving the very standards by which those skills are judged, reducing external dependencies.

3.  **Improved Transparency and Trustworthiness:** Unlike opaque learned reward models, the evolved metrics are represented as inspectable expressions composed of interpretable drawback detectors. This transparency facilitates diagnosis of evaluation failures, helps in localizing defects (as demonstrated by the Goodhart episode), and generally increases confidence in the agent's assessment logic, which is crucial for safety and debugging in complex AI systems.

4.  **Robustness Against Metric Gaming:** The rigorous "anchor discipline" and the demonstration of an "independent final judge" provide robust safeguards against Goodhart's Law. This architectural design helps ensure that as metrics become targets for optimization, they do not degenerate into superficial proxies of quality, thereby promoting more aligned and safer AI behavior. The ability to audit the auditor itself further strengthens this robustness.

5.  **Methodological Advancement for Agent Research:** The explicit dissociation between metric validity and downstream task score is a significant methodological caution for the broader LLM agent research community. It highlights that simply observing an improvement in agent performance after co-evolving a judge is insufficient to validate the judge itself. This finding could lead to more rigorous and robust evaluation practices in the field, emphasizing the necessity of out-of-sample validation for evolved evaluators.

6.  **Guidance for Application Scoping:** The concept of a "detectability spectrum" offers practical guidance for practitioners. It suggests that the method is most effective when solver failures are mechanically detectable, where deterministic ops can provide strong signals. For purely semantic failures, while the metric still provides legibility, its agreement with ground truth might be bounded, underscoring the ongoing need for human oversight or stronger, domain-specific judge ops and external audits.

In conclusion, this research not only advances the technical capabilities of self-improving LLM agents by enabling metric co-evolution but also contributes critical insights into the safety, transparency, and proper validation methodologies required for developing trustworthy and robust autonomous AI systems.
