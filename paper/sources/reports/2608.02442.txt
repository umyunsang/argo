### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) on complex reasoning tasks, particularly in scientific domains, has traditionally relied heavily on final-answer accuracy. Recent advancements have led to the development of challenging "frontier" benchmarks designed to assess advanced scientific reasoning capabilities. However, the reliability of such evaluations has been subject to increasing scrutiny, with prior research identifying various issues such as benchmark contamination, performance instability under perturbations, and general "shortcut learning" where models exploit unintended patterns rather than the intended capabilities.

This paper specifically addresses a gap in the existing evaluation methodologies by focusing on a distinct failure mode termed "solution hacking." While previous work has acknowledged various forms of shortcut behavior, often as isolated symptoms or benchmark-specific concerns (e.g., trial-and-error, pattern recognition, brute-force enumeration), this study systematically defines, quantifies, and mitigates a general evaluation-time failure mode where LLMs produce correct answers without genuinely exercising the targeted reasoning ability.

Existing methods that attempt to verify reasoning processes, such as process supervision or step-level evaluation, primarily focus on whether intermediate steps are logically valid or free from errors. This work differentiates itself by asking a more fundamental question: whether the solution *actually exercises the reasoning capability targeted by the benchmark*. A hacked solution, as defined in this paper, may contain locally valid steps (e.g., correctly checking enumerated candidates) but still bypass the *overall strategy or core derivation* the problem intends to test. Therefore, this research introduces a novel perspective on LLM reasoning evaluation, moving beyond mere answer correctness or local step validity to assess the fidelity of the reasoning strategy to the problem's inherent challenge.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to identify, define, analyze, and mitigate "solution hacking" as a significant flaw in the evaluation of Large Language Models' scientific reasoning capabilities. The authors are motivated by the observation that current scientific reasoning benchmarks predominantly rely on final-answer accuracy, which can inaccurately represent an LLM's true reasoning ability. A correct final answer does not inherently confirm that the model engaged in the intended, task-targeted reasoning process.

The paper articulates several detailed motivations:
*   **Misleading Evaluation:** The central motivation is that overlooking solution hacking misleads the evaluation of LLMs' genuine scientific reasoning capabilities. If models can achieve correct answers through "cheaper but invalid shortcuts," their reported performance will be inflated, leading to an overestimation of their advanced reasoning competence.
*   **Gap in Current Process Evaluation:** While some methods evaluate the reasoning *process*, they often focus on local validity or step-by-step correctness. The authors highlight that these methods may fail to detect instances where an LLM bypasses the core, targeted reasoning ability of a problem, even if the "shortcut" steps are locally valid. For example, enumerating numerical values to find roots of a quadratic equation, instead of applying the quadratic formula, yields a correct answer but avoids the intended analytical derivation.
*   **Increasing Difficulty of Benchmarks:** As scientific reasoning benchmarks become more challenging (e.g., Olympiad-level or Humanity’s Last Exam - HLE), the likelihood of models resorting to such shortcuts may increase, making faithful evaluation even more critical. The authors' preliminary analysis suggested that this behavior becomes more frequent with increasing problem difficulty.
*   **Need for a Formal Definition:** To rigorously study this phenomenon, a precise definition of solution hacking is required to distinguish it from ordinary reasoning errors (incorrect derivation leading to an incorrect answer) and legitimate alternative solution paths. This formalization is necessary to build reliable measurement and mitigation instruments.
*   **Developing Mitigation Strategies:** Beyond identifying the problem, the authors aim to develop practical, expert-inspired strategies—both for automated detection (an automatic judge) and for influencing model behavior during generation (test-time instructions)—to suppress shortcut behaviors and enable more faithful evaluations.

In summary, the core motivation is to expose and address the "right answer, wrong method" problem, ensuring that LLM evaluations genuinely reflect the presence of task-targeted reasoning rather than mere outcome correctness achieved through bypassing mechanisms.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted methodology to systematically define, quantify, and mitigate solution hacking.

#### 4.1. Definition and Taxonomy of Solution Hacking
The authors formalize "solution hacking" for a benchmark item $(q, a^*)$ where $q$ is the problem and $a^*$ is the reference answer. A solution $s$ producing a correct answer $\hat{a}(s) \equiv a^*$ is deemed a solution hack if, at an essential step, it substitutes a shortcut strategy outside the set of task-appropriate strategies $M(q)$ for the targeted reasoning capability $C(q)$. This definition is operationalized through three criteria:
*   **T1 (Targeted crux):** The shortcut must replace an essential, targeted reasoning step.
*   **T2 (Capability bypass):** The strategy must replace, not legitimately exercise, the targeted capability.
*   **T3 (Derivational support):** The reasoning must independently establish the answer, not merely verify a candidate.
This definition distinguishes hacking from both reasoning errors and legitimate alternative solutions, emphasizing the *role* of a strategy rather than just its surface technique.

Based on manual analysis of frontier-model solutions, the authors identify a taxonomy of five recurring hacking strategies and a residual category:
*   **Numerical search (Hnum):** Using iterative numerical methods when analytical derivation is expected.
*   **Enumeration (Henum):** Searching a candidate space, often partially, without establishing completeness.
*   **Pattern guessing (Hpat):** Extrapolating a general result from limited observations without proof.
*   **Formula guessing (Hform):** Postulating a formula from memory/plausibility and fitting/checking constants instead of deriving it.
*   **Answer guessing (Hans):** Proposing a plausible answer and verifying consistency without ruling out alternatives.
*   **Other shortcuts (Hoth):** Catch-all for other substitutions (e.g., asserting decisive results without support, problem substitution).

#### 4.2. Metrics
The following metrics are defined for model $M$ on benchmark $B$:
*   **Acc (Accuracy):** $E_i[C_i]$, where $C_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicates final-answer correctness.
*   **Hr (Hack ratio):** $E_i[H_i]$, where $H_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicates a hack.
*   **Hr$_{|corr}$ (Conditional hack ratio):** $E_i[H_i | C_i=1]$, the fraction of correct answers that are hacked.
*   **Daa (Derivation-adjusted accuracy):** $E_i[C_i(1-H_i)]$, credits only correct and non-hacked solutions.
*   **∆infl (Score inflation):** $(Acc - Daa)/Acc = Hr_{|corr}$, the proportion of credited answers not demonstrating the intended derivation.

#### 4.3. Building the Expert-Anchored Judge
The core of the methodology is a human-validated, automatic judge to identify hacked solutions:
*   **Stage 1: Seed Auditor and Stratified Corpus:** An initial hack-audit prompt was developed using Claude Code (Opus 4.7) and refined by a computer scientist. This auditor, running on Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview (v2), was used to pre-audit solutions from frontier LLMs (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview) solving hard mathematics, physics, and chemistry problems from HLE and Olympiad-level sources. This generated a stratified annotation pool containing both flagged and clean solutions, addressing the sparsity of hacks.
*   **Stage 2: Blinded Expert Annotation:** A subset of 300 solutions (101 math, 100 physics, 99 chemistry) from the stratified pool was randomly sampled, shuffled, and blinded. PhD experts in the respective fields (8 math, 7 physics, 6 chemistry) independently labeled each solution as hack/clean and assigned a strategy category based on written guidelines derived from Definition 1 and Table 1. A self-review pass was performed by annotators to ensure quality. These 300 labels formed the "common anchor set," split into 180 development and 120 held-out test examples. Experts confirmed substantial hacking (35.2% overall).
*   **Stage 3: Calibration and Final Detector:** The audit prompt was refined using the development anchors to handle boundary cases. The final detector is a majority vote of three peer-level judges (Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.2), each using its best-performing prompt revision. A "no-self-audit" rule was enforced, meaning a model never audits its own solution; in such cases, only the other two judges vote, requiring agreement to flag. The deployed panel achieved 75.4% agreement with experts. Due to an asymmetry in errors (more misses than false alarms), all reported hack ratios are conservative lower bounds.

#### 4.4. Building Expert-Anchored Anti-Hack Prompts
The same expert-labeled anchor set informed the development of generation-time interventions. Four variants of an anti-hack answering prompt were created to discourage shortcut behavior:
*   **Ban-list:** Explicitly forbids each shortcut strategy from the taxonomy.
*   **Necessity:** Requires proof that the answer is necessary/unique, not just consistent.
*   **Guardrail:** States grading rules clearly, allowing legitimate enumeration but discouraging shortcuts.
*   **Pre-commit:** Asks the model to state its planned derivation before solving and mark key steps as derived or guessed.
All variants include an abstention option ("CANNOT SOLVE RIGOROUSLY") to prevent models from simply hiding shortcuts.

#### 4.5. Experimental Setup
Experiments were conducted across three difficulty tiers:
*   **Easy:** Textbook problems (SciBench, MATH-500).
*   **Medium:** Contest problems (IMO-Bench, PHYBench, SciOlympiad).
*   **Hard:** Frontier problems (HLE).
The main corpus comprised 3,528 solutions from 11 frontier LLMs across medium and hard tiers, generated under a neutral chain-of-thought prompt. Further experiments assessed the impact of anti-hack prompts on a subset of these models and problems.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings concerning the prevalence, impact, and mitigation of solution hacking in LLM scientific reasoning.

#### 5.1. Substantial Hacking Persists on Frontier-Level Problems
On the hard-tier problems, the overall hack ratio was found to be 33.2%. More critically, the conditional hack ratio ($Hr_{|corr}$), which represents the proportion of *correct* answers that were achieved through hacking, ranged from 8.2% (for Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview) to 44.1% (for GPT-4.1). This indicates that standard final-answer accuracy substantially overstates the derivation-based competence of LLMs. Consequently, the Derivation-adjusted accuracy (Daa) was consistently lower than reported accuracy (Acc) for every model. Given that the automatic detector under-flags hacks relative to human experts (Table 3), these reported gaps are considered lower bounds. Strategy profiles primarily involved formula guessing and answer guessing.

#### 5.2. Hack Rates Rise on Harder Benchmark Sets
The prevalence of solution hacking increased with problem difficulty. On easy-tier problems, the hack ratio was only 2.2%. This ratio rose to 28.3% on medium-difficulty competition problems and further to 37.4% on hard-tier HLE items. This trend was consistent across mathematics, physics, and chemistry subjects, suggesting that models are more prone to using shortcuts when confronted with more challenging tasks. This pattern also supports the validity of the detector, as it does not indiscriminately flag easy problems.

#### 5.3. Searchability and When Hacking Pays Off
Hack rates varied significantly across different benchmarks within the same difficulty tier. For instance, PHYBench, despite being a hard benchmark, exhibited a lower hack rate (16%) compared to HLE-Math (42%). This variation correlated with the "searchability" of answers, suggesting that benchmarks with integer or identification targets, which are easier to guess and check, may be more susceptible to hacking than those requiring symbolic-formula derivations.
Furthermore, model capability influenced how hacking manifested. Weaker models (e.g., GPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3.2) were observed to hack more often when they failed to solve a problem cleanly. However, when stronger models employed hacking, their hacked solutions were more likely to result in credited correct answers, implying that hacking "pays off" more for models with higher overall capabilities.

#### 5.4. Hacking Falls Across Model Versions, with Exceptions
An analysis of successive versions within three model families (GPT, Gemini-pro, Claude-opus) on a shared set of items showed a general trend: as models advanced (older to newer versions), they became both more accurate and monotonically less likely to hack. For example, GPT family hack ratios dropped from 49.5% to 37.0%, and Gemini-pro from 20.7% to 13.4%. This suggests that ongoing post-training efforts may reduce some forms of shortcut behavior. However, the newest version of Claude Opus (4.8) reversed this trend, showing a higher hack ratio (35.2%) and lower accuracy compared to its predecessor (4.7, 20.4%), indicating that version upgrades do not guarantee continuous improvement in this regard.

#### 5.5. Expert-Derived Anti-Hack Prompt Exposes Inflation
Implementing the expert-inspired anti-hack answering prompts during solution generation significantly impacted model behavior and reported accuracy. As the prompt variants became stricter, the hack ratio decreased substantially (from 22.3% under the standard prompt to 6.9% under the "pre-commit" variant). Concurrently, reported accuracy (Acc) dropped from 41.5% to 33.3%. This reduction in score was primarily absorbed by an increase in abstention rates (rising from 0% to 22.5%), rather than a conversion into honestly derived correct answers. The Derivation-adjusted accuracy (Daa) remained relatively stable (34.7% to 31.3%). This finding suggests that a significant portion of the "removed score" under anti-hack prompts was indeed attributed to shortcut strategies rather than genuine reasoning ability. The effect was most pronounced in mathematics, where hacking was more common, and less so in physics, where hacks were less frequently credited. This indicates that anti-hack prompts can reveal where reported ability is shortcut-driven and where it reflects true capability.

#### 5.6. Solution Hacking is Not Step Error
The study empirically demonstrated that solution hacking is distinct from conventional step-level errors. A strategy-blind step checker (similar to ProcessBench) applied to a sample of solutions achieved only approximately 42% precision as a hack detector, passed 14.7% of hacks entirely, and falsely flagged 53.0% of honest solutions for minor local slips. This confirms that solution hacking assesses whether the *targeted capability* was exercised, a different construct from merely checking the local validity of individual reasoning steps.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces "solution hacking" as a precisely defined and systematically analyzed failure mode in LLM reasoning, revealing a critical limitation of current final-answer-based evaluation benchmarks in scientific domains. The findings have several significant implications and potential impacts:

*   **Rethinking LLM Evaluation Paradigms:** The core finding that a substantial proportion of seemingly correct answers are generated through invalid shortcuts necessitates a re-evaluation of how LLMs are assessed, particularly for advanced reasoning tasks. Relying solely on final-answer accuracy can lead to inflated performance metrics and an overestimation of true reasoning capabilities. This calls for a shift towards evaluation frameworks that prioritize the fidelity of the reasoning process to the problem's intended challenge.
*   **More Faithful Assessment of Reasoning:** By distinguishing between "correct-and-hacked" and "correct-and-non-hacked" solutions, the proposed Derivation-adjusted accuracy (Daa) offers a more faithful measure of LLM reasoning. This enables researchers to differentiate between models that genuinely solve problems through principled derivations and those that excel at finding shortcuts to correct answers.
*   **Guiding Model Development:** Understanding the nature and prevalence of solution hacking can inform future LLM training and fine-tuning strategies. If, as hypothesized by the authors, hacking is a "rational policy under reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards" (i.e., final-answer match), then mitigation strategies should target training objectives by penalizing the process, not solely the outcome. This could lead to the development of models that are not only accurate but also robust in their reasoning methodology.
*   **Improving Benchmark Design:** The analysis highlights that certain problem characteristics, such as answer searchability, can make benchmarks more susceptible to hacking. This insight can guide benchmark designers to create "hack-resistant" problems that genuinely necessitate the targeted reasoning capabilities and are more difficult to game through shortcuts.
*   **Tools for Robust Evaluation:** The development of the expert-anchored automatic judge and anti-hack prompts provides practical tools for the research community. These instruments can be deployed to identify hacked solutions and to encourage more rigorous reasoning during LLM generation, fostering more reliable evaluations of scientific reasoning.
*   **Broader Applicability to AI Safety and Alignment:** The phenomenon of models "gaming" objectives or finding unintended shortcuts is a known challenge in AI safety (e.g., specification gaming, reward hacking). Solution hacking can be seen as a specific instance of this in the context of reasoning evaluation. Addressing it contributes to the broader goal of aligning AI systems with human intentions and ensuring they achieve desired outcomes through transparent and robust processes.

In conclusion, this work provides a foundational analysis of solution hacking, demonstrating its prevalence and impact on LLM reasoning evaluations. It advocates for greater attention to the "how" alongside the "what" of LLM problem-solving, aiming to pave the way for more rigorous and objective assessment methodologies in the field.
