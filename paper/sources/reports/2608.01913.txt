### Report: Diagnosing Search Behavior and Failure Modes in Long-Horizon Search Agents

**1. Authors and Institution(s)**

The research was conducted by Qi Liu and Jiaxin Mao from Renmin University of China, Beijing, China, and Fengbin Zhu and Tat-Seng Chua from the National University of Singapore, Singapore.

**2. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape**

Deep search agents, which are large language model (LLM)-based systems, are designed to address complex, multi-hop information-seeking questions by iteratively performing search queries, gathering evidence, and synthesizing information. Existing evaluation practices for these agents predominantly focus on final-answer metrics, such as accuracy on benchmarks like BrowseComp. This approach renders the internal trajectory of the agent largely opaque, hindering a comprehensive understanding of agent behavior, identifying inefficiencies, and diagnosing failure mechanisms.

The current research addresses this gap by proposing a diagnostic framework that scrutinizes the agent's search trajectory at a granular level. While other studies have analyzed aspects of agent search behavior, such as log-scale requests or retrieval backends, this work distinguishes itself by integrating per-query, human-annotated, document-level relevance judgments. This integration, applied across multiple agents within a consistent harness and retriever setup, allows for deterministic attribution of failures to either retrieval or utilization issues, facilitating direct and comparable analysis across different systems. The work is positioned as orthogonal to efforts focused on building or evaluating agents end-to-end, instead concentrating on identifying which specific steps within a long-horizon search process contribute to successful outcomes.

**3. Key Objectives and Motivation**

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing evaluation methods for deep search agents, which often obscure the relationship between search effort and answer quality. The authors identified three concrete challenges:
1.  **Misinterpretation of Wasted Effort as Capability:** Long search horizons and extensive tool use are often showcased as agent strengths. However, an efficient agent should adapt its search effort to the problem's actual requirements, rather than engaging in excessive, potentially unproductive, searching by default. Without trajectory-level analysis, it is difficult to differentiate between genuine capability and inefficient over-searching.
2.  **Misdiagnosis of Bottlenecks:** When an agent produces an incorrect answer, the failure is frequently attributed solely to an inability to locate necessary evidence, leading to interventions focused on improving search capabilities. This overlooks instances where crucial evidence might have been retrieved but subsequently misused or misinterpreted by the agent's reasoning component. Differentiating between these failure types is essential for prescribing appropriate remedial actions.
3.  **Ambiguity Between Persistence and Floundering:** Long search trajectories may reflect either sustained progress towards an answer or prolonged, unproductive searching. Current evaluation metrics, which typically consider only the final answer, cannot distinguish between these scenarios, making it difficult to assess the quality of an agent's iterative search process.

To address these challenges, the research established the following key objectives:
*   **Outcome Attribution:** To determine which aggregate signal – search effort (e.g., search volume, context consumption) or gold-evidence recall – is a more reliable indicator of an agent's final answer accuracy.
*   **Failure-Mode Taxonomy:** To develop a systematic classification of agent errors, distinguishing between "retrieval gaps" (where necessary supporting evidence is never found) and "utilization gaps" (where relevant evidence is retrieved but not correctly applied to formulate an answer).
*   **Trajectory Mechanism and Query Strategy Analysis:** To analyze the step-by-step dynamics of agent trajectories, specifically tracking when and where evidence is retrieved, assessing the utility of individual search episodes, and characterizing the query reformulation strategies employed by agents.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aimed to provide a systematic characterization of long-horizon search agent behavior and their failure modes, thereby identifying practical directions for developing more effective and efficient deep research systems.

**4. Methodology and Approach**

The study employed a diagnostic framework centered on human-annotated, document-level relevance judgments to analyze the internal workings of long-horizon search agents.

**Agent Settings:**
The research focused on six open-weight agents categorized into two scale tiers, all operating under a uniform ReAct-style search-and-reason paradigm.
*   **Mid-scale tier:** Tongyi DeepResearch, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and gpt-oss-120b. These models were served locally from their released checkpoints.
*   **Frontier-scale tier:** Kimi K2.6, GLM 5.1, and Deepseek V4 Pro. These larger models were accessed via their respective provider APIs.
All agents utilized a consistent ReAct harness, which recorded reasoning traces, tool calls, tool returns, and the final answer for each question. Trajectories were capped at 128 turns or 150 minutes, with context overflow or tool errors also leading to early termination.

**Tool Interface:**
Agents interacted with the environment via two tools:
*   **Search:** Issued a query and returned the top-K (K=5) results retrieved by Qwen3-Embedding-8B. Each result included a document ID, retrieval score, and a snippet truncated to 512 tokens.
*   **Visit:** Fetched the full document text based on a given document ID.

**Benchmark and Evaluation Signals:**
The primary benchmark was BrowseComp-Plus [6], comprising 830 English questions against a fixed, approximately 100K-document corpus. This benchmark was selected due to its provision of human-annotated, per-query, document-level relevance judgments (`qrels`), which were critical for grounding the diagnostic framework.
*   **`evidence-qrels` (E(q)):** A broader set of documents topically relevant to the question. This was used for cross-agent attribution and episode-level mechanism analyses.
*   **`gold-qrels` (G(q)):** A stricter subset of `evidence-qrels` containing documents sufficient to derive the correct answer. This was used for classifying failure modes.
End-to-end answer correctness was evaluated by GPT-4o, providing binary correct/incorrect labels, consistent with the official BrowseComp protocol. Retrieval-side metrics, however, were derived directly from the human-annotated `qrels`, making them independent of LLM judgment noise.

**Diagnostic Analysis Framework:**
The analysis was structured across three perspectives:
1.  **Outcome Attribution:** This involved tracking the cumulative retrieval of evidence over time. An "episode" was defined as a sequence starting with a search call and including subsequent visits until the next search. Metrics included cumulative evidence (`C_k(ξ)`) and new evidence (`Δ_k(ξ)`). Cross-agent recall was measured as padded cumulative recall at K=25 episodes, while estimated accuracy under K-episode caps (`dAcc_K`) was also calculated.
2.  **Failure-Mode Taxonomy:** Incorrect answers were categorized into:
    *   **Retrieval Gap (RG):** The agent failed to retrieve any `gold-qrels` documents.
        *   *Directional RG:* No `evidence-qrels` documents were retrieved.
        *   *Last-hop RG:* `evidence-qrels` were retrieved, but `gold-qrels` were missed.
    *   **Utilization Gap (UG):** The agent retrieved `gold-qrels` documents but still produced an incorrect answer.
        *   *True Extraction Failure UG:* All `gold-qrels` were retrieved, but the answer was still wrong.
        *   *Boundary UG:* Only part of the `gold-qrels` was retrieved, and the answer was wrong.
    An oracle reader experiment, where `evidence-qrels` or `gold-qrels` were directly provided to GPT-4o, was conducted to establish a ceiling for answerable questions, thus identifying irreducible utilization gaps.
3.  **Trajectory Mechanism and Query Strategy:**
    *   **Episode Utility:** Episodes were classified as "productive" (adding new evidence), "redundant" (retrieving existing evidence but no new information), or "unproductive" (retrieving nothing).
    *   **Context Budget:** The components filling the context window (search snippets, visit content, reasoning traces, overhead) were quantified to understand resource consumption.
    *   **Query Strategy:** Query reformulations were categorized based on Jaccard overlap with the previous query: "repeat" (≥0.85), "pivot" (<0.25), and "add/drop_constraint" (in between).

**Validation:**
To assess the generalizability of the findings, a subset of the analysis was re-run on BrowseComp-en, utilizing a commercial open-web search API. Due to the absence of `qrels` in this setting, an LLM judge was employed to assess search/visit utility and overall information sufficiency.

**5. Main Findings and Results**

The diagnostic analysis yielded five principal findings, tracing the characteristics of long-horizon search agents from aggregate performance to specific behaviors.

**Finding 1: More Effort Does Not Necessarily Lead to More Accuracy.**
Across the six evaluated agents, there was no strong positive correlation between search volume, number of `visit` calls, or context window consumption and final answer accuracy. GLM 5.1, the highest-performing agent with 74.1% accuracy, exhibited the lowest median search calls (14) and context usage (52K tokens). Conversely, gpt-oss-120b, the lowest-performing agent (38.0% accuracy), performed the most searches (33 calls) and consumed a higher median context (86K tokens). This indicates an inverse relationship between search effort and accuracy in many cases (Pearson r = 0.16 for context budget vs. accuracy).

**Finding 2: Retrieval Recall Is a Strong Predictor of Accuracy.**
In contrast to search effort, the quality of retrieved evidence, specifically cumulative retrieval recall of `evidence-qrels`, showed a strong correlation with answer accuracy (Pearson r = 0.99). Agents with higher recall generally achieved better accuracy. For every agent, the mean `gold-qrels` recall exceeded its answer accuracy, suggesting that agents often retrieve sufficient evidence but fail to utilize it effectively. Within individual questions where agents disagreed on whether `gold-qrels` evidence was retrieved, agents that successfully retrieved it answered correctly in 73.8% of cases, while those that missed it were correct in only 2.2% of cases. Controlling for `gold-qrels` recall absorbed 56-60% of the performance difference between stronger agents and gpt-oss-120b.

**Finding 3: Failures Are Attributable to Both Retrieval and Utilization Gaps.**
Agent failures were decomposed into retrieval gaps (RG) and utilization gaps (UG). For five of the six agents, RG dominated errors, accounting for 51.6–64.1% of incorrect answers. Kimi K2.6 was an exception, with 52.0% of its errors attributed to UG. This highlights that agents with similar overall accuracy can have distinct failure profiles, necessitating different improvement strategies (e.g., query improvement for RG, reasoning/verification for UG). Within RG, "directional gaps" (no evidence found) comprised 55–67% of errors, indicating an initial misdirection in search. For UG, Kimi K2.6 primarily faced "true extraction failures" (71.4% of its UG), where all `gold-qrels` were present but the answer was still incorrect. An oracle reader, given full `evidence-qrels` or `gold-qrels`, achieved 87.5% accuracy, suggesting an irreducible utilization gap of 12.5% that even a perfect reader could not overcome. Analysis of wrong answers also revealed significant "format or subtle" errors (17.7–19.9% for top agents), suggesting evaluation artifacts contribute to UG. The 26-point spread in `gold-qrels` recall across agents on a fixed retriever indicated that RG is primarily a query-formulation issue, not a retriever limitation.

**Finding 4: Useful Evidence Arrives Early, Followed by a Wasted Tail of Search Efforts.**
Trajectory analysis revealed that a substantial majority of search episodes (77–94%) added no new evidence. Only 6–23% of episodes were "productive." Useful evidence, when retrieved, tended to concentrate in the early episodes of a trajectory. For example, roughly half of all cumulative recall was achieved within the first five episodes. Incorrect trajectories were 1.9–2.9 times longer than correct ones, yet their "productive phase" (until the last new evidence was found) was only marginally longer. The extended length of incorrect trajectories was primarily due to a "wasted tail" of redundant or unproductive searches. Furthermore, search snippets, rather than visited document content, were the dominant component of the context window, occupying 66–85% of tokens. This indicates that context overflow (e.g., Tongyi-DR's 38% overflow rate) is largely driven by accumulating snippets from numerous searches, not extensive document visits.

**Finding 5: Stronger Agents Exhibit Cleaner Search Behavior, Not Necessarily More Search.**
The behavioral signature of higher-performing agents was characterized by query discipline. Stronger agents, particularly GLM 5.1 (0% repeat rate) and Deepseek V4 Pro (0.3% repeat rate), rarely re-issued near-identical queries. The "repeat rate" of queries was the strongest negative behavioral correlate with accuracy (Spearman 𝜌 = -0.83). While agents differed in query style (e.g., Kimi K2.6 favored broad pivoting, GLM 5.1 preferred anchor-then-verify), neither style uniformly dominated. The ability to "land" pivots, i.e., to retrieve relevant evidence from new query angles, was more critical than the pivot rate itself. Stronger agents also often employed parallel query batching (e.g., GLM 5.1 with 1.87 queries per turn), which positively correlated with accuracy (𝜌 = +0.75), indicating turn efficiency. High search volume was found to be a symptom of a failing trajectory rather than a mechanism for recovery.

**Finding 6: Findings Generalize to Open-Web Search.**
The core dissociations observed on the fixed BrowseComp-Plus corpus were largely replicated in an open-web setting using BrowseComp-en and a commercial search API. For the four agents evaluated, accuracy correlated strongly with LLM-judged evidence coverage (r = 0.99) but weakly with total search calls (r = 0.12). Retrieval gaps continued to dominate errors (68–88%), and the context window remained predominantly filled by search snippets. This suggests that the identified behavioral patterns and failure modes are not artifacts of the controlled corpus but generalize to more realistic search environments.

**6. Significance and Potential Impact**

This research contributes a diagnostic framework grounded in relevance judgments, offering a fine-grained analysis of search agent trajectories that moves beyond end-to-end accuracy metrics. The comprehensive study of six diverse agents provides empirical evidence supporting the dissociation between search effort and answer quality, while highlighting the critical role of effective evidence retrieval. The planned release of trajectories and code will further support follow-up research and comparisons.

The findings have several practical implications for the development of deep research systems:
*   **Targeted Improvements for Weaker Agents:** For agents primarily suffering from retrieval gaps, the focus should be on enhancing query formulation. This includes improving the direction of queries, enabling agents to recognize and pivot away from failed hypotheses, and preventing the re-issuance of redundant or near-paraphrased queries. The observed 26-point spread in `gold-qrels` recall on a fixed retriever suggests substantial headroom for query-side improvements.
*   **Shift in Focus for Stronger Agents:** For agents nearing the oracle reader ceiling, the primary remaining headroom lies in addressing utilization gaps. This implies a shift from deepening search to strengthening verification mechanisms, improving answer normalization (to mitigate format or subtle errors), and robustly integrating retrieved evidence into a coherent and accurate final answer.
*   **Harness-Level Optimizations for All Agents:** Implementing improvements at the system harness level can significantly boost efficiency. These include sophisticated snippet-stream management (e.g., deduplication, intelligent trimming, aging out irrelevant snippets), visit gating to prevent opening low-value pages, and transitioning from fixed search budgets to evidence-driven stopping criteria. Such changes could reduce wasted search effort and context consumption, addressing the "wasted tail" observed in many trajectories.

The study also provides insights into how current agents differ from human searchers. While humans often engage in "berrypicking," where information needs evolve with accumulating evidence, agents frequently maintain an initial framing despite a lack of new evidence. Furthermore, human search behavior is influenced by satisficing and the economic cost of search, whereas agents tend to continue until hard caps are met or an answer is produced. This suggests that incorporating a more nuanced "internal sense of marginal utility" for new queries and evidence could lead to more human-like and efficient agentic search.

Ultimately, the research quantifies the diagnostic headroom available for improving search agents, suggesting that future gains will come not from deeper search, but from better-directed search and more robust evidence utilization. Realizing these gains will necessitate targeted changes in agent training, prompting strategies, and tool design. The finding that different agents fail on largely disjoint sets of questions also indicates that no single agent currently represents the field's ultimate performance ceiling, pointing to the potential for synergistic multi-agent systems.
