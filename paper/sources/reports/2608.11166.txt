### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research was conducted by Audrey Quessadda-Vial, affiliated with PwC.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid evolution of Agentic AI systems from isolated large language model (LLM) applications to complex software composed of interacting agents, tools, prompts, skills, and composite subsystems presents new challenges for configuration management and governance. Existing paradigms and tools address various aspects of this landscape, but a notable gap persists, which this work aims to address.

**Traditional Software Configuration Management (SCM)** principles, such as immutable revisions, controlled baselines, and change management, have proven effective for conventional software engineering. However, their direct application to agentic systems is not straightforward. Agentic systems rely on heterogeneous configurable artifacts, dynamic execution structures, and runtime-generated elements that fall outside the scope of traditional SCM models, which primarily focus on source code, libraries, and build configurations.

**AI Governance frameworks** (e.g., NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, European AI Act) establish principles for responsible AI development, emphasizing accountability, transparency, risk management, and auditability. These frameworks define organizational objectives and compliance requirements but do not specify a common technical configuration model for heterogeneous agentic systems. Similarly, **Provenance mechanisms** (e.g., W3C PROV, SBOMs, SLSA) enhance traceability for software packages, datasets, and build processes, but do not represent the semantic configuration of a complete agentic system, which includes elements like agents, prompts, models, and policies.

The emergence of LLM-based applications has led to **LLMOps and AgentOps platforms** (e.g., Langfuse, Helicone, LangSmith). These platforms provide operational support for prompt management, execution tracing, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, and observability. While they offer capabilities like prompt versioning and execution lineage, their focus remains on the operational lifecycle of AI applications and runtime management rather than framework-independent configuration governance. Configuration information within these platforms is often tied to platform-specific abstractions, limiting a unified view.

Concurrently, numerous **Agentic Frameworks** (ee.g., LangGraph, CrewAI, OpenAI Agents SDK) have been developed to design and execute autonomous or multi-agent applications. These frameworks orchestrate agents, coordinate interactions, and integrate LLMs with external tools. However, their internal representations of configuration artifacts, dependency structures, and lifecycle conventions are closely coupled to their specific execution models, making interoperability and consistent governance across different frameworks challenging.

This work identifies a crucial gap: the absence of a **common semantic model** for representing, versioning, and governing heterogeneous agentic configurations independently of their execution environment. The paper positions Agentic Configuration Management (ACM) as a framework-independent governance layer that complements existing SCM, AI governance, LLMOps/AgentOps, and agentic execution frameworks by providing a unified configuration representation and consistent governance semantics. ACM aims to bridge the gap by adapting established SCM principles to the unique characteristics of agentic systems while remaining agnostic to specific execution technologies.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for introducing Agentic Configuration Management (ACM) stems from the increasing complexity, dynamism, and heterogeneity of modern agentic systems. As these systems evolve from standalone LLM applications to intricate networks of interacting components (agents, tools, prompts, skills, models, execution workflows), managing their configurations becomes a significant challenge. Current approaches, including existing LLMOps/AgentOps platforms and agentic orchestration frameworks, often provide framework-specific solutions, leading to fragmented configuration management. This fragmentation hinders crucial engineering practices such as reproducibility, consistent impact analysis, and long-term maintenance across diverse agentic ecosystems. The paper highlights the need for a framework-independent approach to describe, validate, and govern these systems as coherent wholes.

The key objectives of this work, articulated as four main contributions, are:

1.  **Propose a framework-independent governance representation**: To establish a universal way to represent heterogeneous agentic configuration artifacts. This includes defining typed and independently versioned Agentic Configuration Items (ACIs) and explicit typed relationships that capture their configuration dependencies for governance purposes. This abstraction aims to allow artifacts from different frameworks to be described and managed uniformly.
2.  **Formalize governance semantics**: To define the lifecycle models, state propagation mechanisms, assurance semantics, and runtime governance principles for agentic configurations. This formalization provides a structured basis for understanding how configuration states evolve and how changes impact dependent components.
3.  **Present a reference operationalization**: To demonstrate the practical feasibility of the ACM model through a Python reference implementation. This includes developing semantic projection adapters that map configurations from heterogeneous agentic frameworks, such as LangGraph, CrewAI, and the OpenAI Agents SDK, into the common ACM representation.
4.  **Evaluate representational coverage and operational feasibility**: To assess the model's ability to represent diverse configurations and the implementation's adherence to the formal semantics. This evaluation involves qualitative governance scenarios, automated validation, quantitative impact analysis, and cross-framework projection experiments.

A central design principle underlying ACM is the explicit separation between **governed configurations** and their **operational realization**. Governed configurations describe the intended, versioned, and governed structure of an agentic system independently of any specific execution framework. Runtime, conversely, represents a particular execution of that configuration under specific operational conditions. ACM focuses on governing the former while providing semantic mechanisms to relate it to the latter without conflating the two. This distinction is foundational to ACM's goal of enabling configuration governance, traceability, reproducibility, and controlled evolution across heterogeneous implementations. The core property investigated is the achievement of *cross-framework governance equivalence* after semantic projection, ensuring that configurations from different execution abstractions can be normalized and governed consistently within ACM.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development, formalization, operationalization, and evaluation of the Agentic Configuration Management (ACM) reference model.

**Design Principles**: The ACM reference model is guided by seven design principles derived from a gap analysis of existing approaches:
*   **P1 – Framework Independence**: The model must operate independently of specific orchestration frameworks or AI providers.
*   **P2 – Explicit Configuration Representation**: All configurable artifacts influencing system behavior must be explicitly represented.
*   **P3 – Immutable Versioning**: Configuration evolution relies on immutable revisions, ensuring reproducibility.
*   **P4 – Configuration–Execution Separation**: Governed configurations are conceptually distinct from runtime execution.
*   **P5 – Explicit Traceability**: Provenance and dependency relationships are preserved throughout the configuration lifecycle.
*   **P6 – Governance by Design**: Governance is an intrinsic property, supporting lifecycle control, policy enforcement, and auditability.
*   **P7 – Extensibility**: The model can accommodate new artifact categories and policies without altering its fundamental governance principles.

**ACM Reference Model**: The model provides a unified representation by abstracting configurable artifacts into **Agentic Configuration Items (ACIs)**, regardless of their technology. It categorizes artifacts into Structural items (system composition), Governance items (policies, assurance), and Runtime entities (execution observations). These are organized into four interconnected conceptual graphs:
*   **Configuration Graph (G_C)**: Represents the governed configuration and structural relationships among ACIs.
*   **Evolution Graph (G_E)**: Tracks configuration evolution, lineage, and derivation history from baselines.
*   **Assurance Graph (G_A)**: Captures governance policies, assurance evidence, and compliance constraints.
*   **Runtime Graph (G_R)**: Represents execution observations, maintaining explicit links to governed configuration items.

An ACI is the fundamental abstraction, characterized by immutable content, a unique identity and revision model, and governance metadata. ACIs specialize to represent various agentic components like agents, prompts, tools, and policies. **Release Baselines** are introduced as immutable collections of specific ACI revisions, defining coherent and reproducible system snapshots, serving as the primary unit for audit and release management.

**Governance Semantics**: The paper formalizes the behavioral aspects of ACM:
*   **Governance State Spaces**: Defines five configuration governance state spaces for ACIs—Lifecycle (L), Quality (Q), Assurance (A), Impact (I), and Eligibility (E)—and a separate Runtime (R) state space for runtime entities. Lifecycle states are explicitly assigned, while others are evaluated or derived.
*   **Propagation Policies (Π)**: These policies (Blocking, Warning, Informational, None) govern how impact propagates across configuration relationships in G_C. They are defined per relationship type and affect how an impact state is transferred.
*   **Governance Evaluation Functions**:
    *   `f_quality`: Assigns a quality state to an ACI revision based on local criteria.
    *   `f_assurance`: Determines the completeness of governance evidence for an ACI.
    *   `f_elig`: Evaluates operational eligibility from the combined lifecycle, quality, assurance, and *stabilized* impact states, after propagation.
*   **Propagation Semantics**: A global operator (`[Prop_G_C,Π`) iteratively updates impact valuations (ι) over G_C until a stable *least fixed point* (ι*) is reached. This process is formally characterized as monotonic, convergent, and terminating over a finite lattice, ensuring deterministic behavior.
*   **Runtime Semantics**: The Runtime Graph (G_R) is built from an ordered sequence of normalized runtime events. A deterministic `Replay` function reconstructs runtime states from a released baseline. Runtime observations never modify immutable configuration revisions, preserving configuration integrity and provenance.

**Operationalization**: The theoretical model is implemented and evaluated:
*   **Reference Architecture**: A layered architecture separates framework-specific extraction from framework-independent governance. Framework-specific adapters project native configurations into the canonical ACM `G_C`. A central governance kernel then applies the normative ACM semantics.
*   **Projection Pipeline**: This pipeline involves native structure extraction, semantic classification, canonical normalization (assigning stable identities, computing digests), and projection validation.
*   **Framework Adapters**: Adapters were developed for LangGraph, CrewAI, and the OpenAI Agents SDK. These frameworks represent distinct introspection regimes (explicit graph, hybrid reconstruction, delegation-based), providing varied test cases for framework independence.
*   **Governance Algorithms**: Four algorithms are central: Semantic Projection (transforms native to ACM), Configuration Validation (verifies integrity), Governance Evaluation (combines local state evaluation and impact propagation using a worklist algorithm), and Runtime Integration (processes normalized runtime events).
*   **Reference Implementation**: A Python prototype using Pydantic v2 implements the ACM concepts. It includes modules for core models, the governance engine, runtime layer, framework adapters, a scenario harness, and a verification suite.

**Experimental Design**: The evaluation consists of two complementary campaigns:
*   **Campaign A (Reference Model Coverage and Semantic Conformance)**: Twenty-seven qualitative governance scenarios were executed on all three framework adapters. These scenarios tested ACI revisions, lifecycle, governance states, dependencies, baselines, runtime governance, and dynamic agents. Outcomes were compared against pre-specified semantic properties, addressing RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3.
*   **Campaign B (Quantitative Impact Evaluation)**: Nine quantitative cases (three frameworks × three change classes: local, intermediate, global) were executed five times each. Metrics included impact size, depth, ratio, fixed-point convergence, and inspection-scope reduction. This campaign addressed RQ4 and complemented the qualitative findings.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation campaigns provided evidence regarding the representational coverage, semantic conformance, framework independence, and quantitative behavior of the ACM reference model and its operationalization.

**RQ1 – Reference Model Coverage**:
*   The qualitative evaluation, encompassing 27 governance scenarios across the three diverse agentic frameworks (LangGraph, CrewAI, OpenAI Agents SDK), indicated that the ACM reference model possessed the necessary abstractions to represent the governance-relevant configuration concepts exercised by these scenarios.
*   Differences in the native introspectability of the frameworks were observed and characterized as properties of the projection process itself rather than limitations of ACM's model expressiveness. All core governance concepts, such as immutable revisions, lifecycle stages, and dependency relationships, were representable within the ACM model.

**RQ2 – Governance Semantics**:
*   The operationalization of ACM consistently conformed to the formally specified governance semantics across all evaluated scenarios. This included the correct implementation of lifecycle management, quality and assurance evaluation, deterministic impact propagation, eligibility computation, release baseline management, and runtime reconstruction.
*   The formal characterization of impact propagation (monotonicity, convergence, termination, and uniqueness of the least fixed point) was realized by the reference implementation, ensuring predictable and stable governance outcomes.

**RQ3 – Framework Independence**:
*   The evaluation demonstrated that heterogeneous native configurations from LangGraph, CrewAI, and the OpenAI Agents SDK could be projected into governance-equivalent ACM representations. This was achieved without requiring framework-specific governance semantics within the ACM core.
*   Semantic projection coverage metrics showed 100% node, relationship, and branch coverage for the governance-relevant perimeter in all evaluated frameworks. High preservation of normative properties was achieved, with only a few properties categorized as approximated (e.g., opaque conditional-branch semantics) or unsupported (e.g., CrewAI Flow state schema, not crucial for governance equivalence).
*   Despite varying native introspection regimes (explicit graph traversal for LangGraph, hybrid reconstruction for CrewAI, and delegation introspection for OpenAI Agents SDK), the projection successfully normalized these into a common ACM configuration model, allowing the same framework-independent governance kernel to operate consistently.

**RQ4 – Quantitative Impact Analysis**:
*   The quantitative campaign, involving nine impact cases repeated five times across the three frameworks, demonstrated reproducible impact analyses. The governance kernel consistently produced identical impacted sets, governance states, and quantitative metrics (impact size, depth, ratio) across repeated executions.
*   Deterministic fixed-point convergence for impact propagation was observed, consistently achieved within three iterations for the evaluated configurations, empirically supporting the theoretical complexity bound.
*   A comparative study on a transitive dependency configuration showed that ACM's fixed-point propagation successfully identified all nine genuinely affected items, including three that were only reachable transitively. In contrast, a naive one-hop dependency inspection missed these three transitively affected items, identifying only six.
*   The analysis indicated a reduction in the residual configuration inspection scope, ranging from 61.5% for global changes to 84.6% for local changes, under the experimental protocol. This quantified the reduction in configuration elements requiring human verification after ACM identified the governed impact set.

**Overall Reproducibility**:
*   The entire evaluation process emphasized reproducibility. Consistent governed ACM representations and identical validation outcomes were produced independently of the originating execution environment. Governance-level reproducibility was maintained, decoupled from the stochastic outputs of underlying language models and framework-specific execution mechanisms, as ACM governs configurations and normalized execution semantics.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The Agentic Configuration Management (ACM) model contributes to the engineering and governance of agentic systems by introducing a framework-independent layer for configuration management. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

**Addressing Fragmentation and Enabling Interoperability**: Currently, configuration information for agentic systems is fragmented across diverse, framework-specific abstractions. ACM provides a common semantic model and representation (ACIs, graphs, baselines) that allows heterogeneous agentic configurations to be described and governed consistently. This fosters interoperability across different orchestration frameworks and enables governance policies to be defined over a stable, universal representation, rather than being tied to specific technologies. The evaluation demonstrated that ACM can normalize configurations from frameworks with distinct introspection regimes (explicit graph, hybrid, delegation-based), suggesting its potential for broad applicability in a diversifying ecosystem.

**Enhancing Reproducibility and Auditability**: By adopting principles from Software Configuration Management, such as immutable revisions and explicit baselines, ACM ensures that any governed execution can be traced back to an exact, reproducible configuration. This capability is critical for scientific replication, regression analysis, and long-term maintenance of complex AI systems. The explicit linking of governance decisions to identified configuration revisions and supporting evidence also improves auditability, making the provenance of validation results and lifecycle transitions transparent for compliance reporting and forensic investigations.

**Improving Change Management and Impact Analysis**: ACM's explicit representation of dependencies and formal propagation semantics enables systematic change management. Before modifications are promoted to a released baseline, dependency-aware impact analysis can provide early visibility into the potential consequences of changes across the system. The quantitative evaluation demonstrated that ACM's transitive impact propagation is more comprehensive than naive one-hop inspection, identifying all affected components and offering a quantifiable reduction in the residual configuration inspection scope. This can reduce the likelihood of unintended regressions and streamline verification processes.

**Complementing Existing Operational Platforms**: ACM is designed as a governance layer that complements, rather than replaces, existing LLMOps and AgentOps platforms. While operational platforms focus on execution monitoring, tracing, evaluation, and runtime metrics, ACM addresses the complementary concern of governing the underlying configuration artifacts. This separation allows operational observability to be enriched with higher-level governance information (lifecycle status, assurance levels, dependency analysis), providing a more complete picture of system behavior and its governance context.

**Foundational for Future AI Governance Standards**: By proposing a common governance vocabulary, formally defined semantics, and a demonstrated operationalization, ACM provides a structured foundation for future standardization efforts in agentic AI. It encourages convergence towards shared governance models, facilitating the exchange of governed configuration items and the development of interoperable toolchains. This can support the evolution of trustworthy agentic systems by decoupling governance semantics from rapidly changing execution technologies.

**Limitations and Future Scope**: The current ACM model intentionally separates governance concerns from execution logic, focusing on configuration management rather than prescribing how agents reason, plan, or learn. While this maintains framework independence and stability, it defines the current scope. Future work includes extending the model to accommodate new abstractions (e.g., composite capabilities, self-learning agents), broader empirical validation across more diverse architectures and industrial scales, and formal verification of governance semantics through machine-assisted proofs. These extensions aim to strengthen ACM's theoretical foundations and practical applicability within the evolving agentic ecosystem.
