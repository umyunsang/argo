## Research Report: Quantifying the Statistical Effect of Rubric Modifications on Human-Autorater Agreement

This report details the findings of the research paper "Quantifying the Statistical Effect of Rubric Modifications on Human-Autorater Agreement" by Huynh et al. The study investigates how various modifications to evaluation rubrics impact the agreement between human ratings and those generated by large language models (LLMs), referred to as autoraters.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of large language models (LLMs) as "autoraters" or "LLM-as-judges" for automated content evaluation and moderation represents a development in computational linguistics and artificial intelligence. These systems offer advantages in scalability, cost-effectiveness, and speed compared to traditional human annotation. However, a critical aspect of their deployment is validating their performance against human judgment, typically through agreement metrics.

Existing literature acknowledges that both human and automated raters are sensitive to the presentation of instructions and scoring guidelines, commonly known as rubrics. In educational contexts, rubrics are categorized as either "holistic," requiring a single overall judgment based on multiple criteria, or "analytic," which decompose assessment into separate criteria. Prior research has demonstrated that instruction specificity and examples can influence human rater accuracy, particularly for non-experts. Similarly, autoraters exhibit sensitivities to various prompt variations, including positional bias, verbosity bias, formatting choices, and example ordering. While previous work has explored prompt modifications to improve LLM performance (e.g., through decomposition or few-shot examples), a systematic statistical analysis of how specific rubric modifications impact *human-autorater agreement* across different subjective evaluation tasks has been less explored.

This study extends the existing body of knowledge by rigorously examining the statistical effect of diverse rubric modifications—such as decomposition level, example selection, context provision, and aggregation methods—on the agreement between human and autorater judgments. It specifically focuses on subjective evaluation tasks where a single "correct" answer may not exist and where human raters themselves can exhibit disagreement, differentiating it from prior work that often focuses on classification or multiple-choice tasks. The research aims to provide a quantitative understanding of these relationships, which is relevant for practitioners seeking to deploy reliable autoraters.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The increasing reliance on autoraters for evaluation and content moderation necessitates a robust understanding of their performance characteristics, particularly their alignment with human judgment. The central motivation for this work stems from the observation that while autoraters offer significant operational benefits, there is a limited statistical understanding of how alterations to the rubrics presented to both human and automated raters impact their agreement.

The research identifies two primary challenges with rubric design:
1.  **Inconsistent Interpretation of Holistic Rubrics:** Rubrics that demand an overall or "holistic" judgment (e.g., rating the "quality" of an essay) can be interpreted inconsistently by raters due to the inherent complexity or subjectivity of the criteria.
2.  **Disagreement from Analytic Rubrics:** While analytic rubrics, which break down criteria into distinct components (e.g., "quality" into "fluency" and "organization"), can potentially improve the individual accuracy of both human and automated scores, this decomposition might inadvertently lead to discrepancies between these two types of scores or with the associated holistic judgment.

Therefore, designing and deploying reliable autoraters requires moving beyond merely observing human-autorater annotation relationships to understanding *how these relationships change* when holistic versus analytic judgments are elicited, and when rubrics undergo specific modifications.

Against this background, the study articulates the following key objectives and hypotheses:

**Overarching Objective:** To statistically analyze how rubric presentation and broader rubric modifications affect human-autorater agreement on subjective evaluation tasks.

**Specific Empirical Examination:** To empirically investigate whether decomposing general holistic judgments into more granular sub-criteria, originally designed for human evaluators, can be an effective approach for improving human-judge agreement.

**Formal Hypotheses:**

1.  **Edited prompts will improve autorater alignment with human ratings over the original prompts.** This hypothesis is driven by the premise that human and autoraters may process information differently, thus requiring distinct prompt optimizations to maximize agreement.
2.  **Analytic rubrics will improve autorater alignment with human ratings over holistic rubrics.** This hypothesis is based on the expectation that decomposing complex holistic constructs into discrete components within analytic rubrics will simplify the evaluation task for autoraters, leading to better alignment with human judgments.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a comparative experimental design across two distinct evaluation domains: Automatic Essay Scoring (AES) and Instruction-Following (IF). The core methodology involves comparing human and autorater performance under various rubric conditions and analyzing their agreement using a rank-based correlation metric.

**4.1 Experimental Setup**
The research defines four primary scores for comparison:
*   Human ratings on holistic prompts ($H_H$)
*   Human ratings on analytic prompts ($H_A$)
*   Autorater ratings on holistic prompts ($LLM_H$)
*   Autorater ratings on analytic prompts ($LLM_A$)

These scores facilitate two main types of comparisons, as depicted in Figure 1 of the paper:
*   **∆Rater:** Assesses human-autorater agreement when using the *same type of rubric* (e.g., $H_H$ vs. $LLM_H$, or $H_A$ vs. $LLM_A$).
*   **∆Rubric:** Investigates the impact of varying the *type of rubric* while keeping the rater type constant (e.g., $H_H$ vs. $H_A$, or $LLM_H$ vs. $LLM_A$). An additional comparison, ∆Rater+Rubric, is explored in the appendix for completeness.

**4.2 Rubric Modifications**
The study introduces several conditions for rubric presentation, comparing "original" human rubrics with "edited" rubrics designed to enhance human-autorater agreement. Modifications include:
*   **Contextual Information:** Adding supplementary details to the rubric.
*   **Examples:**
    *   For AES holistic rubrics, the number of examples was reduced from 10-18 (full) to a representative set of three (3ex: high, medium, low scores) to manage prompt length and create a direct comparison with analytic rubrics.
    *   For AES analytic rubrics, which originally lacked examples, three representative examples were added (3ex).
    *   For IF rubrics, where neither holistic nor analytic rubrics provided examples, three input-output-instruction examples were selected, representing high, medium, and low instruction-following ratios.
*   **Positional Bias Reduction:** For analytic rubrics, evaluation was performed using:
    *   **Batch:** All sub-criteria presented in a single API call.
    *   **Separate:** Each sub-criterion evaluated in separate API calls to mitigate potential positional bias.
*   **Edited (Combined Enhancements):** A condition combining additional context, the "3ex" set, and the "separate" API call strategy for analytic prompts.

**4.3 Datasets**
*   **Automatic Essay Scoring (AES):**
    *   **ASAP (Automated Student Assessment Prize):** Contains over 21,000 essays with holistic rubrics from trained human raters. The study selected essays from prompts 1, 4, and 6, representing diverse prompt types (argumentative, source-dependent) and rubric types (general, task-specific).
    *   **ASAP++:** Provides corresponding analytic rubrics for the ASAP dataset. Acknowledged inherent discrepancies between ASAP and ASAP++ sub-criteria (e.g., "audience awareness" vs. "conventions").
*   **Instruction-Following (IF):**
    *   **InfoBench:** Comprises easy and hard instructions, along with outputs from five LLMs. Outputs were scored holistically (1-5) and analytically (binary yes/no) by three expert annotators.

**4.4 Autoraters**
Experiments were conducted using two prominent LLMs:
*   **GPT-4o-2024-11-20**
*   **Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct**

**4.5 Autorater Rating Calculation**
Autorater ratings were calculated using a probability weighting scheme, where the final rating ($r$) is derived from the exponential of log probabilities ($p_i$) for each possible score ($s_i$).

**4.6 Agreement Calculation**
*   **Kendall’s τ:** Used to measure agreement between raters, as it is robust to shifted or compressed score distributions often observed with autoraters compared to humans. It assesses agreement based on pairwise ordinal comparisons.
*   **Statistical Significance:** Confidence intervals were calculated using bootstrapping (1000 samples). For multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied. Conditions were deemed significantly different if the confidence interval for their difference did not include zero.

**4.7 Aggregation Methods for Analytic Scores**
When comparing analytic rubric scores to holistic scores (or aggregated analytic scores), domain-specific aggregation methods were employed:
*   **AES:** **Pareto dominance** was used. Essay A was considered superior to essay B if all sub-criteria scores of A were at least tied with B, and at least one sub-criterion score from A was higher. This conservative method accounts for the imperfect overlap of sub-criteria between ASAP and ASAP++ datasets and avoids arbitrary weighting.
*   **IF:** The **ratio of correctly followed instructions** was used. Response A was deemed better than response B if A had a higher ratio of correctly followed instructions.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study's findings are categorized based on the rubric modifications explored, the level of decomposition, and the influence of human inter-rater agreement.

**5.1 Edited Rubrics**

*   **Impact of Examples and Context:**
    *   For **AES analytic rubrics**, adding representative examples and contextual information (the "edited" condition) generally led to statistically significant improvements in human-autorater agreement (s,b↑ in Table 1 for GPT-4o). These improvements were substantial, for instance, from 0.439 to 0.554 for prompt 1 word choice. This suggests that even without detailed explanations of examples, such grounding aids autorater scoring.
    *   These edited analytic rubrics also significantly improved the *autoraters' self-agreement* with their own scores on the original holistic rubrics for AES (s,b↑ in Table 1 under ∆rubric), often surpassing human-human agreement levels for the two rubric types. This suggests autoraters can converge on a consistent understanding of "essay quality" across superficially different rubrics.
    *   For **IF rubrics**, however, adding examples of human analytic scores did not consistently increase autorater self-alignment. GPT-4o's alignment decreased or showed no change, while Llama's increased.
    *   For **AES holistic rubrics**, reducing the number of examples from a full set to three (3ex) decreased agreement (τ) for general rubrics (prompts 1 and 4) but increased it for the task-specific rubric (prompt 6). This hints at the importance of example explanations present in the full set for general rubrics and the specific nature of the task.
    *   For **IF holistic rubrics**, adding examples showed a non-significant increase in τ for both autoraters.

*   **Reduction of Confirmation Bias:**
    *   Across most analytic rubric comparisons for both AES and IF, the "separate" API call strategy (which aims to reduce confirmation bias by evaluating sub-criteria independently) significantly outperformed the "batched" strategy (single API call). This indicates that the separation of individual criteria is often more impactful than the addition of examples, particularly for Llama on AES and both models on IF.
    *   Qualitative analysis for IF revealed that batched ratings were skewed towards "yes" responses, contrasting with human distributions, suggesting an underlying bias effect.
    *   A trend of τ decreasing and then increasing based on criteria order was observed in AES prompts 4 and 6, suggesting potential positional biases in autoraters or difficulties with certain criteria like "word choice" or "language."

**5.2 Decomposition Level**

*   **Inconsistent Performance of Analytic Rubrics:** The hypothesis that analytic rubrics would consistently outperform holistic rubrics in aligning autoraters with human judgments was not uniformly supported.
*   **Prompt Complexity:** For AES, analytic batch prompts correlated higher with humans for the highly complex holistic prompt 1. Conversely, for less complex holistic prompts (4 and 6), the full holistic prompt often outperformed most analytic batch prompts. This suggests that while decomposition can aid complex tasks, it might introduce undue complexity or cognitive load for simpler holistic constructs, leading to lower agreement.
*   **Aggregation Methods Influence:** In IF, both GPT-4o and Llama performed worse with analytic prompts than with holistic prompts. The conservative aggregation methods—Pareto dominance for AES and instruction-following ratio for IF—were identified as contributing factors. Pareto dominance is highly sensitive to any single sub-criterion disagreement, and the instruction ratio in IF may not accurately reflect the weighted importance of sub-criteria in a holistic judgment. Thus, the aggregation method itself can influence observed agreement.

**5.3 Agreement Level**

*   **Impact of Human Inter-rater Agreement:** The study demonstrated a consistent positive correlation between higher human inter-rater agreement and higher human-autorater agreement.
    *   For **IF holistic rubrics**, human-autorater agreement was significantly higher when all three human annotators agreed, compared to partial or full disagreement. This pattern also held for IF analytic rubrics (separate and edited conditions).
    *   For **AES holistic rubrics**, a significant increase was observed for prompt 4, and a non-significant increase for prompt 6 when human raters agreed. Prompt 1, which used an average of rater scores, showed less sensitivity, possibly due to the averaging method diluting the impact of individual disagreements.
    *   This finding suggests that a foundational level of human-human agreement is crucial for achieving meaningful human-autorater alignment.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides empirical evidence and statistical quantification regarding the efficacy of rubric modifications in aligning autorater judgments with human evaluations. The findings contribute to the development and deployment of LLM-as-judges by offering actionable insights for practitioners.

The study's significance and potential impact include:

*   **Informed Rubric Design:** The results underscore that effective rubric design for autoraters is not a one-size-fits-all endeavor. Practitioners should consider the specific domain, the characteristics of the autorater being used, and the nature of the evaluation task when crafting or modifying rubrics. Providing representative examples and additional context generally improves human-autorater agreement, as does reducing potential confirmation bias through segmented evaluations.
*   **Understanding Decomposition:** The finding that analytic rubrics do not consistently outperform holistic ones, and can even decrease agreement depending on task complexity and aggregation methods, challenges a common assumption. This highlights the need for a nuanced approach to decomposition, considering the cognitive demands placed on the rater (human or automated) and how individual criteria scores are ultimately combined. Arbitrary aggregation methods may obscure true alignment.
*   **Importance of Human Annotation Quality:** The strong correlation between high human inter-rater agreement and subsequent human-autorater agreement emphasizes the critical role of high-quality human annotation data. For autoraters to be reliably evaluated and trained, the "ground truth" established by human raters must itself be consistent and well-defined. This implies that efforts to improve human annotation guidelines and achieve high inter-rater reliability should precede or accompany the deployment of autoraters.
*   **Guidance for Practitioners:** The detailed analysis of how factors like rubric complexity, example quantity, and positional bias influence agreement offers concrete guidance for practitioners. For instance, prioritizing bias reduction in analytic rubrics (e.g., using separate API calls) and carefully selecting the number and type of examples are specific recommendations.
*   **Foundation for Future Research:** By highlighting domain and autorater dependency, the study lays a groundwork for future investigations into a wider array of evaluation domains (e.g., summarization, dialogue quality), different autorater architectures, and diverse rubric types. It also implicitly points to the need for research into cross-lingual rubric sensitivity, a limitation acknowledged by the authors.

In summary, this work moves beyond qualitative observations to statistically quantify the effects of rubric modifications, offering valuable insights for the responsible and effective integration of autoraters into evaluation workflows.
