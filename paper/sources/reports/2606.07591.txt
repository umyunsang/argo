**ResearchClawBench: A Benchmark for End-to-End Autonomous Scientific Research**

This report provides a detailed analysis of the research paper "ResearchClawBench: A Benchmark for End-to-End Autonomous Scientific Research," authored by researchers primarily affiliated with the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, along with contributors from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Fudan University, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, and other academic institutions.

---

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The primary affiliation for this research is the **Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory**.

Core authors include Wanghan Xu, Shuo Li, Tianlin Ye, Qinglong Cao, Yixin Chen, Hengjian Gao, Yiheng Wang, Qi Li, and Kun Li. Corresponding authors are Lei Bai and Wenlong Zhang.

Additional contributors are affiliated with various universities, including Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Fudan University, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Tongji University, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Xi’an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, Sichuan University, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Beijing Normal University, Tsinghua University, Southeast University, Zhejiang University, University of Oxford, and Nanjing University.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of automated scientific research tools is identified as a significant area within artificial intelligence. Existing AI coding agents are marketed as capable of autonomous research; however, a standardized and comprehensive method for evaluating their end-to-end capabilities has been lacking. This deficiency has made it challenging to objectively assess claims of autonomous research or to compare progress among different systems.

Current benchmarks for AI's scientific capabilities address aspects that are adjacent but incomplete for assessing full autonomous research:
*   **Scientific Question Answering and Reasoning:** Benchmarks such as SciQ, GPQA, MMLU, Humanity’s Last Exam, SciBench, and ATLAS focus on scientific knowledge, factual understanding, and static reasoning. They evaluate local scientific tasks like answering questions or interpreting figures, but do not encompass the entire research loop from raw data to complete reports, or the iterative process required for scientific discovery.
*   **Interactive Scientific Environments:** Platforms like ScienceWorld and DiscoveryWorld involve agents interacting in simulated scientific worlds. While dynamic, these environments often abstract real-world tasks, potentially limiting the direct applicability of findings to real scientific workflows.
*   **Research-Agent Benchmarks and Paper Reproduction:** Efforts such as SciCode, Sci-DataCopilot, MLAgentBench, MLE-bench, MLGym, PaperBench, CORE-Bench, and ReproduceBench evaluate specific sub-skills like scientific coding, data analysis, or the reproduction of published papers. These often concentrate on machine learning contexts or focus on reproducing results from already exposed papers and code, rather than open-ended discovery from initial problem formulation.
*   **Scientific Discovery-Level Benchmarks:** Benchmarks like ScienceAgentBench, SGI-Bench, AIRS-Bench, and MLR-Bench aim for scientist-aligned workflows or the full research lifecycle. However, their primary scenarios frequently emphasize workflow-capability measurement or AI/ML research, often leaving a gap in covering broader natural science tasks, diverse data modalities, and varied evidence standards.

System-level initiatives such as The AI Scientist, AI Co-Scientist, AI-Researcher, and InternAgent-1.5 further highlight the need for a system-agnostic benchmark capable of comparing diverse autonomous research systems. ResearchClawBench is presented as a contribution to address these gaps by providing a benchmark grounded in real scientific papers, requiring re-discovery from hidden target contexts, and directly evaluating end-to-end autonomous scientific discovery across a wide array of scientific domains and data types.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this work is to introduce ResearchClawBench, a benchmark designed to evaluate the end-to-end autonomous scientific research capabilities of AI systems. The primary motivation stems from the observed increase in the use of AI coding agents for scientific tasks and the corresponding absence of a principled methodology to verify their claims of autonomous research, particularly for generating open-ended scientific outputs.

The authors identify several non-trivial challenges inherent in designing such a benchmark:
1.  **Scientific Meaningfulness:** Tasks must be aligned with real-world research scenarios and possess genuine scientific value.
2.  **Evaluation of Open-ended Outputs:** Research outputs are inherently open-ended and cannot be assessed by simple exact-match criteria or unit tests. Relying solely on LLM-as-judge evaluation also risks introducing bias.
3.  **Heterogeneity of Scientific Research:** Scientific research spans diverse data modalities, analytical methods, and evidence standards, requiring a benchmark with broad coverage to prevent systems from overfitting to narrow skill sets.

ResearchClawBench aims to address these challenges through specific design choices:
*   **Task Significance:** Tasks are derived from real, published scientific papers. Domain experts select target papers based on clear scientific questions, accessibility of raw data, and practical research value. These papers are then converted into executable task descriptions.
*   **Open-ended Output Evaluation:** To enable robust evaluation of open-ended scientific outputs, the target paper is concealed during the assessment. Expert-curated rubrics are constructed around the hidden target paper, decomposing expected outputs into verifiable and weighted sub-criteria. This approach aims to allow for target-paper-level re-discovery while also accommodating novel discoveries.
*   **Task Diversity:** The benchmark encompasses 40 tasks across 10 distinct scientific domains: Astronomy, Chemistry, Earth Science, Energy Science, Information Science, Life Science, Material Science, Mathematics, Neuroscience, and Physics. Tasks include both diagnostic analysis and metric optimization, aiming to cover a broad spectrum of scientific problems.

In summary, the motivation is to establish a reproducible evaluation frontier that can reliably measure progress towards autonomous scientific research, quantify the current capabilities of AI systems, and guide future development in this field.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

ResearchClawBench is structured around three core design principles: tasks grounded in scientific work with provided references and raw data, tasks possessing genuine research value, and evaluation rubrics built around hidden target papers to assess open-ended outputs.

**Task Components:**
Each task ($\tau$) in ResearchClawBench is defined by five elements:
*   $q$: A detailed task description.
*   $L$: Related literature relevant to the task.
*   $D$: Raw data required for the research.
*   $p^\star$: A hidden target paper, which serves as a reference for evaluation.
*   $A$: Expert-constructed evaluation artifacts, primarily rubrics.
The objective for the evaluated AI system is to generate an output ($y$) comprising:
*   $\pi$: The experimental code and execution process.
*   $o$: Intermediate results, figures, and output files.
*   $r$: The final research report.

**Data Construction Process (Figure 3):**
The construction of tasks involves a multi-stage process conducted by domain experts:
1.  **Paper Selection:** Experts identify high-quality published papers characterized by clear scientific questions, accessible data, and high research value (scientific, economic, ecological, medical, etc.).
2.  **Task Formulation:** The core scientific question from the selected paper is extracted and rewritten into an executable task description.
3.  **Resource Curation:** Relevant literature and raw data are organized and provided to the AI system.
4.  **Rubric Development:** Crucially, expert-curated rubrics are constructed based on the key scientific artifacts and findings of the hidden target paper. These rubrics define verifiable and weighted sub-criteria for evaluating the generated outputs.
5.  **Task Packaging and Validation:** The materials are packaged into standardized tasks. A cross-expert validation process is performed to ensure task quality, identify and fix issues, and filter out unsuitable samples. The benchmark currently comprises 40 tasks across 10 scientific domains.

**ResearchHarness for LLM Baselines (Figure 2, Table 4):**
To enable the evaluation of native Large Language Models (LLMs) that may not possess a full agentic scaffold, ResearchClawBench introduces ResearchHarness. This lightweight, tool-using harness facilitates LLM interaction within the research environment. It operates on a ReAct-style loop and leverages OpenAI-compatible APIs for native tool calling. ResearchHarness provides three categories of tools:
*   **Web and Retrieval Tools:** For searching the web (Serper API), scholarly sources, and fetching webpage content (Jina Reader).
*   **Local File Tools:** For workspace operations such as discovering files, reading text, inspecting images, and extracting PDF content (MinerU).
*   **Local Execution Tools:** For running one-shot shell commands and managing persistent terminal sessions for longer analytical workflows.
ResearchHarness also incorporates an automatic context compaction mechanism, summarizing interaction history when message history approaches the input token limit to manage context length.

**Evaluation Metric: Reference-Anchored Discovery Score (RADS) (Figure 4):**
RADS is designed to evaluate open-ended scientific discovery by treating each target paper as a human reference study, rather than a fixed ground truth. This approach allows for assessment of re-discovery while also accommodating novel findings.
*   A score of **50** indicates that the system's output provides scientific evidence comparable to the reference paper (target-paper-level re-discovery).
*   Scores **below 50** indicate deficiencies such as incorrect analysis, superficial experiments, missing key evidence, incomplete reporting, or failure to identify the core scientific object.
*   Scores **above 50** suggest new-discovery potential, indicating evidence that surpasses the reference study (e.g., stronger quantitative results, more comprehensive explanations, clearer mechanisms, or novel insights).
RADS aims to quantify an agent's capacity to generate credible scientific evidence, acknowledging that actual scientific discovery requires further validation beyond a single benchmark score. The metric is implemented through expert-constructed rubrics, which specify concrete criteria, technical keywords, and weights for both textual and multimodal (image) scientific artifacts.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation involved:
*   Seven autonomous research agents: Claude Code, EvoScientist, Codex CLI, OpenClaw, ResearchClaw, ARIS Codex, and Nanobot.
*   Seventeen native LLM baselines via ResearchHarness: including various versions of Claude-Opus, DeepSeek, GLM, GPT, Gemini, Grok, Kimi, MiMo, and Qwen.
All systems were assessed across the 40 ResearchClawBench tasks. The final reports were scored against the rubrics by GPT-5.1.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation on ResearchClawBench yielded several findings regarding the current state of autonomous scientific research agents and LLMs.

**Overall Performance (Table 5, Figure 1b):**
Current AI systems demonstrate limited capability in achieving reliable end-to-end scientific re-discovery.
*   The highest-performing autonomous agent, **Claude Code**, achieved an average score of 21.5. The collective "frontier mean" for autonomous agents (best score per task) was 25.8.
*   Among LLMs evaluated via ResearchHarness, **Claude-Opus-4.7** had the highest average score at 20.7. The LLM frontier mean was 26.5.
None of the evaluated systems reached the 50-point threshold, which signifies target-paper-level re-discovery, indicating a substantial gap between current capabilities and the benchmark's objectives.

**System Dominance and Task Difficulty:**
*   While Claude Code achieved the highest overall average among agents, it did not consistently dominate, winning only 12 out of 40 tasks. This suggests a distribution of strengths across different tasks and domains.
*   Task difficulty showed high consistency across different autonomous agents, with a median pairwise correlation of 0.77.
*   Similarly, LLMs exhibited varied strengths across domains; for instance, Claude-Opus-4.6 performed best in Astronomy, Earth, Energy, and Math, while other LLMs led in Chemistry, Information, Life, Material, Neuroscience, and Physics tasks respectively.

**Supplemental Quality Dimensions (Table 6):**
Beyond the core rubric scores, reports were assessed on four supplemental dimensions: Comprehensiveness, Depth, Instruction Following, and Professionalism.
*   Systems generally scored higher in **Professionalism** (often exceeding 70), indicating a capacity to produce well-formatted reports.
*   Scores for Comprehensiveness, Depth, and Instruction Following were notably lower, suggesting that models are currently weaker in the substantive quality of research content compared to presentational aspects.
*   The observed weak correlation between these supplemental dimensions and the main rubric score implies that the primary challenge for AI systems is not merely generating a polished report, but rather producing the critical scientific evidence required by the rubrics.

**Runtime and Cost Analysis (Figure 5):**
An analysis of resource consumption revealed a weak positive correlation between mean task cost/runtime and rubric score.
*   Claude Code achieved a higher score but also incurred higher costs and longer runtimes.
*   Qwen3.7-Max was identified as the "efficient knee" in terms of cost-efficiency, while OpenClaw represented the runtime-efficient knee.
This suggests that for the current tasks, increased computational investment does not consistently translate into a stable improvement in final results, implying that the tasks might lie beyond the stable capability boundary of existing models.

**Error Analysis (Figure 6):**
A detailed error analysis of 280 autonomous agent runs across 40 tasks categorized failures into six types. The predominant failure modes were:
*   **Experiment Design Mismatch:** Differences in experimental protocol, data processing, baselines, or validation compared to the target paper.
*   **Evidence Mismatch:** Discrepancies in figures, numerical results, or conclusions relative to critical evidence in the target paper.
*   **Scientific Core Missing:** Failure to identify or address the core scientific mechanism or finding.
Less frequent errors included Goal Misalignment, Reliability/Reporting Failure, and Execution Failure. This distribution indicates that agents are generally capable of generating reports and executing code, but struggle with scientific fidelity, often departing from the target paper's methodology or interpretation.

**Case Study (Physics 002 with OpenClaw, Figure 7):**
A case study on Physics 002, where OpenClaw achieved the highest score among autonomous agents (27.45), illustrated these limitations. While OpenClaw successfully recovered the direct XEB trend of fidelity decreasing with depth, it failed to reproduce several rubric-critical components of the target paper's fidelity estimation evidence chain, such as multiple scaling analyses, mirror-circuit inference, and a gate-counting error model. This exemplifies that agents frequently capture the most apparent trends but miss finer verification steps and detailed physical modeling crucial for target-paper-level re-discovery.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

ResearchClawBench represents a contribution to the field of AI-driven scientific research by addressing a critical evaluation gap. The benchmark’s design, which incorporates real scientific discovery tasks, end-to-end pipeline evaluation, and fine-grained expert-curated rubrics, provides a more comprehensive framework for assessing autonomous scientific capabilities than previous methods.

**Significance:**
*   **Quantification of Current Limitations:** The findings clearly indicate that contemporary autonomous agents and harnessed LLMs are substantially distant from reliably performing scientific re-discovery. The average scores, significantly below the 50-point re-discovery threshold, highlight the considerable work required to advance AI in this domain.
*   **Identification of Key Failure Modes:** The detailed error analysis, pinpointing issues in experimental design mismatch, evidence mismatch, and missing scientific core, offers specific targets for future AI development. This guidance can help researchers focus on improving AI's ability to adhere to complex scientific protocols, maintain rigorous evidence chains, and grasp underlying scientific mechanisms.
*   **Establishment of a Reproducible Evaluation Frontier:** By providing 40 tasks derived from real papers across diverse scientific domains, complete with raw data, related literature, and transparent rubrics, ResearchClawBench offers a standardized and reproducible platform. This platform can facilitate consistent measurement of progress, enable objective comparison between different AI systems, and support the iterative development of more capable autonomous research tools.
*   **Guidance for AI Development:** The benchmark’s emphasis on hidden target papers and the Reference-Anchored Discovery Score (RADS) encourages the development of AI systems that can not only reproduce known science but also generate credible evidence that could lead to genuine novel discoveries. The weak correlation between superficial report quality and scientific content score also directs future efforts toward improving substantive research capabilities rather than mere presentation.

**Potential Impact:**
*   **Accelerating AI for Science:** By setting a high bar for end-to-end autonomous scientific research and providing detailed feedback on current system limitations, ResearchClawBench has the potential to accelerate the development of more sophisticated AI systems. These advanced systems could eventually augment human researchers, automate repetitive experimental tasks, or even propose new hypotheses.
*   **Enhancing Scientific Rigor:** The benchmark promotes rigor in AI-generated scientific outputs by demanding adherence to scientific protocols, robust evidence generation, and coherent mechanistic explanations. As AI systems improve against this benchmark, they could contribute to more rigorous and reproducible scientific inquiry.
*   **Broader Application of AI in Natural Sciences:** The diverse domain coverage of ResearchClawBench encourages the development of general-purpose AI research agents applicable across various natural sciences, moving beyond specialized applications in a single field like machine learning.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
The authors also identify several limitations of the current benchmark, which delineate its scope and potential future directions:
*   **Focus on Dry-Lab Research:** The tasks primarily involve data analysis, coding, and literature review, rather than wet-lab research requiring physical experimental platforms, sample preparation, or instrument operation.
*   **Evaluation Granularity:** Current scoring primarily targets the final research report, potentially overlooking the nuances of fine-grained research steps.
*   **Novel Discovery Evaluation:** While RADS aims to allow for "discovery," evaluating truly novel scientific conclusions still requires more reliable and independent verification methods beyond rubrics constructed around existing target papers.

Despite these limitations, ResearchClawBench offers a framework for assessing progress in autonomous scientific research and driving the development of AI systems capable of contributing to the scientific discovery process.
