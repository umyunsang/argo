## Research Report: Auditing LLM-as-Judge Reliability

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

*   **Zongyou Yang:** Dyson School of Design Engineering, Imperial College London, London, United Kingdom.
*   **Yinghan Hou:** Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Imperial College London, London, United Kingdom.
*   **Xiaokun Yang:** School of Electronic Information, Nanchang Institute of Technology, Nanchang, China. (Corresponding author)

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement and widespread adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to their use not only as generative systems but also as evaluative tools, commonly referred to as "LLM-as-judge." This paradigm involves using an LLM to assess the quality of responses generated by other LLMs, often in open-ended generation tasks where traditional automatic metrics struggle. Benchmarks such as MT-Bench, Chatbot Arena, PandaLM, JudgeLM, G-Eval, Auto-J, Prometheus, and RewardBench have established the practicality of LLM judges. Previous research has demonstrated that LLM judges can correlate with human preferences, but their performance is observed to vary depending on the task, prompt design, scoring scale, and specific model family used.

Existing literature often presents evaluator performance as a one-dimensional model ordering, which obscures the underlying factors contributing to observed gains or losses in evaluation quality. These factors can include true capability improvements, differences in benchmark slices, changes in bias behavior, or variations in protocol design. Furthermore, prior work has identified various biases inherent in LLM judges, such as position bias, verbosity bias, and sensitivity to rating formats. While these studies highlight the existence of biases, they typically focus on isolated prompt perturbations rather than how bias changes in conjunction with evaluator scaling.

This paper addresses a crucial gap by treating the LLM judge itself as a subject of scaling analysis and scrutinizing its reliability as a multi-dimensional measurement instrument. It moves beyond a singular accuracy score to investigate judgment validity, bias robustness, aggregation independence, and protocol auditability. By comparing different upgrade paths—parameter scaling and sequential model releases—and analyzing the interplay of model capability, bias, and aggregation protocols, the study aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of LLM-as-judge reliability. It also proposes an auditable measurement framework, emphasizing that the reported score is a product of the entire measurement pipeline, not solely the judge model's intrinsic capability.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from what the authors term the "evaluator-replacement ambiguity." This ambiguity arises when an LLM-as-judge score for candidate responses changes simply because the evaluating LLM has been replaced or upgraded, even if the candidate responses remain constant. The source of this change is often unclear; it could signal genuine improvement in the new judge's capability, a shift in its biases, different performance on specific parts of a benchmark, or variations in how its outputs are parsed and aggregated. This lack of clarity hinders the interpretation of LLM-as-judge scores as reliable evidence of candidate system quality.

To address this ambiguity, the paper establishes three primary research questions (RQs):

*   **RQ1: Does judge reliability improve similarly along a parameter axis and a released-model upgrade path?** This question probes whether evaluator capability is the dominant factor in reliability. If so, both scaling methods (increasing parameters or new releases) should yield consistently positive gains across datasets. Conversely, if reliability is workload-dependent, gains should fluctuate across datasets and significance should not transfer uniformly. The study specifically examines Qwen3 dense judges (from 1.7B to 32B parameters) and MiniMax M2–M2.7 released API models.
*   **RQ2: Does higher aggregate accuracy also reduce known judge biases?** This question investigates the relationship between a judge's overall capability (accuracy) and its susceptibility to biases such as position bias and verbosity bias. The expectation is that more capable judges should be less bias-sensitive. However, if biases persist even in high-accuracy judges, it would indicate that increased capability does not eliminate measurement artifacts, necessitating continued bias detection and mitigation.
*   **RQ3: Do protocol-level upgrades, such as juries and debate, change reliability beyond single-judge scaling?** This question explores the effectiveness of multi-judge aggregation strategies. It posits that jury gains might be limited if individual judge errors are highly correlated, and that shifts observed in debate protocols require careful attribution, potentially depending on capability asymmetry and the auditability of the protocol's intermediate steps.

The overarching goal is to contribute an auditable measurement framework for LLM-as-judge reliability. This framework seeks to disentangle model capability from other influential factors such as workload dependence, bias behavior, correlated voting, and various measurement artifacts within the evaluation pipeline. The study's contributions include a two-axis evaluator study avoiding causal claims about MiniMax internals, a decomposition of reliability into single-judge accuracy, bias probes, correlated jury errors, and debate auditability, and a proposed reporting standard that includes benchmark slices, parse-shared tests, A/B randomization, error dependence estimates, and protocol logs.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The study defines LLM-as-judge reliability as a multi-dimensional property, encompassing judgment validity, bias robustness, aggregation independence, and protocol auditability. Each construct is operationalized through specific measures and experiments, as outlined in Table 1 of the paper.

**4.1 Models and Datasets:**
The main panel of judges comprises eight models: Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-32B (representing parameter scaling), and MiniMax-M2, MiniMax-M2.1, MiniMax-M2.5, MiniMax-M2.7 (representing released-model upgrades). Two additional models, GLM-5.1 and mimo-v2-pro, serve as cross-family reference judges in certain experiments but are not part of the primary scaling axes.

Four diverse datasets are employed to ensure findings are not task-specific:
*   **LLMBar:** An adversarial pairwise benchmark containing 419 examples.
*   **PandaLM testset-v1:** Provides broad-domain pairwise judgments, with 894 valid examples after removing tie-majority cases.
*   **Chatbot Arena:** A 2,000-example sample (seed-42) offering broad human preference comparisons, with 1,997 valid pairwise examples.
*   **Judge’s Verdict slice:** 200 TechQA-derived examples with three-level pointwise labels, enabling exact-match and rank-based evaluation.

**4.2 Metrics and Protocols:**
*   **Metrics:** Pairwise experiments report accuracy and Cohen’s κ on parseable verdicts. Judge’s Verdict additionally reports Spearman rank correlation.
*   **Decoding:** Main single-judge runs use near-greedy decoding (T=0.1) to minimize sampling noise. Homogeneous juries use T=0.7 to capture stochasticity, while heterogeneous juries use T=0.1 to isolate model composition.
*   **Statistical Analysis:** Adjacent model comparisons use exact two-sided McNemar tests on parse-shared examples. Holm correction is applied across 18 adjacent tests to control the family-wise error rate.

**4.3 Experimental Design:**
The study consists of four main experiments, each designed to probe a specific aspect of reliability:

*   **Experiment 1: Single-Judge Validity:** Measures accuracy and agreement (Cohen's κ, Spearman rank correlation) for all judges across the four datasets along the two upgrade axes (Qwen3 parameter scaling, MiniMax release path).
*   **Experiment 2: Bias Robustness:** Evaluates position bias (flip rate under A/B reversal), verbosity bias (sensitivity to padding), and granularity sensitivity using the LLMBar dataset. This assesses how robust judges are to simple perturbations that should not alter intrinsic quality.
*   **Experiment 3: Aggregation Independence (Juries):** Investigates the effectiveness of majority-vote juries. It estimates the intra-class error correlation (ρ) for homogeneous juries (multiple calls to the same judge) and compares observed jury accuracy against predictions from independent voters and ρ-corrected beta-binomial models. Heterogeneous juries (combining different judges) are also evaluated.
*   **Experiment 4: Protocol Auditability (Structured Debate):** Examines a structured debate protocol where two judges engage in up to three rebuttal rounds on LLMBar. The experiment focuses on the magnitude of decision shifts and, crucially, the auditability of these shifts, especially concerning raw outputs, parser status, and intermediate verdicts. This is treated as an auditability case study rather than a clean deliberation estimate due to limitations in logging.

**4.4 Robustness Checks:**
The study includes several robustness checks:
*   **Human-ceiling calibration:** Compares best-judge performance against human agreement levels on PandaLM and Judge's Verdict.
*   **Corrected adjacent significance:** Confirms McNemar test results after Holm correction.
*   **Limited prompt sensitivity:** Assesses the stability of cross-family ordering and robustness for stronger judges under minor prompt variations on LLMBar.
*   **Arena seed stability:** Examines the impact of different random seeds on Arena accuracy spread.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded four principal findings regarding LLM-as-judge reliability:

**5.1 Finding 1: Judge Upgrades Are Not Interchangeable**
The expectation that evaluator capability would be the dominant factor, leading to similar adjacent gains along both upgrade paths, was not consistently met.
*   **Qwen3 Parameter Scaling:** The Qwen3 series showed a substantial improvement in accuracy from 1.7B to 4B parameters on LLMBar (0.463 to 0.617) and a more modest gain on Arena. Subsequent parameter steps (4B to 14B, 14B to 32B) exhibited smaller and not uniformly monotone improvements.
*   **MiniMax Release-Generation Path:** The MiniMax M2–M2.7 release generations showed no reliable adjacent improvement in accuracy. Pairwise differences between adjacent MiniMax releases were at most 0.022 accuracy, and none of the nine MiniMax adjacent tests reached an uncorrected p < 0.05.
*   **Statistical Significance:** After applying Holm correction across all 18 adjacent tests, only the Qwen3 1.7B to 4B steps on LLMBar and Arena remained statistically significant.
*   **Overall Performance:** No single judge consistently outperformed others across all datasets; GLM-5.1 led on LLMBar, MiniMax-M2.7 on PandaLM, and mimo-v2-pro on the sampled Arena slice. This indicates that model upgrading is not a uniform reliability intervention and that validity is often slice-specific.

**5.2 Finding 2: Stronger Judges Are Less Biased, Not Unbiased**
Bias probes on LLMBar indicated an association between higher capability and reduced bias, but biases did not vanish.
*   **Position Bias:** The position-flip rate, measuring verdict changes upon A/B reversal, decreased significantly from 0.320 for Qwen3-1.7B to 0.117–0.147 for MiniMax releases.
*   **Verbosity Bias:** Sensitivity to an innocuous padding string, which should not affect judgment, fell from 0.547 for Qwen3-1.7B to approximately 0.13 for MiniMax models. Granularity sensitivity also decreased for stronger judges.
*   **Capability-Fairness Association:** A strong negative Pearson correlation (r = -0.957) was observed between LLMBar accuracy and position-flip rate across the eight evaluated judges. This association, measured on one dataset and panel, suggests that increased capability co-varies with reduced bias.
*   **Persistent Bias:** Despite the reduction, biases remained. MiniMax-M2.7, a strong judge, still altered 14.7% of its verdicts under A/B reversal. This implies that even high-accuracy judges necessitate bias probes and randomization.

**5.3 Finding 3: Jury Size Matters Less Than Error Dependence**
The effectiveness of majority-vote juries was found to be limited by correlated errors among judges.
*   **Error Correlation (ρ):** The study estimated high intra-class error correlation for homogeneous juries. Qwen3 juries had ρ values ranging from 0.944 to 0.972 on LLMBar, while MiniMax juries were lower but still significantly correlated (ρ = 0.664–0.706).
*   **Jury Gains:** Due to high correlation, increasing jury size yielded only marginal accuracy gains. For Qwen3-1.7B on LLMBar, accuracy moved from 0.463 (K=1) to 0.475 (K=3) and 0.482 (K=5).
*   **Prediction Accuracy:** The independence prediction model (assuming ρ=0) had a median absolute error of 0.078 on LLMBar and 0.093 on PandaLM. The ρ-corrected beta-binomial prediction significantly reduced these errors to 0.008 and 0.004, with maximum error below 0.02, indicating its necessity.
*   **Heterogeneous Juries:** Mixing different model families in heterogeneous juries did not fully restore independent errors under a shared prompt. This shifts the design question from jury size to the independence of errors.

**5.4 Finding 4: Debate Shifts Require Audit Trails**
Structured debate protocols produced substantial decision shifts, but their interpretability was hindered by a lack of auditability.
*   **Accuracy Shifts:** Cross-capability debate pairs (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B with GLM-5.1, MiniMax-M2.7, or mimo-v2-pro) showed the largest shifts in final accuracy compared to round-1, with increases of +0.317, +0.305, and +0.289, respectively. Qwen3-4B paired with GLM-5.1 shifted by +0.243, reaching 0.897. Same-family and top-vs-top shifts were much smaller (+0.005 to +0.050).
*   **Auditability Issues:** The debate implementation lacked comprehensive logging. It recorded round and final verdicts but not raw responses or parse-success flags. Round-1 parse failures defaulted to option A, and subsequent failures retained the previous verdict. Without the ability to audit fallback rates, these shifts cannot be definitively attributed to deliberation alone, underscoring the need for transparent protocol logs.

**5.5 Robustness Checks:**
*   **Human-Ceiling Calibration:** PandaLM showed room for improvement (best judge κ=0.753 vs. human ceiling 0.920), while Judge’s Verdict was closer to its noisy ceiling (best judge κ=0.620 vs. human κ=0.562).
*   **Prompt Sensitivity:** Cross-family ordering on LLMBar was preserved, and stronger judges exhibited greater cross-prompt stability.
*   **Arena Seed Perturbations:** Produced minor accuracy spreads (0.004–0.032), smaller than the robust Qwen3 1.7B to 4B gap. These checks supported the main findings without establishing broad cross-family generality.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research repositions LLM-as-judge reliability from a singular model-selection problem to a multi-faceted measurement-validity challenge. The findings have notable implications for the practice and reporting standards of automatic evaluation using LLMs.

**Key Implications:**

*   **Nuanced Understanding of Judge Upgrades:** The finding that judge upgrades are not uniformly interchangeable challenges the assumption that newer or larger models automatically constitute better evaluators. The robust gains observed in the low-end parameter scaling (Qwen3 1.7B to 4B) contrast sharply with the non-significant adjacent improvements along the MiniMax release path. This suggests that practitioners should not assume consistent reliability gains across all model upgrades and should instead evaluate new judges for specific contexts and workloads. This shifts the focus from a one-dimensional evaluator ordering to slice-specific measurement validity.
*   **Persistent Bias in Strong Judges:** The demonstration that even high-accuracy judges exhibit measurable biases (e.g., MiniMax-M2.7 still changing 14.7% of verdicts under A/B reversal) underscores the necessity of continuous bias detection and mitigation. This impacts the design of evaluation protocols, requiring randomization of candidate response order (A/B randomization) and dedicated bias probes, even when employing state-of-the-art LLMs as judges.
*   **Limitations of Jury Aggregation:** The revelation that high error correlation significantly limits the benefits of majority voting in LLM juries challenges the naive application of jury-based aggregation. This suggests that simply increasing the number of jurors (K) is insufficient; rather, the independence of their errors (ρ) must be considered and reported. This could lead to the development of more sophisticated aggregation methods that account for or actively decorrelate errors.
*   **Critical Need for Protocol Audit Trails:** The experience with structured debate protocols highlights that while multi-agent interactions can produce substantial shifts in outcomes, their interpretability and trustworthiness are contingent upon comprehensive audit trails. Without logging raw outputs, parser status, and intermediate decisions, significant shifts in scores cannot be reliably attributed to genuine deliberation or improved judgment. This mandates a new standard for reporting multi-agent evaluation protocols, ensuring transparency and reproducibility.

**Practical Recommendations and Proposed Reporting Standard:**

The paper distills a minimal audit trail for LLM-as-judge reports, aiming to enhance the interpretability and trustworthiness of evaluation results:

*   **Dataset Slice Reporting:** Report valid N, parseable N, and tie handling to prevent hidden sample mismatches.
*   **Parser Artifacts:** Document parser success rates and fallback rules to account for artificial accuracy changes.
*   **Position/Verbosity Bias:** Include A/B reversal and padding probe results to identify and mitigate ranking artifacts.
*   **Correlated Jury Errors:** Report ρ alongside K for jury experiments to provide an accurate estimate of voting gains.
*   **Debate Protocol Artifacts:** Store raw outputs, parser status, and all intermediate verdicts for multi-agent protocols to ensure auditability of protocol gains.
*   **Statistical Robustness:** Utilize paired tests, Holm correction, and uncertainty intervals to avoid false claims of adjacent-step improvements or overstated precision.

In conclusion, this research significantly contributes to the methodological rigor of LLM-as-judge evaluations. By identifying and analyzing critical failure modes and proposing concrete reporting standards, it aims to foster more reliable, transparent, and interpretable assessment of generative AI systems. The proposed framework and lessons learned are crucial for researchers and developers relying on LLM-as-judge evaluations to measure progress and make informed decisions about model quality.
