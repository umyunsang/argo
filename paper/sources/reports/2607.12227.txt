### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly designed to interact with complex environments, and their performance is often mediated by external "harnesses." A harness encompasses various components such as prompts, tools, memory, verification routines, and control logic, which dictate how an LLM agent observes tasks and performs actions. Prior research has indicated that the design of these harnesses significantly influences agent performance, even when the underlying language model remains fixed. Traditionally, harness engineering has been a manual and iterative process, involving developers inspecting agent trajectories, diagnosing failures, and manually revising the harness.

This manual effort has motivated a growing area of research into "automatic harness evolution," where computational methods are employed to automatically improve these external harnesses. Existing approaches in this domain, such as Meta-Harness, Agentic Harness Engineering (AHE), and AEVO, typically involve an iterative search loop: analyzing past trajectories and scores, proposing modifications to the harness, evaluating these modifications on benchmark tasks, and repeating the process.

However, the authors of this paper identify concerns with the prevailing evaluation protocols for automatic harness evolution. Specifically, they note that current methods often use the same public benchmark tasks for both the iterative search process of evolving the harness and for the final performance evaluation of the evolved harness. This practice, the authors argue, introduces two fundamental issues:
1.  **Conflation with Test-Time Scaling:** Harness evolution is itself an iterative search procedure that repeatedly evaluates and revises candidate harnesses. This iterative nature resembles "test-time scaling" methods, which allocate additional computation at inference time to improve outputs without modifying model weights. Examples of test-time scaling include parallel sampling (generating multiple independent answers and aggregating them) and sequential refinement (generating answers iteratively, building on prior attempts). The authors contend that without comparing harness evolution against these simpler test-time scaling baselines under matched feedback and inference budgets, it is difficult to ascertain whether observed performance gains are due to genuine improvements in harness design or merely from the additional search budget allocated to the evaluation tasks themselves.
2.  **Risk of Overfitting:** When the tasks used for evolving the harness and those used for final evaluation are drawn from the same benchmark, there is a risk that the reported performance gains reflect adaptation to task-specific patterns within that benchmark, rather than generalizable improvements in harness design that would transfer to unseen tasks. This raises questions about the robustness and reusability of the "evolved" harnesses.

This work critically re-examines these evaluation practices, positioning itself as a call for more rigorous and fair assessment of automatic harness evolution by advocating for controlled comparisons and generalization tests.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to critically revisit and re-evaluate the effectiveness of automatic harness evolution for large language model (LLM) agents. The authors are motivated by specific methodological concerns in how such systems are typically evaluated, aiming to determine whether the reported benefits of automatic harness evolution are truly attributable to superior harness design or to other factors.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

*   **To address the conflation of harness evolution with test-time scaling:** Current automatic harness evolution methods operate as iterative search procedures, much like test-time scaling techniques (e.g., parallel sampling, sequential refinement) that improve agent performance by allocating additional computation at inference time. These existing evaluation protocols often fail to provide a direct comparison against such baselines, even though both types of methods consume computational resources to improve task outcomes. The motivation is to disentangle whether the gains observed in harness evolution stem from fundamental improvements in the harness's design, which can be reused, or from the inherent benefit of repeated computation and feedback acquisition during the search process, which test-time scaling methods also leverage. The authors seek to establish a fairer comparison by matching feedback and inference budgets across harness evolution and test-time scaling baselines.
*   **To assess the generalizability of evolved harnesses:** A significant concern arises when the same benchmark tasks are used for both the optimization (search) phase of harness evolution and the final evaluation. This setup risks "overfitting" to the specific instances and patterns present in the chosen benchmark. The authors are motivated to investigate whether improvements discovered through harness evolution generalize to held-out, unseen tasks. If an evolved harness only performs well on the tasks it was optimized on, its utility as a generalizable agent design principle is limited. This objective aims to determine if the revisions made to the harness constitute a true improvement in its design that is transferable, or merely adaptations to the specific evaluation instances encountered during the evolution process.
*   **To provide a unified framework for evaluation:** The research introduces a formalized evaluation protocol that defines what feedback each method receives, how it allocates inference compute, and whether it modifies task trajectories or the harness itself. This framework is designed to provide a more structured and transparent way of comparing different approaches to agent improvement, ensuring that methods are assessed under comparable conditions.
*   **To question the perceived effectiveness of automatic harness evolution:** Based on the identified concerns, the authors implicitly aim to challenge the current understanding and enthusiasm surrounding automatic harness evolution. By conducting a rigorous re-evaluation, they intend to generate findings that either confirm or critically re-interpret the reported performance gains of these methods, thereby fostering a more accurate assessment of their real-world utility and guiding future research directions.

In essence, the research is motivated by a desire to ensure that advancements in automatic harness design are genuinely robust and generalizable, rather than being an artifact of evaluation methodologies that may inadvertently inflate perceived performance.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes a unified budget view to formalize the evaluation of harness evolution and compare it against test-time scaling methods. This framework explicitly controls the feedback mechanisms and computational budgets, making explicit whether performance gains reflect reusable harness improvements or task-specific discovery.

**4.1. Common Notation and Framework**

*   **Agent Policy ($\pi_{\theta}$):** The fixed underlying language model agent.
*   **Task ($x$):** A task drawn from a task distribution ($\mathcal{X}$).
*   **Harness ($h$):** The external configuration (prompts, tools, memory, etc.) that interacts with the agent policy.
*   **Trajectory ($y$):** The sequence of states, actions, and observations generated by executing the agent on a task with a given harness, $y \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot | x; h)$.
*   **Outcome ($R(y, g)$):** A binary function (0 or 1) indicating whether a trajectory $y$ successfully solves a task given a unit test case $g$.
*   **Compute Budget ($K$):** The total computational budget, which limits the number of rollouts or iterative steps.
*   **Summarization Map ($\Phi$):** A function, implemented by the same underlying model as the policy, which summarizes an experience store (e.g., recurring failures, redundant attempts) to inform subsequent refinement or editing steps.

**4.2. Methods Compared**

The study compares four methods, categorized into Test-Time Scaling and Automatic Harness Evolution, under a unified budget of $K=5$ iterations or rollouts:

**a. Test-Time Scaling Methods:** These methods operate with a fixed harness ($h$) and explore improvements by iterating on trajectories for a given task.

*   **Parallel Sampling:**
    *   **Approach:** Generates $K$ independent trajectories ($y_1, \dots, y_K \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot | x; h)$) for a given task. Exploration spreads across multiple independent attempts.
    *   **Selection (without unit tests):** A self-judge (the agent itself) selects the best candidate based on its own assessment ($ \hat{y} = \arg \max_{k \le K} J(y_k) $).
    *   **Selection (with unit tests):** Any trajectory accepted by the outcome function is selected ($ \hat{y} \in \{ y_k : R(y_k, g) = 1 \} $).
*   **Sequential Refinement:**
    *   **Approach:** Generates $K$ sequential trajectories ($y_k$) where each subsequent trajectory builds on the previous one, allowing the agent to reflect and revise. The harness ($h$) remains fixed.
    *   **Refinement (without unit tests):** The agent reads the previous trajectory ($y_k \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot | x, \Phi(y_{k-1}); h)$). The final trajectory ($ \hat{y} = y_K $) is returned.
    *   **Refinement (with unit tests):** The agent reads both the previous trajectory and its outcome ($y_k \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot | x, \Phi(y_{k-1}, R(y_{k-1}, g)); h)$). Any accepted trajectory is returned.

**b. Automatic Harness Evolution Methods:** These methods allocate budget to updating the harness itself, either across a task distribution or for a single task instance.

*   **Harness Evolution:**
    *   **Approach:** Optimizes a *shared* harness across a batch of tasks. An iterative loop involves:
        1.  Sampling $m=1$ rollout per task for a batch of $n$ tasks under the current harness ($h_k$).
        2.  Storing this evidence ($e_k$) including the harness and rollouts (and outcomes if available).
        3.  A meta agent ($M$) reads a summary of the accumulated store ($\Phi(C_{k-1})$) and emits the next harness ($h_k = M(\Phi(C_{k-1}))$). This process repeats for $K$ rounds.
    *   **Selection (without unit tests):** The most evolved harness ($ \hat{h} = h_K $) is used for the final trajectory.
    *   **Selection (with unit tests):** The harness with the highest aggregate outcome on the task batch is selected ($ \hat{h} = \arg \max_{k \le K} \bar{R}(h_k) $). The final trajectory is then sampled under this selected harness.
    *   **Implementation:** Instantiated using Agentic Harness Engineering (AHE) [Lin et al., 2026], with its "explore agent" (which retrieves benchmark-tuned harnesses) explicitly disabled to ensure gains are from evolution feedback, not external retrieval.
*   **Harness Scaling:**
    *   **Approach:** Adapts the harness *for a single evaluation instance* by revising it at each step. This is a harness-level analogue of test-time scaling.
    *   **Revision (without unit tests):** The meta agent updates the harness ($h_k = M(x, \Phi(h_{k-1}, y_{k-1}))$) based on the task, previous harness, and trajectory. A new trajectory ($y_k$) is sampled under the updated harness. The latest trajectory ($ \hat{y} = y_K $) is returned.
    *   **Revision (with unit tests):** The update also conditions on the outcome of the previous trajectory ($h_k = M(x, \Phi(h_{k-1}, y_{k-1}, R(y_{k-1}, g))$). Any accepted trajectory is returned.

**4.3. Experimental Setup**

*   **Benchmark:** Terminal-Bench 2.1, a verified suite of 89 command-line interface tasks, used to align with prior work.
*   **Models:** Three frontier LLMs were used: Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.4 mini.
*   **Agent Configuration:** Maximum generation budget of 128k tokens, high reasoning effort setting for all models. All results averaged over two independent runs to reduce variance.
*   **Initial Harness:** All methods were initialized with an identical minimal harness, granting the model access only to a single bash tool, without any pre-defined skills, middleware, or persistent memory. This minimal setup is the same as the base harness used in AHE.
*   **Compute Budget:** $K=5$ iterations for all methods (e.g., 5 independent samples, 5 refinement steps, 5 harness evolution rounds, or 5 harness scaling steps).
*   **Meta Agent/Debugger:** The summarization map ($\Phi$) and meta agent ($M$) utilize the "Agent Debugger" from the AHE framework, which distills actionable evidence from raw rollout trajectories.
*   **Evaluation Metrics:**
    *   **pass@1:** The primary metric, representing the average binary reward (success/failure) over all rollouts across all tasks. Rollouts terminating on infrastructure exceptions (e.g., sandbox crash, API timeout) are scored as 0.
    *   **pass@K (specifically pass@5 for with unit tests):** The fraction of tasks solved by at least one of the $K$ rollouts. This metric is used when unit test cases are available, as an oracle selection can identify the best among multiple attempts.

**4.4. Experimental Settings**

The methods were evaluated across three distinct settings:

1.  **Without Unit Test Cases:** Agents rely solely on self-generated trajectories for revision or selection. This tests self-evaluation capabilities.
2.  **With Unit Test Cases:** Unit test cases provide explicit correctness signals for both iterative refinement and final trajectory selection. This evaluates performance with strong external feedback.
3.  **Generalization Setting (Disjoint Search and Evaluation Tasks):** Terminal-Bench 2.1 was split into 45 training, 10 validation, and 34 held-out test tasks. Harness Evolution was performed on the training set, the best harness selected based on validation performance, and then evaluated on the unseen test set. This assesses transferability of evolved harnesses. Other methods, being instance-level, were not directly applicable here.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments reveal consistent patterns across various settings, indicating that automatic harness evolution, under the tested conditions, does not consistently outperform simpler test-time scaling methods and exhibits limited generalization.

**5.1. Without Unit Test Cases (Table 1)**

In the absence of unit test cases, where agents must rely on self-generated trajectories for feedback:

*   **Overall Underperformance of Harness Evolution:** Automatic harness evolution algorithms, specifically "Harness Evolution," did not yield stronger performance gains than test-time scaling methods. On average across Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.4 mini, Harness Evolution achieved a pass@1 score of 67.4, which was lower than the direct sampling baseline (68.2).
*   **Model-Specific Degradation:** The performance degradation was notable for GPT-5.4, where Harness Evolution's score dropped from 75.3 (direct sampling) to 69.7. This suggests that iterative harness revision guided solely by an agent's own judgment can be detrimental for strong models.
*   **Effectiveness of Parallel Sampling:** "Parallel Sampling" was the most consistent and effective approach, improving the average score from 68.2 to 72.3 and showing gains across all three models.
*   **Limited Impact of Sequential Refinement:** "Sequential Refinement" provided only a marginal average improvement of 1.1 points and slightly decreased performance for GPT-5.4.
*   **Harness Scaling's Competitiveness:** "Harness Scaling," an instance-guided harness adaptation method, performed better than Harness Evolution, achieving an average of 71.8 and the best single result on Claude Opus 4.6.
*   **Conclusion:** These results indicate that even powerful agents struggle to reliably extract useful learning signals from their own noisy trajectories to revise the harness without external correctness signals. Simpler parallel sampling proved more effective in this feedback-limited environment.

**5.2. With Unit Test Cases (Table 2)**

When unit test cases were available, providing explicit correctness signals for both iterative refinement and selection:

*   **General Improvement, but Baselines Lead:** All methods (test-time scaling and harness evolution) showed substantial improvements over direct sampling, confirming the value of unit test feedback. However, "Harness Evolution" and "Harness Scaling" did not outperform the simpler test-time scaling baselines.
*   **Pass@1 Performance:** On pass@1, "Harness Evolution" showed only a modest improvement over direct sampling (75.8 average vs. 72.9). "Parallel Sampling" achieved the highest average pass@1 (86.0).
*   **Pass@5 Performance:** On pass@5, which measures if at least one of five attempts succeeds, "Sequential Refinement" was the strongest method, with an average of 91.8, surpassing "Harness Evolution" (86.2) and "Harness Scaling" (89.3).
*   **Conclusion:** The fact that harness evolution's benefits largely materialized in pass@5, rather than pass@1, suggests that its gains primarily stem from making multiple attempts and selecting the best one, rather than from reliably enabling the agent to solve previously unsolved tasks through improved harness design. Refining solutions (as in Sequential Refinement) appeared to be a more productive use of extra compute than revising the harness itself.

**5.3. Harness Evolution Fails to Generalize Beyond Training Tasks (Table 3)**

To assess generalization, Terminal-Bench 2.1 was split into training, validation, and held-out test sets. A harness evolved on the training set was evaluated on the unseen test set:

*   **Marginal Gains:** The evolved harness yielded only marginal gains on the test set. It improved Claude Opus 4.6 by 1.2 points and showed no improvement for GPT-5.4, resulting in an average gain of just 0.6 points over the initial harness.
*   **Contrast with Prior Results:** This lack of generalization stands in sharp contrast to the improvements reported when harness evolution is evaluated on the same tasks it was optimized on.
*   **Conclusion:** This discrepancy suggests that the revisions discovered during the evolution process tend to encode task-specific shortcuts rather than generalizable harness design principles. Current harness evolution algorithms appear to exhibit limited generalization ability and a strong tendency to overfit to the training tasks.

**5.4. Discussion: Rational Edits but Marginal Gains (Section 5.1)**

A qualitative analysis of harness modifications revealed that the meta agent in harness evolution and scaling made rational, well-motivated edits across various layers (prompt, middleware, tool). These included adding behavioral rules, enforcing runtime constraints (e.g., turn budget trackers), and correcting misleading tool guidance. For Harness Scaling, modifications often encoded knowledge about specific failures, such as command sequences, file paths, or verification checks, into the prompt or memory.

However, despite these edits:

*   **Memorizing Fixes vs. Distilling Strategies:** Most edits tended to "memorize fixes" for specific failures rather than distilling generalizable strategies. This often saved time on tasks the agent could already solve but rarely converted fundamental failures into successes.
*   **Unaffected Hard Tasks:** A "stable core of hard tasks," often stemming from deep domain reasoning demands or constraints beyond harness control, remained largely unaffected by the accumulated knowledge.
*   **Context Bloat:** The increasing volume of persistent prompt text introduced context bloat, which could offset any remaining gains.
*   **Task Difficulty and Harness Sensitivity (Section 5.2):** The authors suggest that the limited effectiveness might also be due to characteristics of Terminal-Bench. The benchmark's tasks might already be relatively easy for frontier LLMs, with remaining failures rooted in model limitations rather than harness deficiencies. Furthermore, Terminal-Bench might not be highly sensitive to harness design, meaning that a minimal setup already suffices for most solvable tasks, thus limiting the impact of harness modifications.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries several significant implications for the evaluation and development of LLM agents and automatic harness design methods:

*   **Challenges Current Evaluation Protocols:** The study directly challenges the validity of existing evaluation protocols for automatic harness evolution. By demonstrating that common practices can conflate genuine improvements in harness design with the benefits of additional computational search or overfitting to evaluation tasks, it highlights a critical methodological flaw in a burgeoning research area.
*   **Re-contextualizes Reported Gains:** The findings suggest that many reported performance gains from automatic harness evolution in prior literature might be overstated or misinterpreted. Researchers are encouraged to re-evaluate the true source of these gains, considering the alternative explanations provided (e.g., test-time scaling effects, task-specific adaptation).
*   **Establishes Strong Baselines:** By formalizing and rigorously comparing harness evolution against test-time scaling methods (parallel sampling and sequential refinement) under matched budgets, the paper establishes these simpler approaches as crucial and strong baselines. Future work on automatic harness design will need to demonstrate consistent and generalizable improvements beyond these baselines to be considered significant advancements.
*   **Emphasizes Generalization:** The stark demonstration of limited generalization for evolved harnesses underscores the importance of evaluating agent systems on held-out tasks. This promotes a focus on developing methods that yield truly transferable design principles rather than task-specific "shortcuts."
*   **Informs Future Benchmark Design:** The discussion regarding task difficulty and harness sensitivity provides valuable criteria for designing future benchmarks. The authors propose that benchmarks for evaluating harness evolution should include tasks that are sufficiently difficult to offer substantial headroom for improvement and, critically, where performance is highly dependent on effective harness design (e.g., requiring specialized tools, skills, or workflows). This would better differentiate the efficacy of various harness design approaches.
*   **Guides Research Directions:** The paper implicitly guides future research in automatic harness design to focus on mechanisms that promote generalizable strategies rather than mere memorization of fixes. This might involve developing more sophisticated meta-agents capable of distilling abstract design principles or exploring methods that explicitly encourage robustness across diverse task distributions.
*   **Promotes Transparency and Rigor:** Overall, this work advocates for greater transparency and rigor in the evaluation of agentic systems. By meticulously defining feedback loops, compute budgets, and evaluation metrics, it sets a higher standard for experimental design in LLM agent research, fostering more robust and reliable scientific progress.

In summary, this research serves as a critical re-evaluation that refines the understanding of automatic harness evolution, urging the community to adopt more stringent evaluation methodologies to ascertain the true impact and generalizability of proposed advancements in agent design.
