## Research Paper Analysis: A Technical Taxonomy of LLM Agent Communication Protocols

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this research paper are Linus Sander, Habtom Kahsay Gidey, Alexander Lenz, and Alois Knoll. All authors are affiliated with Technische Universität München, located in Munich, Germany.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has led to significant advancements in artificial intelligence, with models such as GPT-4, DeepSeek-V3, and Claude Opus 4 demonstrating capabilities in language understanding, generation, reasoning, and planning. These LLMs form the core of LLM-based agents, which are equipped with memory, sensors, and actuators to interact dynamically with environments and perform complex, multistep tasks.

Building upon the concept of single LLM agents, the research landscape is increasingly focused on multi-agent systems (MAS). These systems consist of multiple specialized agents designed to communicate, collaborate, and debate, thereby leveraging collective intelligence to achieve problem-solving outcomes that may exceed the capabilities of individual agents. Key frameworks for building MAS include Microsoft's AutoGen, CrewAI, CAMEL, and LangGraph.

However, the proliferation of diverse MAS frameworks and agent implementations has introduced a challenge: the fragmentation of communication protocols. This fragmentation impedes interoperability, hindering the seamless discovery and collaboration of heterogeneous agents across different use cases. Standardization of communication protocols is identified as a critical requirement for unlocking the full potential of distributed MAS and enabling robust integration with external systems like APIs.

While the field of LLM-based agents is rapidly evolving, a universally accepted standard for communication protocols has not yet emerged. Existing solutions often overlap in functionality but lack interoperability. Prior research has begun to address this area, with Li et al. (2025) highlighting the "protocol wars" and advocating for a unified framework, and Du (2025) examining AI agent communication from an internet architecture perspective. Kong et al. (2025) provided a security-focused survey and proposed a three-class taxonomy for agent communication, while Ehtesham et al. (2025) conducted a comparative analysis of four specific interoperability protocols (MCP, ACP, A2A, ANP). Marro et al. (2024) introduced the Agora protocol, emphasizing a "communication trilemma" involving versatility, efficiency, and portability. Yang et al. (2025) also proposed a two-dimensional taxonomy for LLM agent communication protocols.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the need for a more comprehensive and structured classification framework. It acknowledges the existing efforts but identifies a gap where current taxonomies may be insufficient in providing the abstract, hierarchical structure required for in-depth exploration and analysis of the diverse protocol landscape. The work aims to develop a detailed technical taxonomy to classify and analyze LLM agent communication protocols, thereby offering a more coherent framework to understand, compare, and track developments in this rapidly advancing domain.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the rapid advancement of large language model (LLM) based agents and the increasing recognition of multi-agent systems (MAS) as a promising frontier in AI. As individual LLM agents demonstrate sophisticated reasoning and planning capabilities, the natural progression involves enabling multiple such agents to cooperate towards common goals, thereby harnessing collective intelligence.

However, the ability of agents to communicate effectively is a foundational prerequisite for any form of collective intelligence or cooperation in MAS. Without robust communication mechanisms, the potential benefits of multi-agent collaboration, such as task decomposition, diverse perspectives, and emergent cognitive behaviors, cannot be fully realized. The current state of LLM agent ecosystems is characterized by a fragmented landscape of communication solutions. Numerous frameworks and specialized protocols have emerged, leading to a lack of interoperability between heterogeneous agents and systems. This fragmentation creates significant challenges for developers aiming to build scalable, flexible, and robust distributed MAS, as it often necessitates hard-coded communication pipelines and impedes dynamic agent discovery and interaction. Moreover, the integration of LLM agents with external tools, services, and data sources also requires standardized communication.

Despite the critical role of communication, explicit communication protocols for LLM-based agents have not been extensively studied or systematically classified in the academic literature. This gap creates a need for a structured approach to understand the various designs, functionalities, and implications of these emerging protocols.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this study are:

1.  **To investigate the emerging landscape:** Systematically examine the diverse range of communication protocols currently being developed for LLM-based agents.
2.  **To develop a comprehensive technical taxonomy:** Construct a classification framework that is capable of organizing and categorizing LLM agent communication protocols based on their technical characteristics and communication paradigms. The taxonomy is intended to provide a coherent and abstract structure for analysis.
3.  **To analyze concrete protocol implementations:** Apply the developed taxonomy to a selected sample of actively maintained, open-source LLM agent communication protocols with demonstrable adoption, thereby validating the taxonomy's utility and identifying recurring architectural patterns.
4.  **To provide a coherent framework for understanding and tracking future developments:** Offer researchers and developers a structured tool to comprehend the complexities of agent communication, identify trends, and anticipate future innovations in the field.
5.  **To guide protocol selection and highlight open research gaps:** Facilitate informed decisions regarding the choice of communication protocols for specific use cases and identify areas requiring further research and development, such as privacy, compliance, and policy enforcement in agent interactions.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed for developing the taxonomy adhered to the structured, iterative, traceable, and reproducible procedure outlined by Nickerson et al. (2013) for taxonomy construction. This approach was chosen to ensure systematic development and rigorous evaluation of the classification framework.

**4.1 Taxonomy Development Method**

The process began with defining three foundational elements:
*   **Purpose:** The taxonomy's purpose is to classify and distinguish LLM agent communication protocols based on the type of communication they facilitate. It focuses on protocols that connect an LLM agent to another system (another agent, tool, service, API, or data source) primarily at the application level, avoiding low-level technical implementation details. Its intended users are researchers, agent developers, and non-experts seeking an overview, guidance for protocol selection, and insights into research gaps.
*   **Meta-characteristic:** The meta-characteristic for this taxonomy is defined as "classifying the protocols according to the types of components involved and the characteristics of their communication."
*   **Ending Conditions:** The development process was guided by both objective ending conditions (e.g., no new dimensions emerge, every characteristic is instantiated) and subjective quality criteria for useful taxonomies as defined by Nickerson et al. (2013): conciseness, robustness, comprehensiveness, extendibility, and explanatory power.

Following these foundational definitions, an iterative development cycle was undertaken. A total of five iterations were conducted. The initial three iterations followed an empirical-to-conceptual path, involving the examination of concrete protocol implementations to distill meaningful attributes and group them into abstract dimensions. The subsequent two iterations adopted a conceptual-to-empirical path, where candidate dimensions derived from theory were applied to the protocol sample and refined. Each iteration concluded with a review and evaluation of the nascent taxonomy against the predefined ending conditions and quality criteria. This iterative process ensured that the taxonomy was grounded in empirical reality while also satisfying theoretical design principles.

**4.2 Communication Protocols Sample Selection**

A rigorous selection process was applied to identify the nine communication protocols used to ground the taxonomy development. The criteria for inclusion were:
*   **Target Domain:** Protocols had to be explicitly designed for connecting an LLM agent with another system, which could be another LLM agent or a general information system. Generic agent communication languages (e.g., FIPA ACL, KQML) or abstract task-sharing protocols (e.g., Contract Net Protocol) not specifically tailored for LLM agents were excluded.
*   **Open Source and Implementation Availability:** Only protocols with publicly available, open-source implementations were considered. The implementation did not need to be production-ready, a test or research prototype was sufficient. This criterion led to the exclusion of concept-only protocols (e.g., LOKA) and proprietary solutions (e.g., Firecrawl, uAgents protocol).
*   **Active Maintenance and Adoption:** Protocols were required to be actively maintained, as evidenced by recent GitHub activity (beyond README file changes), and demonstrate real-world traction or community adoption, quantified by GitHub stars (a minimum of 20 stars for protocols released in February 2025 was used as a heuristic). Protocols deemed inactive (e.g., Agent Protocol) or with very low adoption (e.g., AITP protocol) were excluded.

Applying these criteria resulted in a sample of nine protocols: Model Context Protocol (MCP), Agent to Agent (A2A), LangChain Agent Protocol (LAP), agents.json, Agora, Agent Network Protocol (ANP), LMOS, Agent Communication Protocol (ACP), and agntcy. Each selected protocol was briefly summarized to highlight its core function and design principles.

### 5. Main Findings and Results: Taxonomy Design

The iterative development process culminated in a technical taxonomy for LLM agent communication protocols comprising five distinct dimensions. These dimensions, along with their mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive characteristics, provide a structured framework for classifying and analyzing protocols.

**5.1 Accepted Dimensions**

The finalized taxonomy includes the following five dimensions, which are depicted in Figure 2 of the original paper:

1.  **Counterparty:** This dimension categorizes the type of entity an LLM agent interacts with through the protocol.
    *   **Agent:** The protocol facilitates communication between one LLM agent and another.
    *   **Context:** The protocol connects an LLM agent to non-agent entities, such as tools, services, APIs, or data sources.
    *   **Hybrid:** The protocol supports communication with both other LLM agents and non-agent entities.
    *   *Rationale:* This dimension extends existing classifications (e.g., Yang et al., 2025) by explicitly incorporating a "hybrid" category, acknowledging protocols that serve dual interaction purposes.

2.  **Payload:** This dimension classifies the nature of the data exchanged via the protocol.
    *   **Structured data, artifacts:** The protocol is designed to exchange only structured data or specific artifacts, including associated metadata.
    *   **Conversation focused:** The payload is primarily message-centric, with textual content forming a consistent part of the exchange, potentially supplemented by structured or semi-structured data.
    *   **Hybrid:** The protocol is capable of exchanging both conversation-focused textual messages and purely structured data or artifacts.
    *   *Rationale:* This classification focuses on the intended purpose and primary nature of the data exchanged, distinguishing between protocols designed for conversational interaction versus those for structured data transfers.

3.  **Interaction State:** This dimension addresses whether the protocol maintains a stateful unit of work across message exchanges within an interaction.
    *   **Stateless:** The protocol does not inherently retain information about previous messages; each request is processed independently.
    *   **Session state:** The protocol implements mechanisms to preserve context and state across multiple messages, maintaining a unit of work for the duration of a session.
    *   *Rationale:* This binary dimension is aligned with established concepts in communication networks (e.g., Ling et al., 2004), focusing on state persistence within a single interaction session.

4.  **Discovery Mechanism:** This dimension describes how agents locate and identify available counterparties and their interfaces.
    *   **Static:** The requesting agent must possess prior knowledge of the counterparty's endpoint address.
    *   **Centralized:** A central registry or directory service maintains a list of accessible endpoints and their capabilities.
    *   **Partially centralized:** Discovery is facilitated by a limited set of specialized supernodes.
    *   **Decentralized:** No central authority exists; discovery relies on peer-to-peer mechanisms, potentially involving network broadcasting or distributed indices.
    *   **Hybrid:** The protocol supports multiple discovery mechanisms beyond simple static configuration, potentially combining centralized and decentralized elements.
    *   *Rationale:* This dimension draws upon comprehensive surveys of distributed search techniques (e.g., Ahmed and Boutaba, 2010) to provide a nuanced classification of discovery approaches.

5.  **Schema Flexibility:** This dimension assesses the degree to which a protocol permits adjustment or negotiation of communication schemas, message formats, or dialogue structures.
    *   **Single:** The protocol adheres to a single, predefined communication schema or interaction pattern that is fixed before runtime.
    *   **Multiple:** The protocol supports a selection of predefined, static schemas from which the communicating entities can choose at runtime.
    *   **Evolving:** The protocol enables interacting components to dynamically negotiate, generate, or adapt new communication schemas and interaction styles during runtime.
    *   *Rationale:* This dimension highlights a critical aspect of adaptability in LLM agent communication, distinguishing between rigid, pre-defined structures and dynamic, negotiated formats.

**5.2 Rejected Dimensions**

Three dimensions were considered during development but ultimately excluded from the final taxonomy due to issues such as lack of mutual exclusivity, vagueness, or insufficient analytical value:

*   **Initiative Flow:** This dimension aimed to classify which party could initiate communication. It was rejected because most protocols exhibited similar initiative flows (context-bound bidirectional), and nearly any protocol could be implemented symmetrically, limiting its discriminatory power.
*   **Simplicity:** Intended to capture the lightweightness or complexity of a protocol, this dimension was rejected because the concept of "simplicity" lacked objective boundaries, making it difficult to define mutually exclusive characteristics.
*   **Persistent State:** While distinct from "Interaction State," this dimension focused on the long-term storage of interaction data beyond a single session. It was rejected because nearly all protocols implicitly or explicitly allow for metadata persistence, and the protocol itself does not dictate what connected nodes ultimately store. Providing persistent state was deemed an implementation detail for connected nodes rather than a core protocol objective.

**5.3 Classifying Concrete Protocols**

The nine selected protocols were classified according to the accepted dimensions, revealing patterns and validating the taxonomy's utility. A summarized overview is provided in Tables 1 and 2 of the original paper.

*   **Counterparty:** Protocols such as MCP and agents.json were classified as *Context*, as they are designed for LLM agents to interact with external tools or APIs. Protocols like A2A, Agora, ANP, LAP, ACP, and agntcy were classified as *Agent*, focusing on inter-agent collaboration. LMOS was categorized as *Hybrid*, supporting both agent-to-agent and agent-to-context interactions.
*   **Payload:** MCP and agents.json were identified as primarily *Structured data, artifacts* due to their focus on API descriptions and structured tool invocation. The remaining seven protocols (A2A, LAP, Agora, ANP, LMOS, ACP, agntcy) were classified as *Hybrid*, supporting both textual messages and structured data exchanges.
*   **Interaction State:** MCP and agents.json were found to be *Stateless*, lacking explicit mechanisms for session state persistence in their core design. All protocols primarily facilitating agent-to-agent communication (A2A, LAP, Agora, ANP, LMOS, ACP, agntcy) were categorized as supporting *Session state*, crucial for multi-turn interactions.
*   **Discovery Mechanism:** MCP, LAP, agents.json, and Agora primarily relied on *Static* discovery, requiring prior knowledge of endpoints. A2A, ANP, ACP, and agntcy utilized *Centralized* registries. LMOS was classified as *Hybrid*, incorporating multiple discovery approaches.
*   **Schema Flexibility:** LAP and agents.json supported a *Single* fixed schema. MCP, A2A, LMOS, ACP, and agntcy allowed for *Multiple* predefined schemas. Notably, Agora and ANP were classified as *Evolving*, featuring mechanisms for dynamic schema negotiation at runtime.

Overall, patterns emerged indicating that agent-to-agent protocols consistently incorporate session state and hybrid payloads. There is also a trend toward greater schema flexibility (multiple or evolving schemas). However, decentralized discovery remains uncommon, with most protocols relying on centralized registries or static configurations.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the evolving field of LLM agent systems by providing a structured and comprehensive technical taxonomy for communication protocols. The developed framework helps to organize and clarify a complex and rapidly expanding domain, enabling researchers and developers to gain a more intuitive understanding of the various communication paradigms. By classifying current and future protocols, the taxonomy facilitates abstract-level comparisons, aids in identifying overarching trends, and can inform the anticipation of future innovations. Furthermore, it serves as a practical guide for selecting appropriate protocols for specific use cases and highlights critical areas that require further research and development.

**Key Insights and Implications:**

The application of the taxonomy to nine concrete protocols yielded several insights:
*   **Agent-to-Agent Communication Characteristics:** Protocols designed for agent-to-agent interaction consistently support session state functionality, essential for multi-turn dialogues, and utilize hybrid payloads that can transmit both textual messages and structured data. This indicates a recognition of the diverse information exchange needs in collaborative agent environments.
*   **Trend Towards Schema Flexibility:** A clear trend was observed towards greater schema flexibility, with the majority of protocols supporting multiple predefined schemas, and a notable subset enabling schemas to evolve through negotiation at runtime. This flexibility is considered crucial for LLM-based agents to leverage their capabilities fully in dynamic interactions.
*   **Limited Decentralized Discovery:** Despite the potential for a decentralized "Internet of Agents," most protocols still rely on centralized registries or static configurations for discovery. This suggests a research gap in exploring more robust and scalable decentralized discovery mechanisms.
*   **Long-Term Architectural Evolution:** The analysis suggests that while short-term convergence pressure might exist towards protocols unifying agent-to-agent and agent-to-context interactions, a single monolithic standard is unlikely to maximize versatility, efficiency, and portability simultaneously. Instead, the field is anticipated to evolve towards a federated, layered protocol stack, akin to the OSI model in traditional networking. This architecture would allow specialized protocols to operate at different layers, for instance, lightweight specifications for static capability discovery (e.g., agents.json), highly structured protocols for secure tool execution (e.g., MCP), and session-aware, schema-evolving protocols for complex multi-turn deliberations (e.g., ANP or Agora).

**Addressing the Communication Trilemma:**

The study applied Marro et al.'s (2024) Agent Communication Trilemma, which states that a protocol cannot simultaneously optimize versatility, efficiency, and portability. This framework reinforced the observed architectural trade-offs:
*   **Efficiency and Portability:** Protocols primarily focused on agent-to-context interactions, such as MCP, maximize efficiency and portability through rigid schemas, stateless interactions, and strictly structured data payloads. This design minimizes overhead, making them suitable for predictable, high-throughput tool invocations.
*   **Versatility:** Conversely, protocols like Agora and ANP, which feature evolving schemas, prioritize versatility to support dynamic and open-ended multi-agent debates. However, this adaptability inherently incurs higher token overhead and latency due to runtime schema negotiation, reducing efficiency.
*   **Balanced Approaches:** The majority of surveyed protocols (e.g., A2A, LMOS, ACP) occupy a middle ground, offering a balance by adopting hybrid payloads, session state persistence, and multiple pre-established schemas. This provides sufficient versatility for complex interactions without incurring the most extreme efficiency penalties.

The implications of the trilemma strongly support the argument for a layered protocol stack, where different protocols are designed to excel in specific trade-off points, collectively supporting the full spectrum of agent communication scenarios.

**Identified Research Gaps and Future Work:**

A significant gap identified across nearly all investigated protocols is the lack of widespread integration of privacy safeguards, compliance checks, and policy enforcement mechanisms. As LLM agents become deployed in sensitive or safety-critical domains (e.g., healthcare, human resources), these mechanisms will be crucial for protecting personal and security-sensitive data.

Future research could further enrich the taxonomy by incorporating additional technical protocol dimensions, exploring authentication and security mechanisms in greater detail, and examining how policies and norms are embedded within protocols. Classifying newly emerging protocols would also strengthen the taxonomy's validity and enable ongoing analysis of domain developments. The framework also provides a foundation for evaluating which protocols are best suited for particular use cases and application domains.

In conclusion, this work highlights the critical role of robust communication in the advancement of LLM-powered multi-agent systems. By developing a systematic taxonomy and analyzing existing protocols, the study provides a foundational understanding of the current landscape and offers a vision for the future evolution of agent communication towards a federated, layered architecture, thereby laying groundwork for more capable and autonomous agent networks.
