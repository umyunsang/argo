### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The use of Large Language Models (LLMs) as judges for evaluating model outputs has become an established practice, particularly for subjective, open-ended tasks where human annotation is costly and time-consuming. This approach, often termed "LLM-as-a-judge," provides a scalable alternative to human evaluation. Existing work often focuses on subjective evaluations like helpfulness or alignment, where approximate agreement is sufficient.

However, extending LLM-as-a-judge to objective, factual evaluation presents distinct reliability challenges. In such tasks, where answers are definitively correct or incorrect, LLM judges must determine factual accuracy without an explicit reference answer. Two primary issues arise:
1.  **Bounded Parametric Knowledge**: LLMs may lack up-to-date knowledge, information on rare entities, or specialized domain expertise, limiting their ability to distinguish correct answers from plausible fabrications.
2.  **Hallucination**: Even with relevant knowledge, LLMs can produce confident but incorrect verdicts, providing no indication of their unreliability.

Current approaches to mitigate these issues include:
*   **Tool Augmentation**: Methods like SAFE (Wei et al., 2024) and SAGE (Badshah et al., 2026b) equip LLM judges with web search to verify facts or gather evidence. While these improve accuracy, they often introduce additional computational cost and lack formal guarantees on verdict reliability. Furthermore, retrieval is often unconditional, leading to unnecessary computation.
*   **Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)**: Techniques such as predictive entropy (Malinin & Gales, 2021) or consistency checks from repeated querying (Wang et al., 2023; 2024) can identify low-confidence verdicts. However, these methods typically rely on heuristic thresholds, which do not offer formal control over the error rate among accepted verdicts.
*   **Selective Prediction**: Allowing models to abstain when uncertain is a natural strategy. Recent work on risk-controlled selective prediction (Wang et al., 2026; 2025a; Angelopoulos et al., 2024) provides statistical guarantees, bounding the False Discovery Rate (FDR) among selected outputs. However, these methods are often designed for filtering examinee answers rather than meta-evaluation, and generally treat abstention as the only recourse, discarding instances that might be resolvable with additional evidence. Existing risk-controlled LLM judging frameworks like Trust or Escalate (Jung et al., 2025) and SCOPE (Badshah et al., 2026a) primarily target subjective preference tasks.

This work addresses the gap by integrating risk-controlled selective evaluation with adaptive tool use for objective LLM judging, aiming to provide provable guarantees on the reliability of accepted factual verdicts while enhancing coverage by strategically leveraging external evidence.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are:

1.  **Formulating Risk-Controlled Selective Evaluation for Objective LLM-as-a-Judge**: The core motivation is to enable LLM judges to assess factual correctness reliably in a reference-free setting. The absence of ground-truth annotations at scale and the challenge of silent failures (confident but incorrect verdicts) necessitate a mechanism to formally quantify and control the risk associated with LLM judgments. This work aims to adapt and apply risk-controlled selective prediction techniques to the meta-evaluation context of LLM judging for factual QA.

2.  **Providing Formal Guarantees on Verdict Reliability**: A significant limitation of existing LLM-as-a-judge systems is the lack of formal control over the error rate of accepted verdicts. The paper seeks to address this by proposing a framework that calibrates uncertainty thresholds on a held-out dataset, ensuring that the False Discovery Rate (FDR) among accepted verdicts remains below a user-specified risk level (α) with high probability (1-δ), using finite-sample Clopper-Pearson intervals. This provides a provable risk guarantee, enhancing trust in automated evaluations.

3.  **Enhancing Evaluation Coverage through Adaptive Tool Use**: Traditional selective evaluation methods typically resort to abstention when the model is uncertain, potentially discarding many instances where uncertainty could be resolved. Motivated by LLM-based agents that use external tools to overcome parametric limitations, this work aims to integrate web search into the judging process. The objective is to design a mechanism where the judge adaptively retrieves external evidence *only when initially uncertain*, thereby recovering evaluations that would otherwise be abstained, and converting these abstentions into informed verdicts. This adaptive approach aims to reduce computational costs associated with unconditional retrieval while maximizing the number of instances that can be reliably evaluated.

4.  **Extending Finite-Sample Guarantees to a Multi-Mode Routing System**: A key technical objective is to demonstrate that the finite-sample FDR guarantee, typically associated with single-mode selective prediction, can be formally extended to a two-threshold routing setting without requiring additional distributional assumptions. This involves proving that the Bernoulli structure required for the Clopper-Pearson bound holds even when decisions involve sequential evaluation across parametric and retrieval-augmented modes.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for more reliable, transparent, and efficient LLM-based factual evaluation, moving beyond heuristic approaches to provide statistically guaranteed control over error rates while making intelligent use of external tools to maximize evaluative coverage.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework introduces a risk-controlled selective evaluation mechanism for LLM-as-a-judge, featuring adaptive two-mode routing to balance reliability and coverage.

**4.1 Problem Formulation**
*   **Task**: An examinee LLM `F` generates an answer `ˆy` for a question `x`. A judge LLM `J` produces a binary verdict `v ∈ {0, 1}` (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) on `ˆy`'s correctness relative to the ground-truth `y*`.
*   **Admission Function**: A ground-truth evaluation label `e*` is obtained either from human annotation or by comparing `ˆy` to `y*` using a relevance function `A_ref` and a threshold `λ_A`. A verdict `v` is admissible if `v = e*`.
*   **Selective Evaluation**: The judge can abstain if uncertain. A failure indicator `W = 1[v ≠ e*]` is defined. Given an uncertainty score `U`, the judge outputs a verdict only if `U ≤ t` for a threshold `t`. The False Discovery Rate (FDR) is defined as `R(t) = E[W | U ≤ t]`.
*   **Goal**: For a user-specified risk level `α ∈ (0, 1)` and significance level `δ` (e.g., 0.05), the objective is to find thresholds such that `Pr(R(t) ≤ α) ≥ 1 − δ`. This ensures that, with high confidence, the error rate among accepted verdicts is at most `α`.
*   **Calibration Data**: A held-out set `D_cal` of `N` i.i.d. samples with known ground-truth labels `e*` is used for calibration.

**4.2 Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)**
*   The framework utilizes predictive entropy (PE) as the uncertainty measure. PE is computed from the judge’s token-level log-probabilities at the verdict position during a single forward pass. Lower entropy indicates higher confidence. The choice of UQ method primarily impacts coverage, not the formal FDR guarantee.

**4.3 Risk-Bounded Selective Evaluation (Single Mode)**
This describes the base case without retrieval, forming the foundation for the two-mode system.
*   **Empirical Failure Analysis**: For each calibration instance `i`, the judge provides a verdict `v_i` and an uncertainty score `U_i`. For a candidate threshold `t`, the framework calculates the selection size `ˆm_cal(t)` (number of instances with `U_i ≤ t`) and the error count `ˆw_cal(t)` (number of incorrect verdicts among selected instances). The empirical FDR is `ˆr_cal(t) = ˆw_cal(t) / ˆm_cal(t)`.
*   **Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)**: To account for sampling variability, the Clopper-Pearson exact method is applied. Since selected instances are i.i.d. Bernoulli with parameter `R(t)`, a one-sided upper confidence bound `ˆR_upper_1-δ(t)` is computed. This bound satisfies `Pr(R(t) ≤ ˆR_upper_1-δ(t)) ≥ 1 − δ`.
*   **Threshold Selection**: The largest threshold `ˆt` is selected such that its UCB `ˆR_upper_1-δ(t')` remains below `α` for all `t' ≤ t`. This maximizes coverage while satisfying the risk constraint.

**4.4 Adaptive Retrieval via Two-Mode Routing**
To increase coverage, the framework routes uncertain instances to a retrieval-augmented mode.
*   **Two Evaluation Modes**:
    *   **Mode 1 (direct)**: The judge `J` evaluates using only its parametric knowledge, yielding verdict `v_1` and uncertainty `U_1`.
    *   **Mode 2 (retrieval-augmented)**: If Mode 1 is uncertain, the system queries a web search engine using the question, retrieves top-k results (`E_i`), and appends them to the judge's prompt. The judge `J` then re-evaluates with this augmented context, producing `v_2` and `U_2`.
*   **Routing Logic (Test Time)**: Given calibrated thresholds `(ˆt_1, ˆt_2)`:
    1.  If `U_1 ≤ ˆt_1`, accept `v_1`.
    2.  Else (if `U_1 > ˆt_1`), retrieve evidence `E`. If `U_2 ≤ ˆt_2`, accept `v_2`.
    3.  Else, ABSTAIN (flag for human review).
    This ensures retrieval is invoked only when necessary, saving computational cost.
*   **Joint Threshold Calibration**: During calibration, both modes are pre-computed for `D_cal`. For a candidate pair `(t_1, t_2)`, the selection size `ˆm_cal(t_1, t_2)` counts instances accepted by either mode. The error count `ˆw_cal(t_1, t_2)` sums errors from accepted verdicts in both modes. The empirical FDR and Clopper-Pearson UCB (`ˆR_upper_1-δ(t_1, t_2)`) are computed. The pair `(ˆt_1, ˆt_2)` is chosen to maximize `ˆm_cal(t_1, t_2)` subject to `ˆR_upper_1-δ(t_1, t_2) ≤ α`. This optimization is performed via a grid search over sorted unique uncertainty values. A conservative variant employing Bonferroni correction for multiple hypothesis testing is also mentioned but not used for main results.

**4.5 Theoretical Guarantee**
*   **Lemma 1**: The paper formally proves that the Bernoulli structure required for the Clopper-Pearson bound extends to the two-mode routing setting. This is because the routing decision for each instance is a deterministic function of its uncertainty scores and fixed thresholds, preserving the i.i.d. nature of the failure indicators over the selected subset. This enables the direct application of the Clopper-Pearson bound to the joint two-threshold policy, providing a provable FDR guarantee (`Pr(R(t_1, t_2) ≤ ˆR_upper_1-δ(t_1, t_2)) ≥ 1 − δ`).

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on four open-domain QA benchmarks (TriviaQA, Natural Questions, HotpotQA, PopQA) using two candidate models (Qwen3-8B, LLaMA-3.1-70B) and four judge models (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct). Predictive entropy was used for UQ, and an LLM evaluator for determining ground-truth correctness. A 50/50 calibration/test split was run 100 times with `δ = 0.05`.

**5.1 FDR Control Across All Configurations**
*   The framework consistently maintained the empirical FDR at or below the target risk level `α` across all 32 candidate–judge–dataset configurations (Figure 1). This confirms the validity of the finite-sample guarantee.
*   FDR typically tracked close to `α` with narrow confidence bands, suggesting stable calibration. The gap between observed FDR and `α` was smaller on easier datasets (TriviaQA, PopQA), indicating tighter control when the judge possesses sufficient knowledge.

**5.2 Coverage Scales with Risk Tolerance and Judge Capability**
*   Coverage, the fraction of accepted instances, smoothly increased with higher `α` (Table 1, Figure 3).
*   Coverage was influenced by dataset difficulty and judge capability. On easier benchmarks (TriviaQA, PopQA), even smaller judges achieved high coverage at `α = 0.25`.
*   On knowledge-intensive tasks (NQ-Open, HotpotQA), larger judges (Qwen3-14B, LLaMA-3.1-8B) were required to achieve over 80% coverage at `α = 0.20`.
*   Evaluating the stronger LLaMA-3.1-70B candidate consistently led to lower coverage across all judges, reflecting increased complexity and knowledge demands.

**5.3 Adaptive Retrieval Recovers Substantial Coverage**
*   Analysis of the routing distribution revealed that Mode 2 (retrieval-augmented evaluation) was the dominant acceptance mechanism across various configurations (Figure 2).
*   Retrieval-augmented evaluation accounted for the majority of non-abstained verdicts, indicating its crucial role in recovering instances that a single-mode framework would have abstained from due to initial uncertainty.
*   The effectiveness of Mode 2 varied with judge capability and task difficulty. Stronger judges resolved more instances parametrically on easier tasks, while all judges heavily relied on retrieval for harder tasks.
*   Notably, retrieval did not always decrease uncertainty; for 14-29% of instances, uncertainty increased. The second calibrated threshold `ˆt_2` addressed these cases by routing them to abstention, ensuring only reliable retrieval-based verdicts were accepted.

**5.4 Single-Mode vs. Joint Two-Mode Routing**
*   Comparing the "Mode 1 Only" baseline against the full joint framework demonstrated significant coverage gains from adaptive retrieval (Table 2).
*   On TriviaQA, where Mode 1 already achieved high coverage for strong judges, retrieval provided modest, consistent gains.
*   On more challenging datasets like NQ-Open and HotpotQA, the coverage increase was substantial. For example, Qwen3-8B on NQ-Open saw coverage rise from 7% (Mode 1 Only) to 82% (Joint). LLaMA-3.1-8B on HotpotQA increased from 40% to 85%.
*   The weakest judge (Qwen3-4B) benefited significantly on PopQA, with coverage increasing from 4% to 100%.

**5.5 Robustness to Retrieval Non-Stationarity**
*   The framework's robustness to retrieval drift was tested by re-querying the web after three months for TriviaQA and HotpotQA. Despite substantial changes in retrieved URLs (Jaccard overlap ~0.30), the calibrated thresholds maintained FDR control below `α` (Table 3). Coverage also remained largely stable. This suggests that while specific web content changes, the overall quality and ability of the framework to provide sufficient evidence for reliable judging remained consistent, preserving the effectiveness of the calibrated thresholds.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a framework that advances the reliability and efficiency of LLM-as-a-judge systems for objective, factual evaluation. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1.  **Provable Reliability for LLM Judgments**: The primary contribution is the provision of formal, finite-sample guarantees on the False Discovery Rate (FDR) of accepted LLM verdicts. This moves beyond heuristic uncertainty quantification, offering a statistically rigorous method to control the error rate with high probability. This provable guarantee can enhance trust in automated LLM evaluation, which is critical for sensitive applications where factual correctness is paramount.

2.  **Enhanced Evaluation Coverage**: By introducing an adaptive two-mode routing mechanism, the framework efficiently leverages external knowledge (web search) only when the LLM judge is uncertain based on its parametric knowledge. This strategy significantly increases the number of instances that can be reliably evaluated compared to single-mode abstention-only approaches. This improvement in coverage means fewer instances require costly human review, leading to greater efficiency in large-scale evaluation pipelines.

3.  **Adaptive and Cost-Effective Tool Augmentation**: The selective activation of retrieval minimizes unnecessary computational cost associated with external tool use. Retrieval is invoked only when uncertainty is high, optimizing resource allocation by focusing evidence gathering on challenging cases. This pragmatic approach makes tool-augmented LLM judging more practical and scalable.

4.  **Theoretical Foundation for Multi-Modal Selective Prediction**: The proof that the Clopper-Pearson FDR guarantee extends to a two-threshold routing setting without additional distributional assumptions provides a robust theoretical foundation. This expands the applicability of conformal risk control methods to more complex, multi-stage decision-making processes common in advanced AI systems.

5.  **Applicability to Real-World LLM Deployment**: The framework enables the safe deployment of LLM judges in scenarios where factual accuracy is critical and ground-truth labels are scarce or expensive. This includes the evaluation of LLMs operating as autonomous agents or in open-ended query environments. The demonstrated robustness to retrieval non-stationarity suggests practical utility in dynamic real-world settings where web content evolves.

6.  **Guidance for Future Research**: The work opens avenues for future research into extending risk-controlled selective evaluation to other challenging settings, such as graded and subjective evaluations, as well as developing methods for black-box judges without direct access to model logits.

In summary, this research provides a scientifically grounded solution for building more reliable and efficient LLM-as-a-judge systems, offering provable risk guarantees that are crucial for advancing the trusted deployment and evaluation of large language models.
