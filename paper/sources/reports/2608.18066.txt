## Research Paper Analysis: On the Fragility of Self-Improving Agents: Variance, Task Order, and Underspecification

This report provides a detailed analysis of the research paper "On the Fragility of Self-Improving Agents: Variance, Task Order, and Underspecification," covering its authors, broader research context, objectives, methodology, main findings, and significance.

---

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this paper are Qinyuan Ye, Yu Li, Yada Pruksachatkun, Jiaxin Zhang, and Chien-Sheng Wu, all affiliated with Salesforce AI Research.

### 2. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of self-improving agents, systems designed to learn from past experiences and autonomously refine their performance over time, represents a significant and active area of artificial intelligence research. Recent literature has highlighted the potential of memory-based self-improving agents, particularly those that learn from online task streams and improve by maintaining textual memory banks. These agents show promise for automating workflows, creating adaptable systems, and facilitating open-ended research.

However, the existing body of work has primarily focused on demonstrating capabilities and progress, often overlooking critical aspects of reliability and robustness. Prior evaluations frequently report single-run results on predetermined task sequences, which may not adequately capture the variability or real-world performance of these systems. This paper addresses this gap by conducting a rigorous re-evaluation that stresses the reliability of self-improving agents under more challenging and realistic conditions.

This work specifically builds upon and re-evaluates two representative memory-based self-improving systems: Agent Workflow Memory (AWM) [11] and ReasoningBank (RBank) [12]. It contextualizes its findings against the backdrop of rapidly advancing large language models (LLMs) and the increasing integration of agents into real-world systems where reliability is paramount. The paper aligns with a growing call within the research community for more robust evaluation methodologies for AI agents, moving beyond simple capability metrics to include considerations of stability and behavior under diverse conditions. It extends previous discussions on agent reliability [10] from single-session agents to multi-session self-improving agents, where challenges in measurement and potential for cascading errors are amplified.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to critically re-evaluate the reliability of memory-based self-improving agents, specifically focusing on two representative methods: Agent Workflow Memory (AWM) and ReasoningBank (RBank). The authors aim to expose potential fragilities in these systems that have been under-examined in prior work.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several factors:

*   **Overlooked Reliability in Prior Work:** While self-improving agents demonstrate promise, their reliability aspects have not been thoroughly stress-tested. Previous evaluations often present single-run results and utilize default task orderings, potentially masking underlying issues.
*   **Practical Implications of Failure:** In real-world applications, particularly enterprise deployments, there is often minimal tolerance for errors. Initial failures can erode user trust and, if these systems are adopted in high-stakes domains, early mistakes can cascade silently, leading to irreversible or prohibitively costly impacts over time. Before broad deployment, a comprehensive understanding of their reliability under realistic, challenging conditions is necessary.
*   **Advancements in Underlying Language Models:** The capabilities of general-purpose language models have significantly advanced since the introduction of methods like AWM and RBank. This advancement necessitates a reassessment of the effectiveness of these self-improvement techniques when built upon stronger baseline models, as the initial "initialization" can influence observed gains.
*   **Understanding Performance Variability:** Web agent evaluations are characterized as inherently noisy, especially in complex and multi-step tasks. The paper is motivated to quantify how this intrinsic noise is affected, and potentially amplified, by the self-improving loop itself.
*   **Impact of Task Ordering:** Prior works typically employ a predetermined task order, often implicitly creating an easy-to-hard curriculum. This implicit curriculum might act as an unrecognized prerequisite for success, potentially leading to an overestimation of the agents' generalizability. The research aims to investigate how performance changes when tasks are presented in a random, more realistic order.
*   **Identifying Failure Mechanisms:** Beyond quantifying reliability issues, a key motivation is to understand *why* these systems exhibit fragility. This involves a detailed manual examination of agent memories to uncover the underlying causes of observed performance degradation and variance, hypothesizing that issues like environment and task underspecification play a role.
*   **Informing Future Development and Evaluation:** By systematically exposing and analyzing these fragilities, the authors aim to advocate for more rigorous evaluation protocols and inform the development of more robust self-improving agent systems and interfaces that enable effective human oversight.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond mere demonstrations of capability to a deeper understanding of the robustness and practical deployability of self-improving agents, particularly under conditions that more closely resemble real-world scenarios.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a comprehensive re-evaluation methodology for memory-based self-improving agents, broadening the scope of assessment along two primary axes: quantifying variance across multiple runs and investigating the effect of task order.

**Agent Architectures and Baseline:**
The study focuses on two representative memory-based self-improving methods:
*   **Agent Workflow Memory (AWM) [11]:** At the end of each task, a memory module summarizes reusable workflows from successful trajectories. These workflows are checked for duplication and all are included in the agent's context for subsequent tasks.
*   **ReasoningBank (RBank) [12]:** This method generates more generic reasoning traces and insights from both successful and failed trajectories. A retriever selects the most relevant past memories for future tasks.

These self-improving methods are built on top of a "no-memory baseline" agent. For WebArena and VisualWebArena, the baseline harness from [20] is used, and for SCUBA, the baseline from [15] is adopted. The underlying language model for both the agent backbone and the memory construction module is GPT-5-mini, representing an advancement over models used in original AWM and RBank implementations. Crucially, the memory construction model is provided with ground-truth rewards (r ∈ [0, 1]), rather than potentially noisy proxy rewards from LLM-Judges used in some prior works.

**Benchmarks:**
Evaluation is conducted across three web-browsing benchmarks:
*   **WebArena [13]:** Comprising 812 tasks across six domains (Shopping, Shopping Admin, GitLab, Reddit, Map, Multisite).
*   **VisualWebArena [14]:** An extension of WebArena with 910 tasks requiring visual understanding across three domains (Classifieds, Reddit, Shopping).
*   **SCUBA [15]:** An enterprise-centric benchmark with 267 tasks focused on platform administration, sales, and service on web-based CRM software. Tasks invalid due to website updates were manually excluded.

**Broadened Evaluation Axes:**

1.  **Quantifying Variance:**
    *   **Multiple Runs:** For each experimental setting, three identical runs are conducted to capture and quantify variability.
    *   **Metrics:** The primary evaluation metric is the run-level average pass rate (pass@1) per domain. Variance is characterized by reporting the standard deviation across the three runs and the absolute best-worst gap between the highest and lowest pass rates observed. This contrasts with common prior practices of reporting single-run averages.

2.  **Investigating Task Order:**
    *   **Default Order:** Experiments are initially run using the default task order (from smaller to larger task IDs) commonly adopted in prior works. The authors analyze this order for implicit curricula.
    *   **Shuffled Orders:** To stress-test the agents under more realistic, arbitrary task arrivals, experiments are repeated with two distinct randomly shuffled task orders (Shuffle-1, Shuffle-2).

**Analysis of Fragility:**

*   **Manual Memory Inspection:** To understand the root causes of observed variance and performance degradation, the authors manually inspect the memories generated by the agents (specifically RBank). This qualitative analysis aims to identify recurring patterns of problematic memory construction.
*   **Hypothesis Formulation:** Based on the manual inspection, the hypothesis is formulated that environment and task underspecification contribute to agent fragility. This includes cases where agents generate plausible but inapplicable memories (e.g., API usage in a browser-only environment) or create erroneous memories due to ambiguous task queries or evaluator bugs.
*   **Case Studies:** Specific examples are provided, such as agents recommending "API" usage, "user confirmation," or the "Haversine Formula" for map tasks, illustrating how these inappropriate memories are generated and propagated.

**Addressing Underspecification:**

To validate the underspecification hypothesis and explore mitigation strategies, the authors introduce additional context into the RBank memory construction process:
*   **Rubrics and Scores (+Rub):** Ground-truth evaluation rubrics (e.g., `must_include`, `exact_match`) are provided to the memory module during post-completion memory construction to clarify task intent. The agent itself does not see these during task execution.
*   **Environment Feedback (+Env):** Information about web environment interaction outcomes (e.g., successful/failed clicks) is fed into the memory construction.
*   **Prompt Modification (+PMod):** The memory generation prompt is revised to explicitly discourage unsupported actions (e.g., API calls, human confirmation requests) and encourage relevant knowledge (e.g., procedural knowledge, site navigation).
*   **Combined Approach (+All):** Experiments are conducted with all three modifications applied simultaneously.

These modifications are first ablated under the Shuffle-1 task order to assess individual and combined impact, then further tested under Default and Shuffle-2 orders with the combined approach.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The re-evaluation revealed significant fragility in memory-based self-improving agents, particularly regarding performance variance and sensitivity to task order.

**5.1. Variance in Agent Performance:**
*   **Intrinsic Noise in Baselines:** The no-memory baseline agent already exhibits notable variance across multiple runs. For instance, on the WebArena GitLab subset, the gap between the best and worst baseline runs was 4.4%, with a standard deviation of 1.98%. Similar best-worst gaps were observed in VisualWebArena (up to 2.4%) and SCUBA (up to 6.7%). This indicates that single-run evaluations, common in prior work, may be insufficient and potentially misleading.
*   **Amplification by Self-Improvement:** Incorporating self-improving procedures, such as AWM and RBank, generally amplifies this intrinsic variance. In approximately 71% of the evaluated cases (17 out of 24), variance increased, with 11 cases showing a relative increase exceeding 50%. For example, in WebArena GitLab, applying RBank enlarged the best-worst gap to 7.8% (from 4.4% for baseline) and the Map domain saw an 8.2% gap (from 2.75% for baseline). This amplification is attributed to the stateful nature of self-improving methods, where early stochasticity in LLM sampling and task outcomes can lead to divergent memory states across runs.
*   **Diminishing Gains with Stronger Baselines:** With a stronger baseline agent (GPT-5-mini) that achieves comparable performance to memory-enhanced agents in prior works (e.g., 55.3% on WebArena subset for baseline vs. 53.9% for RBank with Gemini-2.5-Pro), the self-improvement methods yield less consistent gains. RBank showed an average improvement of 1.5% over the baseline, but this gain had a p-value of 0.23, indicating limited statistical significance. AWM even showed slight performance degradation in some cases.

**5.2. Sensitivity to Task Order:**
*   **Implicit Easy-to-Hard Curriculum:** The default task order used in prior evaluations often exhibits an implicit easy-to-hard curriculum. Visualizations showed higher success rates for earlier tasks (e.g., 75%) which then declined for later tasks (below 40% after task ID 150) in WebArena Shopping Admin and GitLab domains. This suggests that the default ordering might inadvertently favor self-improving methods by providing simpler tasks early on for memory formation.
*   **Performance Degradation on Shuffled Orders:** When evaluated under randomly shuffled task orders (Shuffle-1, Shuffle-2), the agents' performance degraded significantly. On WebArena, RBank, which showed a 1.5% gain under the default order, experienced a 4.5% degradation on average under shuffled orders (e.g., from 56.3% to 49.8% with Shuffle-1). AWM was also significantly impacted. This indicates that the expected self-improvement is highly dependent on the structured, easy-to-hard task flow, raising concerns about reliability in real-world scenarios where tasks arrive arbitrarily.

**5.3. Underspecification as a Cause of Fragility:**
Manual inspection of agent memories identified environment and task underspecification as key drivers of fragility:
*   **Environment Underspecification:** Agents frequently generated memories recommending actions unsupported by the environment, such as "API" usage in a browser-only context or "user confirmation" in an automated evaluation. These plausible but inapplicable memories appeared across domains and could distract or confuse the agent, leading to repeated unexecutable strategies (e.g., repeatedly issuing "wait" actions).
*   **Task Underspecification:** Ambiguous task queries led to agent misunderstandings, overthinking, and the creation of irrelevant memories. An example included a medical query where the agent gave general advice instead of locating a product, then generated memories about "gathering patient details." Evaluator bugs leading to false negatives also caused agents to generate spurious memories.
*   **Contagious Memories:** The study observed instances where initially stochastic "unintended strategies" were incorporated into memory and subsequently amplified. For example, in map tasks, when the website's routing failed, the agent occasionally resorted to the "Haversine Formula" for distance estimation. When this approximation yielded a correct answer by chance, the strategy was added to memory and increasingly retrieved, despite being a less reliable or intended approach. This contributed to increased variance.

**5.4. Mitigation Efforts:**
*   **Partial Improvement with Additional Information:** Incorporating additional information during memory construction (Rubrics and Scores, Environment Feedback, Prompt Modification) yielded modest performance gains. The combined approach (+All) improved RBank performance by 2.9% under the Shuffle-1 task order (from 49.8% to 52.7%). Similar improvements were observed under Shuffle-2, while default order performance was maintained.
*   **Remaining Performance Gap:** Despite these improvements, a significant performance gap persisted. The agents with enhanced memory construction still underperformed the no-memory baseline under shuffled orders. The mitigation efforts only closed 31% of the observed performance degradation, suggesting that other uncharacterized factors contribute to the fragility.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a critical assessment of memory-based self-improving agents, highlighting their fragility under conditions that more closely resemble real-world deployments. The findings have several significant implications and potential impacts:

*   **Elevating Reliability as a Core Evaluation Metric:** The most direct impact is the strong advocacy for more rigorous evaluation protocols for self-improving AI systems. By demonstrating significant variance across runs and sensitivity to task order, the paper challenges the common practice of single-run evaluations and underscores the necessity of reporting results across multiple runs and under diverse, challenging conditions (e.g., randomized task orders). This can lead to a shift in how research in this field is conducted and presented, fostering a more robust understanding of agent capabilities.
*   **Challenging Assumptions about Self-Improvement:** The observation that agents may degrade rather than improve under random task orders, especially when built on strong baselines, challenges the intuitive notion of continuous self-improvement. This suggests that current memory-based approaches might not be robust enough for truly adaptive learning in dynamic environments. It motivates a deeper investigation into the mechanisms of effective and non-detrimental memory construction.
*   **Identifying Underspecification as a Key Challenge:** Pinpointing environment and task underspecification as a primary cause of fragility is a significant contribution. This detailed analysis provides concrete examples of how seemingly plausible but unexecutable or irrelevant memories can be generated and propagated, leading to system failures. This insight moves beyond simply observing performance drops to understanding *why* they occur.
*   **Guiding System Development:** The work motivates the development of new techniques for memory validation and refinement. The "wrong lessons learned" by agents, such as proposing API usage in a browser-only environment or using the Haversine formula instead of the map's routing engine, emphasize the need for mechanisms to filter out problematic memories and ensure long-term robustness. This could involve explicit environmental constraints, richer task specifications, or learned meta-policies for memory curation.
*   **Informing Human-Agent Interaction Design:** The inherent and often unforeseen nature of underspecification, coupled with the irreversibility of actions in real-world deployments, highlights the need for effective human oversight and intervention interfaces. Systems designers should consider how humans can timely identify and correct erroneous agent learning or behaviors before they cascade into significant failures. This could lead to research on explainable AI for agent memory, interactive debugging tools, and human-in-the-loop validation processes.
*   **Advancing AI Safety and Responsible Deployment:** By exposing these fragilities, the paper contributes to the broader discussion on AI safety and responsible deployment. Understanding the conditions under which self-improving agents might fail or behave unexpectedly is crucial for preventing unintended harms, particularly as these agents are increasingly integrated into high-stakes domains. The recommendations for stress-testing and robust evaluation are directly applicable to de-risking agent deployment.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond celebrating agent capabilities to critically examining their practical reliability, providing empirical evidence and mechanistic insights that can inform the next generation of robust and trustworthy self-improving AI systems.
