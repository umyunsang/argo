#### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of enabling AI agents to conduct scientific research autonomously over extended periods, a domain characterized by iterative cycles of exploration, experimentation, and abstraction. This endeavor integrates advancements in large language model (LLM) agents with principles of scientific methodology.

Current LLM agents have demonstrated capabilities in code editing, tool utilization, information retrieval, and executing experiments for durations ranging from hours to days. Systems such as Codex, Claude Code, and OpenHands have shown progress in modifying real codebases. However, these systems primarily focus on persistent task execution and often lack mechanisms to maintain a cohesive research state that supports cumulative hypothesis refinement and verified artifact improvement across many local attempts.

The existing landscape of autonomous research agents includes systems that automate parts of the scientific process. The AI Scientist and Agent Laboratory organize stages like idea generation, implementation, execution, and interpretation, often within a workflow. Other systems, such as AIDE and AI Scientist-v2, incorporate iterative code search or agentic tree search over research plans. Recent developments like MARS, AutoHarness, Meta-Harness, AHE, and DataMaster have expanded the scope of automated search to encompass reflective components, code harnesses, or data discovery. While these systems contribute to research automation, many still adhere to predefined workflows or concentrate on refining a single line of work, which may limit their ability to manage competing directions and integrate lessons from diverse experimental outcomes.

This research positions itself by formalizing the problem as Autonomous Optimization (AO), emphasizing the need for a persistent research state that records hypotheses, evidence, and insights. It aims to bridge the gap between persistent task execution and cumulative scientific progress by providing a general framework that enables agents to move beyond sequential local attempts toward a self-directed, evidence-structured research process. The authors contend that such a framework is necessary to automate the long-horizon work typically performed by human researchers, transforming transient experimental trials into durable knowledge that informs subsequent exploration.

#### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an AI agent capable of autonomously performing the full scientific research loop—exploration, experimentation, and abstraction—over long time horizons. This involves sustaining progress amidst uncertain hypotheses, costly experiments, failed attempts, and delayed feedback, and interpreting evidence to guide future endeavors.

The authors formalize this problem as **Autonomous Optimization (AO)**. In this setting, an AI agent is presented with an initial research artifact and a defined objective. Its task is to iteratively improve this artifact through experimental feedback, operating without step-level human supervision. This formalization highlights several motivations and challenges:

1.  **Sustained Progress over Uncertainty:** Scientific progress is not a linear process but involves navigating uncertainty. Agents need to maintain an evolving understanding of the problem, allowing each experimental attempt to reshape subsequent actions. The challenge is to enable an AI to interpret both successes and failures and use these lessons to refine future exploration, rather than treating each trial as an isolated event.
2.  **Delayed and Costly Feedback:** Research feedback is often delayed, and experiments can be resource-intensive. An agent must be able to extract meaningful information from failed or inconclusive attempts to guide later, potentially expensive, experiments.
3.  **Preventing Loss of Research Structure:** As the research horizon extends, an agent that treats each trial independently risks losing the overall structure of the research process. This necessitates a persistent research state that comprehensively records what has been attempted, the evidence obtained, and how these results should influence the generation and prioritization of future hypotheses.
4.  **Addressing Gaps in Current Agent Systems:** Existing agent systems, despite their advancements in execution autonomy, often lack a general framework for managing long-horizon autonomous research. Many follow predefined workflows or focus on refining a single line of investigation, which limits their capacity for cumulative hypothesis refinement, managing competing ideas, and transforming local experimental outcomes into reusable insights.
5.  **Establishing Core System Requirements for General AO:** To overcome these limitations, the authors identify three core requirements for a general AO system:
    *   **Branching with coherence:** The system must support the exploration of multiple competing hypotheses simultaneously, yet maintain an organized, comparable, and actionable research frontier. Unrestricted branching without structure can lead to an unmanageable log of attempts.
    *   **Global strategy with local execution:** Strategic decisions, which require an understanding of accumulated evidence, must be decoupled from the local, short-horizon tasks of implementing and debugging individual hypotheses. This separation ensures that low-level execution details do not obscure the global research state, and experimental results remain attributable to their specific hypotheses.
    *   **Exploration with held-out admission:** Development feedback should guide the iterative search for improvements. However, actual artifact-level progress should only be admitted when it is validated on a held-out evaluation set, preventing overfitting to the development environment.

The motivation for introducing Arbor is to develop a general framework that integrates these requirements, allowing an agent to form research directions, test them through concrete artifact changes, and convert the resulting evidence into actionable memory. This framework aims to make long-running experimentation a self-directed research process, moving beyond an extended sequence of independent local attempts.

#### 4. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **Arbor**, a general framework designed for Autonomous Optimization (AO), which aims to manage the research process through **Hypothesis Tree Refinement (HTR)**. This framework separates responsibilities between a long-lived coordinator and short-lived executors, using a persistent tree structure as its central research state.

**4.1. Overall Architecture**

Arbor operates with two main components:
*   **Long-lived Coordinator:** This component maintains the global research state, which is represented as a hypothesis tree. It formulates research strategy, decides which directions to pursue, and integrates results.
*   **Short-lived Executors:** These components are dispatched by the coordinator to implement and test individual hypotheses in isolated environments. They return structured evidence to the coordinator.

**4.2. Hypothesis Tree as Research State (HTR)**

The core of Arbor is the hypothesis tree, which serves as a durable record of the research process. Each node `n` in the tree is a research unit comprising three key fields: `⟨h_n, ι_n, μ_n⟩`.

*   **Hypothesis (`h_n`):** Describes a verifiable claim about how the research artifact should be modified to achieve the objective. Node depth determines granularity: root-level nodes represent broad directions, while deeper nodes specify concrete, executable interventions. This hierarchical structure organizes exploration as progressive refinement.
*   **Insight (`ι_n`):** Stores the reusable interpretation derived from the evidence associated with the hypothesis. For executed leaf nodes, it summarizes the experiment, its outcome, and why the result supports, contradicts, or constrains the hypothesis. For internal nodes, it aggregates and abstracts the insights from its children, forming a higher-level understanding of that research direction. This acts as a compact semantic memory.
*   **Metadata (`μ_n`):** Connects the hypothesis to executable evidence. It includes the node's status (pending, running, done, merged, pruned), development score, factual result record, and a git branch reference (`code_ref`). The material (codebase) itself is not stored in the tree; instead, the tree references external artifact states managed in isolated worktrees.

The hypothesis tree performs three functions: it acts as the **search frontier** (recording active, validated, or pruned directions), a **long-term memory** (storing evidence from both successes and failures), and an **auditable research record** (linking artifact changes to their motivating hypotheses and evidence).

**4.3. Hypothesis Tree Refinement Process**

The coordinator maintains the tree through a six-step iterative procedure:

1.  **Observe:** The coordinator begins each cycle by reading a structured projection of the hypothesis tree, including active frontier nodes, recent executor reports, ancestor insights, and the current best artifact (`M_best`). This step re-grounds the coordinator in the authoritative research state.
2.  **Ideate:** The coordinator selects a parent node and proposes a small set of child hypotheses based on the accumulated tree evidence. This process is constrained by validated insights (assumptions to build upon) and pruned nodes (negative constraints). Each new hypothesis is added as a pending node with specified mechanism, hypothesis, observable success conditions, and risks.
3.  **Select:** The coordinator chooses which pending nodes to execute next. Selection criteria balance expected utility, accumulated evidence from ancestors and siblings, estimated implementation cost, and diversity across active directions. The aim is not just score maximization but strategic frontier control.
4.  **Dispatch:** Selected hypotheses are sent to independent, short-lived executors. Each executor creates an isolated `git worktree` branched from the current `M_best`, implements the assigned hypothesis, evaluates the modified artifact using the development evaluator (`E_dev`), and returns a compact report.
5.  **Backpropagate:** Upon receiving executor reports, the coordinator writes the evidence (dev score, factual result, distilled insight, `code_ref`) to the corresponding leaf nodes. The distilled insight is then propagated upward, abstracting local experimental outcomes into direction-level lessons and contributing to a global understanding at the root.
6.  **Decide:** After integrating new evidence, the coordinator determines whether to continue expanding a direction, prune a falsified subtree, or attempt to merge a candidate branch. A **held-out merge gate** is applied: a candidate is evaluated on the held-out test evaluator (`E_test`) in a fresh worktree, and only if it strictly improves over `M_best` is it merged.

**4.4. Executor Functionality**

Executors are tasked with implementing and evaluating a single, fixed hypothesis. They receive the hypothesis (`h_n`), relevant ancestor insights, the current `M_best`, and `E_dev`. Each executor:
*   Creates a fresh, isolated `git worktree`.
*   Applies the minimal code changes to realize `h_n`.
*   Runs `E_dev`, diagnoses failures, and repairs its implementation within the confines of `h_n`.
*   Returns a report containing the dev score, factual result, distilled insight, and the `git` branch reference (`b_n`). The executor is prohibited from altering the assigned hypothesis or using `E_test`.

**4.5. Experimental Setup**

Arbor was evaluated across two main types of benchmarks:
*   **AO Task Suite:** Six real-world research tasks across model training (Optimizer Design, Architecture Design), harness engineering (Terminal-Bench 2.0, BrowseComp), and data synthesis (Search-Agent Data Synthesis, Math-Reasoning Data Synthesis). Each task provided an initial artifact, natural-language objective, task-native metric, and a dev/test protocol.
*   **MLE-Bench Lite:** A long-horizon machine learning engineering benchmark for comparison against established systems.

Baselines included strong coding agents like Codex (GPT-5.5) and Claude Code (Claude Opus 4.6) for the AO tasks, and various reported systems for MLE-Bench Lite. Performance was measured using native task metrics and a normalized held-out improvement for cross-task comparison, with stochastic methods run three times to report Avg@3. Both coordinator and executors primarily utilized Claude Opus 4.6 (with GPT-5.5 and Gemini-3-Flash for backbone studies) and operated under a 48-hour wall-clock limit, with a default of 20 coordinator cycles and a maximum tree depth of 2.

#### 5. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Arbor on both the custom AO task suite and the MLE-Bench Lite benchmark yielded several findings regarding its performance and the efficacy of its underlying design principles.

**5.1. Performance on Real Research Tasks (AO Task Suite)**

Arbor achieved the best held-out results across all six custom AO tasks, covering diverse domains including model training, harness engineering, and data synthesis. This performance indicated the framework's generality, as the same controller and hypothesis-tree depth were applied uniformly, with only the initial material and evaluator specific to each task.

*   **Relative Gains:** Arbor demonstrated more than 2.5 times the average relative held-out gain compared to the Codex and Claude Code baselines, which operated under the same task interface and resource budget.
    *   For instance, on **BrowseComp** (harness engineering), Arbor improved held-out accuracy from 45.33% to 67.67%, while Codex and Claude Code reached 50.00% and 53.33%, respectively.
    *   On **Math-Reasoning Data Synthesis**, Arbor improved the held-out pass-gap by 19.79 points, whereas Codex achieved 5.21 points and Claude Code achieved 7.29 points.
*   **Dev/Test Split Effectiveness:** The evaluation protocol, which included a strict dev/test split, exposed instances of overfitting by baselines. For example, on **Terminal-Bench**, Claude Code achieved the highest development score (75.00%) but experienced a drop to 71.70% on the held-out test set. Arbor, with a slightly lower development score (72.22%), achieved a higher held-out score of 77.36%. This highlights the role of Arbor's held-out merge gate in distinguishing exploratory success from verified, transferable artifact improvement.

**5.2. Performance on MLE-Bench Lite**

Arbor's framework was also applied to MLE-Bench Lite, a long-horizon machine learning engineering benchmark.

*   **Gemini-3-Flash Backbone:** Using a Gemini-3-Flash backbone, Arbor achieved a 100% valid submission rate, 86.36% above-median rate, and 81.82% Any Medal rate, matching the best recorded Any Medal result for the same backbone while exhibiting a higher gold medal rate than comparable systems.
*   **GPT-5.5 Backbone:** When configured with a GPT-5.5 backbone (without altering the controller, depth, scheduler, or adapter), Arbor further improved to 86.36% Any Medal and 77.27% gold medal rate, representing the highest values among the compared methods on this benchmark. These results suggest the hypothesis-tree organization's transferability to established ML engineering benchmarks.

**5.3. Backbone Generality and Cross-Task Idea Transfer**

*   **Backbone Agnosticism:** Arbor demonstrated functional generality across different backbone models (Gemini-3-Flash, Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.5). While final performance was influenced by the fit between the model's capabilities and task requirements (e.g., Claude Opus for broad reasoning tasks like BrowseComp, GPT-5.5 for ML-engineering knowledge in MLE-Bench Lite), the HTR framework provided a consistent structure for exploration and memory.
*   **Transferability of Learned Artifacts:** A search harness optimized by Arbor on the **BrowseComp** task showed positive transfer to two unseen search-agent tasks, HLE and DeepSearchQA, without further task-specific optimization. The frozen codebase improved HLE accuracy from 25.50% to 31.50% and DeepSearchQA accuracy from 61.00% to 69.00%. This indicates that Arbor can discover generalizable design changes rather than solely optimizing for the source benchmark.

**5.4. Ablation Studies**

Ablations on MLE-Bench Lite explored the impact of the hierarchical hypothesis tree and insight feedback:

*   **Impact on Refinement:** All variants maintained a 100% valid submission rate, indicating that HTR's contribution is primarily to research refinement quality rather than basic executability.
*   **Importance of Tree Structure and Insights:** The full Arbor system achieved 81.82% Any Medal. A variant without the tree structure (reduced to a flat experiment queue) dropped to 63.64% Any Medal. A variant retaining the tree but disabling upward insight propagation performed even lower at 54.54% Any Medal. These results suggest that both the hierarchical organization and the propagation of distilled insights are critical, and that hierarchy alone is insufficient without the semantic memory provided by insight accumulation.

**5.5. Token Consumption and Search Cost**

Arbor's total token consumption (20.12M–43.19M tokens across six completed tasks) was found to be comparable to that of single-trajectory baselines. Despite this, Arbor achieved larger held-out gains, suggesting that its improvements stemmed from the structured organization of the search budget—maintaining competing hypotheses, isolated executions, and evidence comparison—rather than simply using a substantially larger token budget. Analysis of node statistics showed that many development-improving nodes were not merged, affirming the filtering role of the held-out admission gate.

**5.6. Analysis of Internal Research Traces**

*   **Hypothesis Refinement:** Analysis of the BrowseComp run showed a deepening of task understanding. Early nodes tested broad mechanisms, later nodes localized bottlenecks, and ancestor insights compressed results into constraints that shaped subsequent proposals. This suggested that HTR fosters cumulative constraint accumulation, reducing the arbitrariness of later search.
*   **Search Efficiency:** Strong candidates frequently appeared in the middle or later stages of a run, after the search state had accumulated constraints from earlier experiments. This indicated that later proposals were more targeted and conditioned on prior evidence.
*   **Idea Quality:** Most effective ideas generated by Arbor were local, executable, and evidence-conditioned, often directly responding to prior observations or failure modes (e.g., the BrowseComp evidence-dossier design evolving from verifier limitations). This conversion of local failures into design constraints contributed to more precise hypotheses. The authors also noted that high-level problem reformulation beyond concrete refinements still depended on human-provided task design.

#### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Arbor as a functional framework for Autonomous Optimization (AO), a formalized class of long-horizon research tasks. The core contribution is the Hypothesis Tree Refinement (HTR) mechanism, which establishes a persistent and operational research state for AI agents. This design allows for cumulative progress in autonomous research, moving beyond sequential, isolated attempts.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1.  **Advancing Autonomous Research Capabilities:** Arbor demonstrates that AI agents can manage complex, long-horizon research processes by maintaining a structured, auditable record of hypotheses, experimental evidence, and distilled insights. This represents a step towards more generalist AI agents capable of scientific discovery.
2.  **Addressing Key Challenges in AI Agents:** The framework directly tackles issues such as managing uncertainty, integrating lessons from failures, preventing overfitting to development environments through a held-out merge gate, and maintaining research coherence across diverse experimental directions.
3.  **Empirical Validation of Structured Search:** The consistent outperformance of Arbor on a novel AO task suite, alongside strong results on the established MLE-Bench Lite benchmark, provides empirical evidence for the value of evidence-structured research processes over simpler iterative trial-and-error approaches.
4.  **Enabling Generalizable Improvements:** The cross-task transfer results, where a harness optimized on one benchmark improved performance on unseen tasks, suggest that Arbor can facilitate the discovery of genuinely useful and generalizable artifact improvements, rather than merely optimizing for specific benchmark idiosyncrasies.
5.  **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of Arbor as an open-source system and the introduction of a new AO task suite provide valuable resources for the research community, enabling further development and more rigorous evaluations of autonomous research agents.
6.  **Insights into AI Research Processes:** The detailed analysis of Arbor's internal traces offers insights into how autonomous research progresses, highlighting the evolution of hypothesis refinement, the efficiency gains from accumulated constraints, and the nature of ideas generated by such systems. This understanding can inform the design of future AI research tools.

While acknowledging limitations such as the scope of evaluation, the scalar nature of objectives, and reliance on underlying LLM capabilities, this work contributes to the foundational understanding and practical implementation of AI systems capable of self-directed scientific inquiry and engineering. Its impact could extend to accelerating scientific discovery, streamlining ML engineering workflows, and fostering the development of more robust and intelligent AI systems.
