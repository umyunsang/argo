## Research Report: Dive into Claude Code: The Design Space of Today’s and Future AI Agent Systems

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the evolving field of AI-assisted software development, which has progressed from basic autocomplete tools like GitHub Copilot to integrated development environment (IDE) assistants such as Cursor, and now to autonomous agentic systems capable of planning, executing, and iterating on multi-step modifications, shell commands, and file operations. Claude Code, an agentic coding tool developed by Anthropic, represents a production-grade example of this advanced generation of AI agents.

Previous academic and industry research on AI agents has frequently focused on performance benchmarks (e.g., SWE-Bench, HumanEval), the development of agent orchestration frameworks (e.g., LangGraph, AutoGen), or product-centric descriptions of user functionalities. This paper distinguishes itself by offering a source-grounded architectural analysis of a commercial agentic system, Claude Code, rather than merely evaluating its performance or describing its user-facing features. By examining the publicly available TypeScript source code, the authors aim to reverse-engineer and document the design decisions and underlying motivations of a system deployed in a real-world context.

The study contributes to the broader understanding of the design space for AI agent systems by providing a detailed case study of how one prominent system addresses recurring architectural questions, particularly in areas such as safety, context management, extensibility, and delegation. Furthermore, by contrasting Claude Code’s architecture with two independent open-source agent systems—OpenClaw (a multi-channel personal assistant gateway) and Hermes Agent (a single-process, multi-surface assistant)—the research illuminates how different deployment contexts and product goals lead to diverse architectural solutions for similar design challenges. This comparative approach helps to identify both common design principles across agent systems and critical divergences. The paper also extends the discussion to encompass the long-term implications of agent autonomy, including the impact on human developer capabilities and emerging governance considerations, thereby positioning itself at the intersection of AI engineering, software architecture, and human-AI interaction research.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the shift in AI-assisted software development from assistive tools to autonomous agentic systems. This paradigm shift, exemplified by Claude Code, introduces complex architectural requirements that are not adequately addressed by existing documentation or research focusing solely on performance metrics. While Anthropic provides user-facing documentation for Claude Code, detailed architectural descriptions are not publicly available. Understanding the internal workings of such a system is critical because, as indicated by Anthropic’s internal surveys, architectures like Claude Code enable "qualitatively new workflows," with approximately 27% of tasks reportedly undertaken that would not have been attempted without the tool. This suggests that the architectural design significantly influences user behavior and the scope of achievable tasks.

The study establishes three key objectives:

1.  **Design-Space Analysis of Claude Code**: The first objective is to provide a detailed, source-level analysis of Claude Code's architecture. This involves identifying recurring design questions that any production coding agent must address—such as how reasoning is structured, how iteration loops are designed, the approach to safety, the partitioning of extension surfaces, context management strategies, mechanisms for subagent delegation, and methods for session persistence. The research aims to trace Claude Code’s specific answers to these questions through its seven-component high-level structure and five-layer subsystem architecture, linking design choices to particular source files. The ultimate goal of this deep dive is to build a foundational understanding of system mechanisms to inform the design of future, more capable agent systems.

2.  **Architectural Contrast with Open-Source Agents**: The second objective is to compare Claude Code's design philosophy and architectural choices with those of two independent open-source AI agent systems: OpenClaw and Hermes Agent. These systems, while addressing similar design challenges, operate within different deployment contexts (e.g., a multi-channel personal assistant gateway versus a single-process, multi-surface assistant). By contrasting these systems across six key design dimensions, the authors seek to highlight how product goals, deployment settings, safety requirements, and user assumptions shape architectural decisions differently in commercial versus open-source software. This comparison aims to offer practical insights and guidance for future agent system designers by illustrating common principles and key divergences.

3.  **Identification of Open Directions for Future Agent Systems**: Building upon the detailed analysis of Claude Code and the comparative study, the third objective is to identify and articulate six open design directions for future agent systems. These directions span critical areas such as the observability-evaluation gap, cross-session persistence, the evolution of harness boundaries, horizon scaling, governance, and the long-term impact on developer capability. This objective seeks to ground these future considerations in empirical, architectural, and policy literature, including the "paradox of supervision" concern where over-reliance on AI might atrophy human skills. The paper explicitly addresses how Claude Code’s architecture, while amplifying short-term capabilities, offers limited mechanisms for explicitly supporting long-term human understanding and sustained codebase coherence.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology combining detailed source code analysis, architectural decomposition, and comparative study.

1.  **Source Code Analysis**: The primary methodology involves a deep analysis of the publicly available TypeScript source code of Claude Code (v2.1.88). This low-level, code-verified approach allows for the identification of specific design choices, subsystem boundaries, and implementation trade-offs directly from the system's actual construction, rather than inferring them from high-level product descriptions. The analysis is supplemented by information from official Anthropic documentation and selected community analyses to contextualize code-level observations with design intent and user behavior.

2.  **Analytical Framework**:
    *   **Value-Driven Design**: The analysis is framed by identifying five human values and philosophies that motivate Claude Code's architecture: human decision authority, safety, security, and privacy, reliable execution, capability amplification, and contextual adaptability. These values, derived from Anthropic's public statements and empirical findings, serve as a lens through which architectural decisions are interpreted.
    *   **Design Principles**: Thirteen design principles are identified as operationalizations of these values, each addressing a recurring question in agent system design (e.g., "Deny-first with human escalation," "Context as scarce resource with progressive management"). These principles connect the abstract values to concrete architectural choices.
    *   **Hierarchical Decomposition**: The architecture is systematically decomposed into:
        *   A **seven-component high-level structure**: User, Interfaces, Agent loop, Permission system, Tools, State & Persistence, and Execution Environment. This provides a functional overview.
        *   A **five-layer subsystem architecture**: Surface, Core, Safety/Action, State, and Backend layers. This offers a finer-grained view, mapping each layer to specific source directories and responsibilities.

3.  **Running Example**: To maintain concreteness throughout the architectural description, the task "Fix the failing test in auth.test.ts" is used as a running example. This task is traced through various architectural layers, illustrating how user requests activate tool invocations, permission checks, context selection, iterative repair, delegation, and session persistence.

4.  **Comparative Analysis**: The study extends its scope beyond Claude Code by conducting a structured comparison with two architecturally independent open-source AI agent systems: OpenClaw (a multi-channel personal assistant gateway) and Hermes Agent (a single-process, multi-surface assistant). This comparison is performed across six dimensions: system scope and deployment model, trust model and security architecture, agent runtime and tool orchestration, extension architecture, memory and context management, and multi-agent architecture and routing. This comparative approach aims to highlight how the same fundamental design questions can yield different architectural answers based on deployment context and product objectives.

5.  **Evidence Tiers**: Claims made in the paper are categorized into three evidence tiers: Tier A (product-documented, from official Anthropic sources), Tier B (code-verified, directly from source files), and Tier C (reconstructed, from community analysis or inference), enhancing the transparency and credibility of the findings.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of Claude Code's architecture reveals a system designed around a core philosophy of enabling model autonomy within a robust deterministic operational harness, supported by layered mechanisms for safety, context management, and extensibility.

**Core Architecture and Design Philosophy:**
The system’s core is a simple `while-loop` implemented as an `AsyncGenerator` named `queryLoop()` in `query.ts`. This loop iteratively calls the model, dispatches tool requests, and processes results. The research highlights that an estimated 1.6% of Claude Code’s codebase constitutes AI decision logic, with the remaining 98.4% comprising operational infrastructure. This ratio indicates a deliberate architectural choice to invest in deterministic infrastructure rather than extensive decision scaffolding.

**Safety and Permissions:**
Claude Code adopts a "deny-first with human escalation" safety posture. This involves seven independent layers of protection: tool pre-filtering, deny-first rule evaluation, permission mode constraints, an ML-based auto-mode classifier (`yoloClassifier.ts`), optional shell sandboxing, non-restoration of session-scoped permissions upon resume, and hook-based interception. The system features a "graduated trust spectrum" with seven distinct permission modes, ranging from `plan` (requiring explicit user approval for plans) to `bypassPermissions` (minimal prompting). A significant finding is the identification of vulnerabilities (CVE-2025-59536, CVE-2026-21852) where initializations (hooks, MCP servers) execute *before* the interactive trust dialog, creating a pre-trust execution window outside the main deny-first pipeline.

**Context Management:**
The context window is identified as the primary binding resource constraint. Claude Code manages this through a "progressive management" strategy, employing a five-layer compaction pipeline (`services/compact/`) that executes before every model call: budget reduction, snip, microcompact, context collapse, and auto-compact. Memory is managed through a "transparent file-based configuration" approach, utilizing a four-level `CLAUDE.md` hierarchy (`utils/claudemd.ts`) and auto-memory, which are inspectable and version-controllable plain-text files. `CLAUDE.md` content is delivered as user context (a user message) rather than system prompt content, implying probabilistic model compliance.

**Extensibility:**
The system features "composable multi-mechanism extensibility" through four distinct mechanisms, each with varying context costs:
*   **MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers**: The primary path for external tool integration, contributing tool definitions to the model’s flat tool pool.
*   **Plugins**: A packaging and distribution mechanism for multiple component types (commands, agents, skills, hooks, MCP servers, etc.).
*   **Skills**: Defined by `SKILL.md` files, injecting domain-specific instructions into the context with minimal context cost.
*   **Hooks**: 27 event types (`types/hooks.ts`) for lifecycle interception, allowing modification, blocking, or annotation of tool requests without direct context consumption by default. Five of these are directly related to the permission flow.

**Subagent Delegation and Orchestration:**
Claude Code implements "isolated subagent boundaries" via the `AgentTool` (`AgentTool.tsx`), which spawns child agents (e.g., Explore, Plan, General, Custom) to perform delegated tasks. These subagents operate in isolated context windows with their own rebuilt permission contexts and tool sets. Isolation modes include `worktree` (Git-based filesystem separation) and `in-process`. Subagents return only summary text to the parent conversation, conserving the parent’s context window.

**Session Persistence and Recovery:**
Persistence follows an "append-only durable state" principle. Session transcripts are stored as mostly append-only JSONL files (`utils/sessionStorage.ts`), which are human-readable and auditable. Global user prompt history is maintained in `history.jsonl`, and subagent histories are stored as separate sidechain files. Notably, `resume` and `fork` operations do not restore session-scoped permissions, requiring re-granting for security. Compaction mechanisms (`compact_boundary` markers) enable read-time chain patching, preserving full history for auditing without destructive edits.

**Comparative Analysis Findings:**
The comparison with OpenClaw and Hermes Agent reveals that while all three systems address similar design questions, their answers diverge significantly based on their deployment contexts:
*   **Trust Model**: Claude Code emphasizes per-action, layered safety with an ML classifier; OpenClaw focuses on perimeter-level access control for a trusted operator; Hermes Agent uses per-action approval rendered across multiple surfaces, with an auxiliary-LLM for "smart" approvals.
*   **Agent Runtime**: Claude Code's `queryLoop()` is the central `AsyncGenerator`; OpenClaw embeds an SDK runner within a gateway RPC dispatch; Hermes Agent uses a synchronous `while` loop with thread-managed concurrency.
*   **Extension Focus**: Claude Code's mechanisms extend a single agent's context and tool surface; OpenClaw's plugins extend the gateway's capabilities across all agents; Hermes Agent adds pluggable memory and model provider backends alongside per-agent extensions.
*   **Multi-Agent**: Claude Code uses task-delegating subagents with worktree isolation; OpenClaw differentiates between multi-agent routing (independent instances) and sub-agent delegation; Hermes Agent combines per-turn child agents with a SQLite-backed Kanban system for multi-process worker coordination.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a detailed, source-grounded architectural analysis of Claude Code, a production-grade AI coding agent. Its significance and potential impact span several dimensions:

1.  **Architectural Blueprint and Guidance for Agent Builders**: The study offers a comprehensive blueprint of a real-world agent system, moving beyond theoretical frameworks to document concrete design choices and their implementations. This serves as a valuable case study and practical guide for developers and researchers building future agent systems, particularly highlighting the efficacy of investing in robust, deterministic operational infrastructure (like permission systems, context management, and recovery mechanisms) over extensive decision scaffolding for increasingly capable models.

2.  **Illuminating Design Space and Trade-offs**: By systematically dissecting Claude Code's answers to recurring design questions and contrasting them with OpenClaw and Hermes Agent, the research delineates the diverse design space of AI agent systems. It clearly illustrates how product goals, deployment contexts, and inherent value tensions (e.g., safety vs. autonomy, context efficiency vs. transparency) drive different architectural trade-offs. This understanding can inform more intentional and context-aware architectural decisions in future agent development.

3.  **Advancing Understanding of AI Agent Safety**: The detailed analysis of Claude Code's layered safety architecture, including the deny-first principle, ML-based classification, and shell sandboxing, contributes to the field's understanding of how to build robust safety mechanisms into autonomous agents. The identification of temporal vulnerabilities during initialization, where extensibility mechanisms could operate before full safety enforcement, offers critical insights for designing more resilient security postures.

4.  **Informing Future Research Directions**: The paper identifies six key open design directions, grounded in literature, that are crucial for the evolution of AI agent systems:
    *   **Addressing the Observability-Evaluation Gap**: This calls for new architectural solutions within the harness to detect and surface "silent failures" beyond mere crashes, potentially requiring novel evaluation interfaces and scaffolding.
    *   **Developing Cross-Session Persistence**: The need for durable, inspectable memory structures that transcend single sessions and support longitudinal human-agent relationships, perhaps incorporating learned strategies and shared organizational context, is highlighted.
    *   **Evolving Harness Boundaries**: Future agents will require more dynamic and adaptable harnesses that redefine *where* (virtualized components), *when* (proactive action), *what* (multimodal actions), and *with whom* (advanced multi-agent coordination) they operate. The emergence of "orchestration-as-code" (dynamic workflows) illustrates this shift.
    *   **Scaling for Long-Horizon Dependability**: The current session-bounded architectures need to evolve to support tasks spanning weeks or months, requiring new coordination primitives beyond turns, sessions, and subagents.
    *   **Navigating Governance and Oversight**: As AI regulation matures, agent architectures will need to expose new interfaces for logging, transparency, risk management, and human oversight to comply with external constraints.
    *   **Prioritizing Long-term Developer Capability**: The paper's most significant forward-looking contribution is framing the potential erosion of human understanding and skill atrophy due to AI over-reliance as a "first-class design problem." This calls for architectural mechanisms that explicitly preserve and enhance long-term human learning and codebase coherence, rather than merely amplifying short-term productivity.

5.  **Highlighting Compositional Systems**: The comparative analysis demonstrates that different agent systems can be composable (e.g., via ACP), implying a layered and interoperable design space for AI agents, where specialized components can be integrated to form more complex ecosystems.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond superficial descriptions to offer a foundational understanding of how a leading production AI coding agent is architected. Its findings and identified future directions are poised to influence the design, development, and responsible deployment of next-generation AI agent systems, particularly in the critical domain of software engineering.
