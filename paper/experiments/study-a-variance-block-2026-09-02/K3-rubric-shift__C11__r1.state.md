# Research state (fill every field before writing the design)

decision_id: rubric-shift-effect-detection
question: Does a rubric edit change systematic agreement between human raters and an automatic rater, or only inject variance?
alternatives:            
  - Reject-pair design: Score each item twice with Rubric A and once with Rubric B on different subsets, then use cross-dataset comparison. (Rejected: loses pairing efficiency; cannot separate severity shift from agreement change; low power per item; conflates inter-rubric with inter-subset variance.)
  - Single-call LLM rater with kappa-only analysis: Rate all items once under each rubric, report Cohen's kappa or Fleiss' kappa as the sole agreement metric. (Rejected: kappa is location-blind—two raters can differ greatly in severity but report identical kappa; cannot decompose rater effects; cannot distinguish signal from noise across rubric variants.)
  - Human-only analysis (no automatic rater): Rate items under both rubrics with trained humans only, test if human inter-rater agreement changes. (Rejected: cannot answer the specific question about automatic rater agreement; human raters are expensive; question is framed around automatic rater behavior.)

sampling_frame: The population is the set of scoreable items under both rubric variants. The unit of observation is (item, human rater, automatic rater, rubric variant, replicate). Sampling design: a fixed set of N items (concrete: those items for which human ratings already exist and are available for reuse), each rated by the same pool of human raters under rubric A and rubric B (paired on items, independent on rubric), each paired rating episode scored by the automatic rater under that rubric. Each (item, human, rubric) tuple is replicated K times by the automatic rater (K=3 or 5) to separate within-rater variance from systematic effects. Human ratings are singleton (no replication needed for humans).

evidence_used:
  - "2605.30315": Paired resolution targets and minimum detectable effect. Used for: paired designs are 2-3× more efficient than unpaired on the same N items; resolution ratio q=N/N* determines adequacy. Citation: Kotawala (2026), ICML Workshop, shows that paired McNemar required-N is median 2.15× smaller than unpaired Gaussian formula; this efficiency carries to paired rater-agreement designs.
  - "2608.29517": Judge severity, halo, and drift. Used for: LLM judges differ in severity (up to 219 points on 0–1000 scale); version contrasts shift severity significantly; kappa is location-blind (two judges can have identical kappa but differ vastly in severity). MFRM (many-facet Rasch) and generalizability theory decompose rater effects. Citation: Sunkavalli (2026), LAK27 track, finds judge severity SD is 8–15× that of trained humans; all five version contrasts shift severity beyond permutation null (up to 133 points).
  - "2607.13304": Variance components for repeat allocation. Used for: G-theory decision studies project reliability of alternative designs; within-facet replication buys stability but not necessarily accuracy; variance partitioning shows which facets matter most. Citation: Ẑatuchin (2026) shows that spreading query budget across languages/models reduces variance more per unit cost than replication; a repeat past k=5 reduces relative error variance by only 0.0003.
  - "2010.06595": Statistical power norms. Used for: power analysis is critical; most NLP evaluation studies are underpowered; typical test sets are too small to detect small differences. Citation: Card et al. (2020, 2010) benchmark NLP power and recommend power calculations as routine.
  - "2608.03501": Stage isolation and redline scoring in experiment design. Used for: stage isolation (separating rubric design from rater behavior from statistical analysis) improves design quality; ablation experiments (e.g., rubric dimensions) provide evidence for which changes matter. Citation: Liu et al. (2026) report that OptED, using stage isolation and tool augmentation, resolves configuration bottlenecks that plague multi-factor designs.
  - "2606.07591": Hidden-target task packaging and rubric limits. Used for: rubric decomposition and weighting of sub-criteria matter; open-ended outputs require expert-curated rubrics; scoring rubrics have limits on granularity and can hide failures. Citation: ResearchClawBench uses expert-curated multimodal rubrics; scores above 50 indicate discovery level; rubric limits are acknowledged.
  - "2609.00038": Outcome-only judging blind spots. Used for: outcome-only scoring misses silent faults; step-by-step rubrics catch failures that aggregate metrics hide. Citation: Mohammadi (2026) shows outcome-only judge catches 84% of loud faults but only 45% of silent ones; step-rubric judge reaches 77% recall with zero false alarms at higher cost. Implication: rubric granularity matters for detecting real changes.

falsifier: If the automatic rater's agreement with humans is identical under both rubric variants (Pearson r, quadratically weighted kappa, or tau correlation all fall within the 95% CI of each other), and the automatic rater's severity (mean score, or MFRM severity parameter) does not shift beyond the permutation threshold, then the rubric edit changed neither signal nor systematic noise, and the observed variance differences are consistent with random sampling variation. The design would fail to refute this if the study is underpowered (q < 1) or if severity measurement is insensitive (e.g., single call, no replication).

stopping_rule: Collect ratings until the resolution ratio q ≥ 1 for the primary paired-agreement hypothesis test (paired t-test on agreement correlations between rubric A and B), OR until practical constraints (time, human-rater availability) force a fixed stopping point. Secondary stopping: if severity-shift permutation test reaches family-wise α=0.05 before agreement test is resolved, report both; do not stop early on severity alone. Tertiary: if ablation (e.g., one rubric dimension held constant) shows zero effect on agreement, investigate which sub-dimensions matter and document findings; do not claim rubric effect is zero, only narrow the culprit.
