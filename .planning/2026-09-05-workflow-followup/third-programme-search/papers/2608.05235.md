### Research Report: Auditable Experimental Records for Industrial Research Agents

#### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The paper's authors are Zijie Zhuang, Changxin Lao, Pengbo Xu, Hanwen Xu, Ruochen Yang, Yingzhi He, Peng Zhang, Jiangxia Cao, Yusheng Huang, Guohong Mu, Jian Liang, Ruiming Tang, Shuang Yang, Zhaojie Liu, Wenwu Ou, and Kun Gai. All authors are affiliated with Kuaishou Technology in Beijing, China.

#### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a critical challenge in the evolving landscape of automated machine learning (ML) experimentation and research agents. Modern research agents are increasingly capable of automating complex, multi-round scientific workflows, including proposing interventions, modifying code, executing experiments, interpreting results, and revising actions. This iterative process generates extensive "experimental trajectories" comprising proposals, code states, execution logs, measurements, failures, and revisions.

Existing research in this area, exemplified by systems such as MLAgentBench, PaperBench, The AI Scientist, and Agent Laboratory, focuses on establishing the task-level capabilities of agents in executing and reproducing research workflows. These systems demonstrate an agent's ability to perform specific research tasks but do not explicitly address the subsequent process of determining which findings from these workflows are robust enough to be considered reliable, persistent knowledge beyond the immediate task.

Concurrently, there is a growing body of work on equipping language agents with memory and experience reuse mechanisms. Systems like Reflexion, ExpeL, and Agent Workflow Memory store prior experiences or induce workflows from trajectories to improve future actions. In industrial settings, agent systems for recommendation, such as Self-Evolving Recommendation System and NOVA, retain experimental history for continued optimization and hypothesis generation, sometimes incorporating semantic verification. While these approaches leverage past trajectories to guide subsequent actions or ensure traceability (e.g., ModelDB, MLflow), they largely leave the *qualification* of persistent experimental claims implicit. Lineage tracking, for instance, records *what* was done but does not inherently validate *what* an experiment genuinely supports as evidence.

This paper positions itself by identifying a gap: the conversion of an accumulated experimental trajectory, which merely records actions and events, into actual, verifiable evidence that supports specific claims. It differentiates itself by focusing on explicit "trajectory-to-evidence conversion" to consolidate evidence at the intervention-claim level, preserving only findings that are supported under explicit conditions as auditable records, rather than relying solely on feedback to improve agent actions or maintaining raw historical data.

#### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to formulate and address the problem of "trajectory-to-evidence conversion" in the context of industrial machine learning research agents. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current automated experimental processes:

1.  **Lack of Automatic Evidence Conversion:** While research agents generate multi-round ML experiments and accumulate trajectories, a completed trajectory does not automatically constitute reliable evidence. The paper argues that generated artifacts may be unsupported or incomplete, executed rounds might be invalid or confounded, and later modifications can obscure earlier, potentially valuable findings. This necessitates a structured approach to ascertain what a completed research process has genuinely established.

2.  **Unreliability of LLM-Generated Content:** Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used by research agents to generate proposals, implementations, and summaries. However, LLM outputs can contain unsupported content, hallucinations, or omit information critical for downstream steps. This inherent unreliability poses a risk to the integrity of the experimental process if not addressed proactively.

3.  **Challenges in Experimental Validity and Attribution:** The successful execution of an experiment does not inherently guarantee that the intended intervention was faithfully realized, that the comparison was valid, or that any observed change is genuinely attributable to the intervention. This can lead to misleading conclusions if not rigorously verified.

4.  **Non-Monotonicity of Trajectory Evolution:** Experimental trajectories are not always monotonic; they may contain abandoned hypotheses, retries, failures, and later modifications that can sometimes erase previously observed gains or lead to suboptimal final states. Relying solely on the final state of a trajectory as experimental knowledge can lead to overlooking valuable intermediate findings or adopting less effective solutions.

5.  **Limitations of Existing Memory Systems:** Current agent memory and feedback systems primarily focus on improving subsequent actions or providing traceability. They generally lack a mechanism for explicit qualification of persistent experimental claims, meaning that findings are not systematically assessed for their robustness, generalizability, or explicit applicability conditions before being retained for future use.

To address these issues, the paper sets out to:
*   **Formulate the problem:** Clearly define trajectory-to-evidence conversion as the challenge of identifying which intervention-level claims are sufficiently supported by evidence distributed across research-agent trajectories.
*   **Introduce a comprehensive framework:** Develop a system that combines bounded verification of consequential artifacts with post-execution claim qualification. This framework aims to ensure that only supported findings, complete with explicit provenance and applicability boundaries, are preserved as auditable records.
*   **Identify bottlenecks:** Through systematic, stage-wise analysis, characterize the challenges and limitations in the proposed framework, particularly concerning record construction and downstream reuse, such as non-monotonic adaptation and the reliability of applicability judgments.

#### 4. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for trajectory-to-evidence conversion is structured into three main stages: Source Adaptation, Claim Qualification, and Downstream Reuse.

**Framework Overview:**
During **Source Adaptation**, a research agent generates proposals, code changes, experiment specifications, and result summaries. A verification harness checks each consequential output before it progresses. In **Claim Qualification**, executed rounds are grouped, and evidence is consolidated to determine which claims are supported, under what conditions, and then converted into auditable records. In the **Downstream Reuse** stage, a controller evaluates these frozen records for applicability to new target tasks, making decisions to apply, defer, or reject them.

**Problem Setting:**
The framework is applied to "paper-to-target adaptation" tasks, where a research agent adapts a published paper's defining mechanism to a specific target setting (repository, data, evaluation, constraints). The agent is limited to a budget of `B` machine-executed experiments. Crucially, the system aims to build claims from *evidence across rounds* rather than relying solely on individual rounds or the final state. Records are defined with five semantic blocks: source context, intervention, measured outcome, supporting evidence, and applicability boundary.

**Per-Step Verification Harness:**
This component operates at consequential handoffs in the workflow (e.g., paper-to-proposal, proposal-to-code). Its purpose is to check artifacts for potential issues before they influence downstream reasoning or consume significant resources.
1.  **Generate-Verify-Repair Loop:** The producer samples an initial artifact. Before release, this artifact undergoes two checks. If issues are found, the producer revises the artifact, and the checks are repeated for up to `K_t` repair attempts. If the artifact still fails, the process branch may be stopped.
2.  **Evidence and Constraint Check:** An "evidence-and-constraint verifier" receives the step input, the candidate artifact, and available evidence. It identifies localized objections if the artifact conflicts with evidence, contains unsupported statements, inconsistent actions, or violates constraints.
3.  **Downstream Requirement Check:** A separate "requirement reviewer" independently reconstructs the information required by the next step's input contract, without accessing the candidate artifact itself. It then compares these requirements with the candidate artifact to identify missing information. This design ensures that the completeness criterion is independent of the artifact being checked.
4.  **Context Isolation:** The producer, verifier, and reviewer operate in separate contexts, communicating only through typed artifacts and explicit issue sets. This prevents rationale sharing that might introduce correlated blind spots.

**Experimental Claim Qualification:**
After artifacts pass pre-execution verification and experiments are dispatched, this stage assesses intervention effectiveness.
1.  **Execution Validity:** An experiment is deemed "execution-valid" if it passes four gates: implementation fidelity (code realizes intervention), execution completion, adherence to the evaluation protocol, and preservation of the paper's defining mechanism. Runs failing these gates are retained for diagnostics but excluded from persistent claims.
2.  **Claim Attribution:** Separate from execution validity, attribution assesses if an observed effect is genuinely caused by the intervention and bounded to specific tested conditions.
3.  **Qualification Labeling:** Related execution-valid rounds are grouped, and based on their evidence (code changes, logs, measurements, comparisons), the candidate claim is labeled:
    *   **Repair:** Supported by execution-valid and attributable evidence for an implementable intervention, with explicit trigger and applicability boundary.
    *   **Guard:** Supported by attributable negative or null results, establishing a bounded failure mode under explicit conditions.
    *   **Withheld:** If evidence is insufficient, confounded, inconsistent, or an explicit trigger/boundary cannot be established.
Only Repair and Guard claims become auditable records, containing details on intervention, outcome, provenance, and applicability boundaries.

**Downstream Use of Experimental Records:**
1.  **Controller Decisions:** For a new target adaptation task, a hybrid LLM-assisted controller queries the frozen record registry. It combines deterministic checks with an LLM-based semantic assessment to decide whether to "Apply," "Defer," or "Reject" each candidate record based on its compatibility with the target mechanism, conditions, and observations.
2.  **Actionable Outcomes:** An "Apply" decision for a Repair record activates a target-specific experiment or generates an implementable method contract. An applied Guard becomes a planning constraint or diagnostic directive.

**Experimental Setup:**
The system uses GLM-5.2 (a self-hosted LLM) for all LLM components. Verification effort is controlled by a fixed retry limit and occurs pre-dispatch to conserve machine experiment budget. Evaluation is stage-wise, assessing artifact quality, record support, applicability judgments, and production outcomes.

#### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings across different stages of the framework:

1.  **Non-Monotonic Adaptation Trajectories:** Analysis of 30 industrial paper-to-target adaptations revealed that iterative experimentation often discovers better observed states; 26 out of 30 methods showed at least one later valid round outperforming the first. However, these gains were frequently not preserved: for 22 methods, the final round performed worse than an earlier best. This finding highlights that the final state of an experimental trajectory is not consistently the most informative or useful knowledge, underscoring the need for explicit evidence qualification across rounds.

2.  **Bounded Self-Verification Effectiveness:**
    *   **Proposal Handoff:** For paper-to-proposal handoffs, the generate–verify–repair loop significantly improved output quality. Initial gate acceptance was 73.3%, which increased to 100% after allowing up to three retries, eliminating the need for manual correction.
    *   **Code Handoff:** At the proposal-to-code handoff, the loop increased implementation completeness from 92% to 97% and observability from 67% to 100%. Code alignment with the proposal showed a marginal change from 50% to 52%.
    *   **Post-hoc Audit:** A separate audit confirmed that 100% of the finally accepted paper-reproduction bundles had source-traceable spans and source-supported core mechanisms. Coverage of source paper "Method" sections and blocks, however, was 92.8% and 87.4% respectively, indicating some incompleteness.
    *   Conclusion: Context-isolated self-verification provided measurable corrections, particularly for completeness and observability, although it did not fully address all alignment issues.

3.  **Automated Reproduction on a Public Benchmark:** The adaptation pipeline successfully reproduced results for five models on the Amazon Electronics dataset. The reproduced models maintained the reported relative ordering (Spearman ρ = 1.0), and all non-Factorization Machine (FM) models still outperformed FM, indicating the pipeline's capability to translate paper specifications into executable and verifiably consistent experiments.

4.  **Experimental Record Audit:**
    *   Out of 14 candidate claims processed, 8 were qualified as actionable "Repairs," 1 as a diagnostic "Guard," and 5 were "Withheld." This resulted in a frozen registry of 9 usable records.
    *   Each of the nine records contained the five predefined semantic blocks, specifying an explicit trigger and applicability boundary. The 8 Repair records were compilable into executable method contracts, and the Guard record encoded a planning constraint.
    *   All 34 referenced source artifacts were traceable and matched their archived SHA-256 values, confirming provenance. Withheld candidates were correctly excluded from reusable entries.
    *   Conclusion: The qualification process successfully distinguished supported, attributable claims from those lacking sufficient evidence, demonstrating that evidence structure, rather than just outcome, dictated qualification.

5.  **Memory-Conditioned Adaptation (Diagnostic):**
    *   A paired diagnostic, comparing no-memory runs with memory-conditioned runs, revealed that memory reuse is conditional. Out of five pairs where the reference audit labeled records as "Apply," three resulted in beneficial outcomes, one was neutral, and one was harmful after execution.
    *   For three pairs where the reference labeled records as "Defer" or "Reject" but were manually dispatched (bypassing the controller's decision), two were harmful and one was neutral.
    *   Conclusion: While reference applicability provided a filter for plausible reuse, the ultimate target-specific outcome was determined by execution, indicating that even seemingly applicable records do not guarantee benefit.

6.  **Controller Error Analysis:**
    *   Analysis of the hybrid LLM-assisted controller's decisions on a diagnostic set of 64 target–record pairs showed an affirmative precision of 25% (1 out of 4 Apply decisions matched the reference-Apply). The controller's overall accuracy was 67.2%, which was lower than a hypothetical "always-Reject" rule (79.7%) in this class-imbalanced dataset.
    *   Conclusion: The tested controller exhibited a tendency for false affirmatives, indicating that its affirmative applicability judgment is a bottleneck. It did not reliably balance conservative filtering with the objective of generating experimental progress, highlighting an area for future improvement.

7.  **Production Case Studies:**
    *   Despite the identified limitations in controller judgment, the complete workflow produced two candidates that yielded positive online lifts over frozen production baselines in real-world A/B tests. One candidate improved live streaming page duration by +0.75%, and another boosted user growth net growth utility by +6.34%.
    *   Conclusion: The framework can successfully generate deployable candidates that demonstrate positive impact in industrial settings.

#### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces a paradigm shift in how research agent outputs are treated, moving from raw experimental trajectories to rigorously verified, auditable evidence. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1.  **Enhanced Reliability and Trustworthiness of Automated Research:** By integrating bounded artifact verification and post-execution claim qualification, the framework directly addresses issues of unsupported content, invalid experiments, and confounded results, particularly pertinent with the use of LLMs. This can lead to more reliable and trustworthy scientific findings generated through automation.

2.  **Creation of Auditable and Reusable Knowledge Bases:** The explicit qualification of claims into auditable records, complete with provenance and applicability boundaries, transforms transient experimental trajectories into a structured, reusable knowledge base. This enables systematic reuse of validated findings, reducing redundant experimentation and facilitating cumulative scientific progress.

3.  **Mitigation of Non-Monotonic Progress:** The framework's ability to extract and preserve "best" or "attributable" findings from non-monotonic trajectories, rather than just the final state, prevents the loss of valuable intermediate insights and ensures that effective interventions are not overlooked.

4.  **Guidance for Future Agent Development:** The stage-wise analysis, particularly the identification of the controller's affirmative applicability judgment as a bottleneck, provides concrete directions for improving the intelligence and decision-making capabilities of future research agents. This feedback loop is crucial for the continuous evolution of automated scientific discovery systems.

5.  **Practical Industrial Impact:** The demonstration of positive online lifts in production settings validates the framework's practical utility for industrial ML research and development, especially in domains like recommendation systems. This suggests that the proposed methodology can accelerate innovation and improve the efficiency of deploying new models in real-world applications.

6.  **Generalizability of Principles:** While evaluated in industrial recommendation, the core principles of artifact verification, explicit claim qualification, and the creation of boundary-aware records are generalizable. They could be adapted to enhance the rigor and output quality of automated scientific discovery in other fields, contributing to broader efforts in AI-powered research automation.

In summary, this work provides a systematic approach to convert the extensive output of research agents into actionable, verifiable knowledge, addressing a critical gap between automated experimentation and the accumulation of reliable scientific evidence.
