**2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape**

The field of AI agent development has progressed towards creating systems capable of performing complex, long-horizon tasks that involve search, tool use, evidence inspection, and answer synthesis. Current evaluation methods for these "deep-research agents" largely focus on the correctness of their final output. Benchmarks such as GAIA, BrowseComp, WebArena, OSWorld, DeepResearch Bench, and LiveResearchBench exemplify this outcome-centric approach, assessing task completion and citation-grounded reports.

However, a limitation of outcome-level evaluation is its inability to pinpoint *where* in a multi-step trajectory an agent's reasoning deviates or becomes unreliable. Errors often originate from early, unvalidated commitments that are subsequently inherited and propagated through later steps, leading to an unreliable final answer even if the final step itself appears plausible. Raw agent logs, while containing the necessary evidence for diagnosis, are typically long, heterogeneous, and framework-specific, which complicates direct analysis by humans or even large language models (LLMs). Attempts to use LLMs directly for error finding in full trajectories have shown instability, potentially mistaking benign exploration for errors or overlooking critical early unsupported commitments.

Previous efforts in process-level evaluation and trajectory diagnosis, such as ProcessBench, PRMBench, DeltaBench, and TRAIL, have addressed step-level or tool-use errors. These benchmarks are often tailored for shorter, more structured domains like mathematical reasoning, visual language model (VLM) reasoning, coding traces, or API workflows. Deep-research trajectories, however, present distinct challenges due to their greater length, higher noise levels, and complex interplay of useful exploration, weak evidence, failed searches, and harmful mistakes.

This research addresses this gap by proposing a method for span-level error localization specifically for deep-research agents. It shifts the focus from merely identifying *if* an agent fails to diagnosing *which part* of the recorded decision process makes the outcome unreliable. By introducing a benchmark (TELBENCH) and an auditing framework (DRIFT) designed for the unique characteristics of long, noisy, and claim-dependent deep-research trajectories, this work contributes to a more granular understanding of agent reliability and offers a diagnostic capability beyond final-answer assessment.

**3. Key Objectives and Motivation**

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inadequacy of current outcome-based evaluation methods for deep-research agents. While knowing whether an agent succeeds or fails is important, it does not reveal the underlying reasons for unreliability or failure, nor does it provide actionable insights for improvement. The core problem is that detrimental errors often occur early in a trajectory as an agent makes an unsupported or incorrect commitment, which then propagates through subsequent reasoning steps, leading to an unreliable final answer. Existing methods struggle to identify these root causes within the long and complex logs of deep-research processes.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

*   **To establish span-level error localization as a diagnostic approach for deep-research agents.** The researchers argue that raw logs are too detailed and framework-dependent, while overall trajectory correctness is too coarse. They propose semantic spans as an intermediate analysis unit, enabling precise localization of harmful commitments without being overwhelmed by low-level events.
*   **To create a large-scale, annotated corpus of real deep-research agent trajectories.** This involves collecting trajectories from diverse agent frameworks, backbone models, and benchmarks, and then systematically converting raw logs into semantic spans for annotation. The goal is to provide rich data for understanding agent behavior and failure modes.
*   **To develop TELBENCH, a benchmark specifically for span-level trajectory error localization.** TELBENCH is designed to evaluate a model's ability to identify harmful error spans amidst normal exploration, failed searches, tentative hypotheses, and harmless noise within deep-research trajectories. A key design goal for TELBENCH instances is that errors must be verifiable from internal trajectory evidence, span boundaries must be stable, and instances must contain sufficient non-error behavior as distractors.
*   **To propose DRIFT (Deep-Research Agent Trajectory Auditing Framework), a claim-centric auditing framework.** The motivation for DRIFT is to overcome the limitations of direct LLM prompting, which can be unstable and misinterpret benign actions as errors. DRIFT is designed to systematically track claims made by an agent, verify their evidential support within the trajectory, and identify where unsupported or conflicting claims become consequential and impact the answer path. This claim-centric approach aims to provide a more robust and accurate method for error localization.
*   **To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed DRIFT framework.** The research aims to show that DRIFT can improve span-level error localization and first-error accuracy compared to existing methods, thereby providing a more effective tool for process-level reliability analysis of deep-research agents.

**4. Methodology and Approach**

The methodology of this research involved three main components: data curation for the TELBENCH benchmark, the development of the DRIFT auditing framework, and experimental evaluation.

**4.1. Data Curation for TELBENCH**
The data curation pipeline for TELBENCH proceeded through several stages:

1.  **Trajectory Collection**: A corpus of 2,790 deep-research agent trajectories was collected. These trajectories were generated by running three frontier large language models (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-Sonnet-4.5) under two representative agent frameworks (MiroFlow, OAgent) across three public deep-research benchmarks (GAIA-val, XBench, BrowseComp-test, with BrowseComp downsampled to 200 tasks for balance).
2.  **Span Segmentation**: Raw, framework-specific agent logs, which contain low-level artifacts, were first normalized into unified execution-unit sequences. These sequences were then segmented into *semantic spans*, where each span represents a coherent segment of execution related to a local objective (e.g., planning, retrieval, verification). Segmentation criteria included changes in search target, candidate set, time scope, verification criterion, or reasoning objective. LLM assistance and human inspection were used to audit and refine segment boundaries.
3.  **Error Span Annotation**: Each semantic span was labeled as either an "error" or "non-error." An error span was defined as one that introduces, relies on, amplifies, or finalizes a mistaken, unsupported, contradicted, or prematurely committed judgment that affects the answer path. Benign activities like normal exploration, failed searches (if not committed to), tentative hypotheses, or recovered mistakes were not labeled as errors. The annotation process involved an LLM-assisted expert review: two independent LLMs proposed candidate error spans with rationales, which were then validated, revised, or added by two expert human annotators (from a pool of seven, collectively spending over 300 hours).
4.  **Mechanism Labels (for analysis)**: After binary error labels, additional labels were added for analytical purposes. Each span received an "operation-stage label" (e.g., planning, retrieval, decision-making). Error spans also received a "primary-fault label" (e.g., constraint semantics error, unsupported commitment), chosen from an 18-fault taxonomy grouped into six families. This taxonomy was induced from LLM-generated rationales, clustered, and manually normalized. These labels were used for analysis, not as input for evaluation.
5.  **TELBENCH Dataset Construction**: From the 2,790 trajectories, 1,890 contained at least one error span. This pool was filtered to 1,000 verified instances, ensuring clear error boundaries, trajectory-internal evidence, stable segmentation, and sufficient non-error distractors. The dataset was split into 600 "easy" and 400 "hard" instances based on trajectory complexity and error subtlety, with an average of 11.95 semantic spans per trajectory.

**4.2. DRIFT Framework**
DRIFT (Claim-centric multi-agent auditing framework) operates in a modular fashion to localize errors:

1.  **A: Claim Keeper**: This module performs an initial global pass over the trajectory to build a "claim ledger." It identifies decision-relevant beliefs or commitments made by the agent (e.g., selected entity, accepted constraint, evidence interpretation). For each claim, the ledger records: when it was introduced, when it first became consequential, which later spans reuse it, its type, and its commitment status (exploratory, tentative, consequential, or finalized). This distinguishes exploratory actions from committed reasoning steps.
2.  **B: Support Seeker**: Given the claim ledger, this module checks whether each *consequential* claim is supported by evidence within the trajectory. It assigns one of four support statuses: DIRECT, WEAK, MISSING, or CONFLICTING, along with the spans providing or failing to provide support. Its role is to expose potential support risks without making final error judgments.
3.  **C: Dependency Tracer**: The final module uses the claim ledger and support records to determine which risky claims correspond to harmful error spans. It marks spans that commit to, reuse, amplify, or finalize unsupported or conflicting consequential claims as errors, distinguishing them from benign exploration or noise.

**4.3. Experiment Settings**
Experiments were conducted using TELBENCH with five contemporary LLM families (Qwen-series, GPT-5.4, DeepSeek-V3.2, Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) and four diagnostic frameworks: Bare LLM (direct prompting), Claude Code (adapted agentic auditing baseline), Codex (adapted agentic auditing baseline), and DRIFT. All frameworks received the same input (question and ordered semantic span texts) and were evaluated on span-level macro precision, recall, F1, and first-error accuracy. Each setting was repeated three times.

**5. Main Findings and Results**

The research yielded several key findings regarding deep-research agent reliability and the performance of the proposed framework:

*   **Process Errors vs. Final Outcomes**: Analysis of the full 2,790-trajectory corpus revealed that while 97.3% of failed trajectories contained at least one error span, 36.9% of *successful* trajectories also exhibited process errors. This indicates that span-level errors are closely associated with final failure but are not strictly equivalent, as agents can sometimes recover or reach a correct answer despite intermediate mistakes. Failed trajectories generally contained a higher density of error spans (17.4% vs. 6.3% in successful trajectories).
*   **Error Mechanism Analysis**:
    *   **Stage-Structured Errors**: Error mechanisms were found to be highly stage-dependent. While retrieval spans were frequent, they had the lowest normalized error rate (2.9%). In contrast, decision-making (60.5%) and finalization (51.8%) stages exhibited significantly higher error rates, suggesting that many failures stem from how information is committed, verified, or aggregated, rather than just search activity.
    *   **Fault Taxonomy**: The induced fault taxonomy categorizes errors into six families, with primary faults such as 'evidence gaps', 'unsupported commitments', 'missed checks', and 'constraint semantic errors'.
    *   **Failure Patterns**: Fault frequency alone did not correlate directly with trajectory failure probability; some rarer faults were associated with a higher likelihood of overall trajectory failure. First-error mechanisms varied across benchmarks, frameworks, and models, with evidence and constraint errors being prominent. Failed trajectories showed more first errors at the earliest and latest stages.
*   **DRIFT Performance**:
    *   **Superior Localization**: DRIFT consistently achieved the best overall F1 score for span-level error localization across all tested backbone models. It significantly outperformed both bare prompting and general agentic auditing frameworks (Codex, Claude Code). The improvements were observed in both precision and recall, across easy and hard evaluation splits, indicating a more targeted and accurate error detection rather than merely increasing the number of predicted suspicious spans.
    *   **Generic Auditor Limitations**: Generic agentic auditing frameworks like Codex and Claude Code showed inconsistent gains, and in some configurations, they could degrade performance compared to direct LLM prompting. This suggests that simply structuring an LLM into an agentic workflow is not sufficient; a domain-specific, claim-centric approach like DRIFT is beneficial.
*   **First-Error Localization Challenges**: Despite DRIFT's improvements in overall span-level F1, first-error accuracy remained comparatively lower, particularly on the hard split. This indicates that precisely pinpointing the *earliest* point of error within a long trajectory presents a distinct and more difficult diagnostic challenge than identifying any erroneous region.
*   **Scaling and Complexity Sensitivity**:
    *   **Model Scale**: Increasing model scale (e.g., across Qwen variants) did not result in a monotonic improvement in trajectory diagnosis performance, implying that raw model capacity is not the sole bottleneck; structural diagnostic methods are also crucial.
    *   **Span Complexity**: Performance for both Bare and DRIFT degraded as span complexity increased, but DRIFT maintained a consistent performance advantage across all complexity levels, especially in longer trajectories.
*   **Ablation Study**: Step-by-step addition of DRIFT's modules (Claim Keeper, Support Seeker, Dependency Tracer) showed a steady performance improvement. The Claim Keeper provided a substantial initial gain, followed by further enhancements from Support Seeker (improving recall by surfacing weakly supported commitments) and Dependency Tracer (refining evidence grounding and localization). This confirms the complementary contributions of DRIFT's modular design.
*   **Efficiency and Error-Type Coverage**: DRIFT generally demonstrated a favorable efficiency-performance trade-off, improving F1 without disproportionate token overhead, with the exception of Gemini which incurred higher costs due to 'thinking' tokens. DRIFT also improved recall across nearly all error categories, with notable gains in evidence- and constraint-related failures, aligning with its claim-centric design.

**6. Significance and Potential Impact**

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of large language model (LLM) agents and their evaluation:

*   **Advancement in Agent Evaluation Methodology**: The work introduces a process-level evaluation paradigm for deep-research agents, moving beyond the traditional outcome-centric assessment. By enabling span-level error localization, it provides a more granular and diagnostic understanding of agent behavior, which is critical for developing robust and reliable AI systems. This shift is particularly relevant for long-horizon, complex tasks where final answers alone do not reveal the intricacies of failure or success.
*   **Novel Diagnostic Benchmark**: The creation of TELBENCH addresses a specific need for a benchmark designed for span-level error localization in deep-research agent trajectories. Unlike existing process-level benchmarks, TELBENCH is tailored for the unique challenges of long, noisy, and claim-dependent reasoning, offering a robust platform for evaluating and comparing diagnostic capabilities of agents.
*   **Structured Auditing Framework**: DRIFT provides a principled and modular approach to auditing agent trajectories. Its claim-centric design, which systematically tracks commitments, evaluates their support, and traces dependencies, offers a more effective mechanism for identifying harmful errors than direct LLM prompting or generic agentic wrappers. This framework provides a blueprint for developing more transparent and explainable agent diagnostics.
*   **Insights into Agent Failure Modes**: The detailed mechanism analysis conducted using the annotated corpus and fault taxonomy offers empirical insights into common failure patterns of deep-research agents. Identifying that decision-making and finalization stages are high-risk, or that certain rare faults are highly correlated with overall trajectory failure, can directly inform the design of future agent architectures and error-handling strategies.
*   **Guidance for Future Agent Development**: The findings suggest that merely increasing the scale of backbone LLMs is insufficient for robust trajectory diagnosis; specialized, structured diagnostic frameworks are necessary. This insight guides researchers and developers towards building more sophisticated self-correction and debugging capabilities within agents, rather than relying solely on larger models.
*   **Practical Debugging and Improvement**: The ability to localize errors to specific spans within a trajectory has practical implications for debugging and iteratively improving deep-research agents. Developers can pinpoint precise points of failure, understand the propagation of unsupported claims, and therefore implement targeted corrections, ultimately leading to more trustworthy and efficient AI research assistants.

Overall, this research represents a step towards creating more reliable, auditable, and understandable deep-research agents by providing tools and methods for analyzing their internal reasoning processes rather than just their final outputs.
