## Detailed Report: Long-Horizon Autonomous Architecture Research with a Language-Model Agent: A Behavioural Case Study

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The authors of this research paper are Aon Safdar and Mohamed Saadeldin, both affiliated with University College Dublin, Ireland.

### 2. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research advances the field of automated machine learning (AutoML) and the application of large language models (LLMs) as autonomous agents. Traditionally, AutoML has focused on optimizing fixed search algorithms over predefined spaces for tasks like hyperparameter tuning or neural architecture search (NAS). In these classical systems, the proposer, evaluator, and selector components are distinct. More recent work has integrated LLMs into existing AutoML pipelines, typically assigning them a single role such as proposal generation or code writing.

This paper takes a further step by assigning a single general-purpose LLM all research roles: proposer, implementer, executor, interpreter, and archivist. The primary interest of this study is not to determine if an LLM agent can fully substitute for a human researcher, but rather to analyze the agent's behavior over an extended research horizon and to understand the influence of the surrounding workflow on this behavior.

The work distinguishes itself from other LLM-agent benchmarks by focusing on long-horizon, open-ended research over weeks of wall-clock time and hundreds of experiments, as opposed to short-horizon, task-completion settings. This extended timescale allows for the observation of behavioral phenomena such as saturation plateaus, anchoring, success-chasing, risk aversion following failures, and cross-scale anti-transfer effects, which are not visible in shorter benchmarks. Furthermore, this study focuses on structural architecture research, requiring the agent to modify model code itself, differentiating it from tasks like program synthesis, manuscript generation, or hyperparameter optimization. The contribution is primarily descriptive and methodological, concentrating on analyzing the agent's actions and the impact of workflow choices, rather than making claims about LLMs as general substitutes for human researchers. The authors maintain an agnostic stance on the spectrum of human-AI collaboration, with the system operating mostly autonomously but with bounded human intervention.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research was to investigate the behavior of a single general-purpose large language model when tasked to act as the sole researcher on a long-horizon neural-architecture design problem. This involved equipping the agent with a scientific question, an initial hypothesis, motivation, a compute allocation, and a specific set of research affordances, then observing its autonomous operation over an extended period.

The motivation behind this study was to move beyond simply demonstrating an LLM's capability to perform isolated tasks and instead analyze its systemic behavior in a prolonged, open-ended research context. The authors were particularly interested in understanding:
*   The nature of the agent’s research behavior over a "long horizon," which encompasses hundreds of GPU-hours and dozens of iterations.
*   The role played by the surrounding workflow in shaping the agent's investigative and decision-making processes.

To achieve this, a specific research problem was chosen: the design of a channel-primary vision transformer constrained to use only channel attention with convolutional spatial mixing, explicitly excluding spatial self-attention. This problem was selected for three main reasons:
1.  **Substantive:** It represented a genuine architectural question with existing prior art, providing a meaningful context for research.
2.  **Constrained:** The elimination of spatial self-attention prevented the agent from relying on established Vision Transformer (ViT) architectural "recipes," forcing it to explore novel design spaces.
3.  **Unfamiliar:** Given that general-purpose LLMs are predominantly trained on data related to spatial attention, this unfamiliar regime of pure channel attention was expected to challenge the agent's pre-existing intuitions, offering an opportunity to observe how it recognized and responded to such discrepancies.

The study aimed to deliver specific contributions framed as observations from this behavioral case study, rather than generalized laws. These contributions included:
1.  Documenting a workflow capable of supporting long-horizon, single-agent autonomous research on a real high-performance computing (HPC) allocation.
2.  Quantitatively analyzing the agent's hypothesis trajectory, including identifying productivity structures (e.g., rapid gains, saturation, recovery) and the impact of expanded tool access.
3.  Examining the agent’s hypothesis-generation patterns to decompose biases towards greedy, incremental proposals.
4.  Cataloging instances of independent rediscovery of established machine learning results, as well as directions not pursued by the agent and instances where standard design choices were overturned.
5.  Proposing candidate workflow modifications, grounded in observed failure modes, as testable hypotheses for future autonomous-research systems.

Ultimately, the research sought to provide foundational data for designers of future autonomous-research systems, highlighting that understanding how workflow discipline influences a persistent LLM agent is crucial for fostering productive research collaboration over extended periods.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research involved designing and implementing an autonomous research loop driven by a single general-purpose large language model (LLM). This LLM was a frontier instruction-tuned model accessed via a terminal-oriented command-line interface, utilizing shell and file-system primitives for all tool interactions. No fine-tuning, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) layers, or domain-specific scaffolds were employed. The agent’s sole persistent memory across sessions was a structured research log.

**Loop Design Principles:** The autonomous research loop was built around four core principles:
1.  **Single Agent, Single Hypothesis:** The agent executed one experiment at a time, avoiding parallel execution to ensure clear causal signals and facilitate failure diagnosis.
2.  **Commit-or-Discard Evaluation Rule:** Following each experiment, the agent compared its result against the current champion. Improvements led to the change being committed to source control and becoming the new baseline; otherwise, the experimental change was reverted. This rule was noted to structurally impose a greedy hill-climbing search strategy.
3.  **Structured Persistent Memory:** Every hypothesis was recorded in a fixed-template research log capturing motivation, literature grounding, exact changes, budget (parameters, compute), and outcome. This log served as the agent’s primary cross-session memory and the main unit of observation for the study. The agent was required to read the log at the start of each session and append to it before each experiment.
4.  **End-of-Turn After Submission:** The agent was instructed to end its turn immediately after submitting a training job, with external signals returning control upon job completion. This practice conserved token budget for scientific reasoning and mitigated certain autonomy failures.

**Research Affordances:** The agent was equipped with four minimal classes of research affordances:
1.  **Source and Experiment Management:** A Git repository managed version control, with one branch per phase and one commit per accepted hypothesis. Rejected hypotheses were reverted from the working tree but documented in the log.
2.  **Experiment Tracking:** A standard experiment-tracking service recorded per-epoch metrics. The agent was expected to use training curves for interpreting ambiguous results.
3.  **Literature Access:** From the second phase (Phase 1b) onward, the agent could query preprint servers (e.g., arXiv), code repositories, and a model/dataset hub via a uniform tool protocol. Prior to this, only internal reasoning was available.
4.  **Compute:** Experiments were run on a shared HPC cluster managed by Slurm, utilizing a mix of accelerator generations. The agent submitted jobs exclusively through this scheduler and was prohibited from running training on interactive login hosts. The total compute footprint over the program's approximate 10-week wall-clock span was about 2,400 GPU-hours across roughly 150 submitted jobs, with a median substantive run duration of approximately 2.5 hours.

**Human-Authored Interface:** Three documents governed the agent's behavior:
1.  **Research Brief:** Defined the scientific question, design constraints (e.g., pure channel-attention), compute and parameter budgets, and illustrative research directions.
2.  **Workflow Template:** Outlined the per-hypothesis loop, the required structure for log entries, and explicit prohibitions (e.g., no scheduler polling, single active job).
3.  **Literature-Grounding Requirement:** Introduced in Phase 1b, this rule mandated that every hypothesis either cite a prior result or be explicitly declared an internal extrapolation.

**Human Intervention Record:** Human interventions were strategic and bounded, limited to declaring phase transitions, repairing autonomy failures, or expanding the agent's tool surface. Tactical decisions such as hypothesis selection, hyperparameter choices, code writing, and result interpretation remained the agent's responsibility. These interventions included issuing the initial research brief, enabling literature access, adjusting parameter budgets and dataset difficulty, nudging the agent towards structural innovation during plateaus, enforcing memory discipline, and prescribing a "bold structural hypothesis" requirement.

**Programme Phases:** The study was structured into three distinct phases, separated by human-declared transitions:
*   **Phase 1 (H1–H42):** Focused on CIFAR-10 with 5.8M parameters, utilizing configuration/code editing, tracking, and source control. Achieved a 36% success rate.
*   **Phase 1b (H43–H67):** Continued on CIFAR-10 with 5.8M parameters, but with the addition of literature access. Achieved a 38% success rate.
*   **Phase 2 (P2-H1–P2-H32):** Transitioned to CIFAR-100 with a larger budget of 22M parameters, using the full toolkit. Achieved a 32% success rate.
*   **Phase 3 (H3-1–H3-18):** Scaled up to ImageNet-1K with 22M parameters, incorporating multi-GPU distributed configurations. Achieved a 12% success rate.

The study acknowledged that exact run-level numerical reproducibility is not claimed due to LLM stochasticity, but predicted that qualitative behavioral patterns would recur across similar problems. The per-hypothesis record, experiment logs, and workflow details were extensively documented to enable audit and replication of the analysis.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the LLM agent's behavior and the impact of its operational workflow over a long-horizon research program.

**Headline Outcomes:** The agent successfully improved the non-standard channel-primary vision transformer. On CIFAR-10, the architecture progressed from a baseline of 69.67% top-1 accuracy to a champion design achieving 96.59% (100 epochs) and 97.25% (300 epochs). On CIFAR-100, it reached 83.37% (100 epochs) and 85.07% (300 epochs). For ImageNet-1K, the agent achieved a usable but sub-SOTA design, improving from a 77.65% baseline to 77.91% (100 epochs) with a low-rank channel-attention block, before the program was stopped due to observed saturation. The initial, largest accuracy gain (on CIFAR-10) was attributed to a single early hypothesis, with subsequent improvements being refinements.

**Outputs of the Loop:** Beyond accuracy improvements, the agent produced a complete architectural specification, a time-stamped research log of approximately one hundred hypotheses (each with motivation, literature grounding, implementation details, budget, and outcome), a per-hypothesis source-control history, comprehensive training telemetry, and agent-authored synthesis documents summarizing learned empirical laws.

**Productivity Regimes:** The program's first phase on CIFAR-10 showed a distinct three-regime productivity structure:
1.  **Discovery Regime (H1–H6):** Characterized by rapid architectural gains, with a 67% success rate and an average gain of +5.71 percentage points per successful hypothesis.
2.  **Exploitation Regime (H7–H13):** Focused on tuning hyperparameters and reorganizing the architectural hierarchy, showing a 71% success rate but smaller average gains of +0.32 percentage points.
3.  **Saturation Regime (H14–H42):** A prolonged period where the agent proposed numerous plausible configuration variations, but few resulted in significant improvements (21% success rate, +0.17 pp/success). This saturation was identified as a key behavioral phenomenon, indicating that the agent efficiently mined finite design spaces but lacked a mechanism to independently escalate the type of hypotheses when a space was exhausted. Exit from this regime required a human-declared transition that expanded the agent’s action surface. Rolling success rates across phases visibly supported this pattern, dropping sharply after exploitation and only recovering with expanded action surfaces or scale transitions.

**Innovation Attribution:** Analysis of the +26.92 percentage point CIFAR-10 gain revealed a highly long-tailed distribution of contributions. The single largest gain (+21.41 pp, 79.5% of total) resulted from the first accepted hypothesis: the introduction of a feedforward sublayer within each channel-attention block. Subsequent gains were modest, with approximately two-thirds originating from training-recipe configuration tweaks in Phase 1 and one-quarter from literature-derived code changes in Phase 1b. The agent's role was primarily selecting and adapting existing ideas from literature rather than inventing novel mechanisms.

**Tool Access as a Phase Transition:** The transition from Phase 1 (no literature access) to Phase 1b (with literature access) significantly altered the *type* of hypotheses generated. The fraction of hypotheses involving code modifications, as opposed to mere configuration changes, increased from 5% to 76%. While it did not increase the magnitude of the largest gains, literature access enabled the agent to overcome the configuration-saturation wall. This observation suggests that the action surface provided to the agent is a critical determinant of long-horizon research productivity.

**Cross-Scale Anti-Findings:** Several training-recipe decisions that were beneficial on CIFAR-10 at 5.8M parameters reversed their effect on CIFAR-100 at 22M parameters. Examples included Mixup, label smoothing, and drop-path removal. The agent implicitly carried over these small-scale decisions, requiring several re-tuning hypotheses at the larger scale. This highlighted a need for explicit regime-aware re-validation in autonomous systems.

**Hypothesis-Generation Patterns:** Six recurring patterns were observed:
*   **Systematic Sweeping:** Iterating along continuous dimensions (e.g., learning rate, kernel size), often overshooting and then retreating.
*   **Ablation after Success:** Consistently proposing ablations of new components after an accepted hypothesis.
*   **Greedy Anchoring:** All hypotheses built upon the current champion, without re-rooting the search on discarded branches.
*   **Context-Sensitive Retrial:** Re-testing previously rejected mechanisms in new contexts or after scale transitions, sometimes finding success.
*   **Literature-Grounded Adaptation:** Citing specific papers and adapting their mechanisms, with varying success.
*   **Risk Aversion after Bold Failure:** Consistently reverting to incremental proposals for several turns after a significant failure, even when bolder attempts were permitted.

**Vignette: Saturation around a Phase 3 Champion:** In Phase 3, after accepting a low-rank channel-attention block (from Linformer), the agent proposed five subsequent narrow variations, all of which were rejected. Despite its internal reasoning explicitly identifying saturation and the need for structural shifts, its actions consistently followed a local gradient, illustrating the combined effect of greedy anchoring and risk aversion.

**Independent Rediscoveries:** The agent experimentally rediscovered several established machine learning findings without prior explicit knowledge, such as the benefits of deeper-than-wider architectures and the utility of FFNs in channel-attention models. Notably, it found that single-head channel attention outperformed multi-head, an observation contrary to common intuition from spatial attention, and correctly attributed this to the specific geometry of channel attention.

**Directions Not Pursued:** The agent consistently failed to pursue several directions explicitly mentioned in its research brief (e.g., ensemble analysis, curriculum training, knowledge distillation), interpreted as "success-chasing" where it preferentially explored neighborhoods of recent successes.

**Instruction-Induced versus Inherent Bias:** A key finding was the decomposition of the agent's observed bias towards conservative, incremental choices. The workflow design, which structurally imposed a greedy hill-climbing approach (commit-or-discard rule, single mutable copy, one-job-at-a-time), was identified as the primary driver of this behavior. Evidence included the shift from configuration to code changes when literature access and code editing were permitted, and the agent's internal suggestions for backtracking that the workflow did not support. A residual inherent bias, however, was also observed, manifested as risk aversion after bold failures and anchoring on a small set of familiar literature. This suggests that workflow design was at least as impactful as the agent’s intrinsic capabilities in shaping long-horizon research behavior.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides critical insights into the dynamics of long-horizon autonomous research powered by large language models, emphasizing the paramount importance of workflow design. The findings demonstrate that while LLM agents possess significant capabilities for proposing, implementing, evaluating, and recording experiments, their macroscopic research behavior is profoundly shaped by the operational framework within which they function.

**What Worked:** The study identified several effective workflow choices: a single, authoritative instruction document for task guidance; a fixed, persistent per-hypothesis record template that fostered structured reasoning and self-use; per-hypothesis source-control history for audit and rollback; and integrated training-telemetry tools for informed result interpretation. These elements collectively enabled the agent to operate effectively over an extended period.

**What Did Not Work:** Several design choices led to negative lessons: unsupervised agent autonomy proved fragile, requiring iterative instruction amendments to address issues like premature turn termination or misreading scheduler states. A permissive research brief lacking explicit requirements for literature grounding initially led to a dominance of configuration tweaks over structural innovations. Token-rate limits and context-window saturation necessitated aggressive pruning of information and the introduction of compact synthesis documents. The greedy workflow intrinsically led to incremental behavior, and the agent's post-failure risk aversion was self-reinforcing, requiring external prompts to encourage bolder explorations.

**Recommendations for Future Autonomous-Research Loops:** Based on the observed failure modes, the authors propose five testable hypotheses for future workflow modifications:
1.  **Diversify the proposal strategy:** Implement a mixed policy combining greedy exploration with explicit "diversification" modes (e.g., trying less explored directions, revisiting earlier branches, combining previously failed ideas).
2.  **Budget moonshot hypotheses:** Mandate periodic generation of structurally novel hypotheses, regardless of perceived success probability, to counter inherent risk aversion.
3.  **Support explicit architectural forks:** Allow the agent to maintain and concurrently develop multiple distinct architectural champions, enabling parallel exploration and eventual reconciliation.
4.  **Track regime metadata on every accepted finding:** Annotate findings with their validation context (scale, dataset, etc.) to trigger automatic re-validation during regime transitions, preventing the silent carry-over of context-specific decisions.
5.  **Utilize compact synthesis documents:** Periodically generated and re-read agent-authored summaries can help manage the growing size of the research log, improving the coherence of long-term proposals.

**Cost and Effort:** The research spanned approximately ten weeks of wall-clock time, with roughly 90% spent on queue waiting or training. Total GPU-hours amounted to about 2,400. LLM token consumption was estimated to be in the tens of millions. Human researcher involvement was limited to strategic interventions and daily oversight, not tactical decision-making.

**Limitations and Open Questions:** The study acknowledges limitations, including its grounding in a single run of a single agent on a single problem, meaning some quantitative findings may be problem-specific. The absence of a matched human control group makes direct comparative claims indicative rather than controlled. Workflow and tool-access confounds exist due to simultaneous changes, hindering precise attribution of specific effects. The use of different evaluation protocols across phases and the reliance on a single long run rather than multiple replicates also pose limitations. Open questions remain regarding the generalizability of productivity structures, whether LLM "rediscoveries" are evidence of genuine intuition or re-emission of training data patterns, the true extent of saturation versus behavioral risk aversion, and how the loop scales with problem difficulty.

In conclusion, this paper underscores that for long-horizon autonomous research with LLM agents, investing in thoughtful workflow design is crucial. While LLM agents are capable collaborators—proposing, implementing, recording, and adapting information—it is the workflow that transforms a "flawed-but-tireless" agent into a productive research partner, directing its capabilities to overcome inherent biases and structural limitations. The proposed workflow modifications offer testable avenues for future research into enhancing autonomous scientific discovery systems.
