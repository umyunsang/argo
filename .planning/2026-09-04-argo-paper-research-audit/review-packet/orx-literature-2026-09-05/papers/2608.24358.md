### Research Report: The Handoff Tax: Continuing Non-Native Trajectories in LLM Agents

#### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research was conducted by Roy Ganz, Mor Shpigel Nacson, Adi Kalyanpur, and Ron Litman. All authors are affiliated with AWS, specifically the Agentic AI division. Mor Shpigel Nacson and Roy Ganz are noted as having equal contribution, and Roy Ganz is indicated as the corresponding author.

#### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language model (LLM) agents has progressed to enable long-running tasks that involve numerous model calls, tool uses, and code modifications. In these scenarios, the selection of an appropriate LLM model presents both a technical and an economic challenge, given the varying cost-quality profiles of available models. Higher-capability models generally offer superior performance but incur higher operational costs, while lower-capability models provide a more economical option at a reduced performance level.

Prior research has addressed the cost-quality trade-off in LLM inference through methods such as model cascades and routers. Cascades typically involve invoking more capable models only when less expensive ones prove insufficient, while routers dispatch requests to an optimal model from a pool. These approaches have been extended to multi-turn interactions, determining which model should act at specific junctures. Some specialized work, such as SWE-Router, explores using partial coding trajectories to decide on model continuation or restart, though without transferring the full trajectory. Other studies examine "directional drift" in dialogue prefixes or agents taking over interrupted tasks, focusing on information exposure to successors rather than cost-profiled alternatives.

This work distinguishes itself by systematically investigating a practical user pattern in long-horizon coding agents: mid-task switching between commercial LLMs with distinct cost and capability profiles. Unlike existing routing mechanisms that primarily decide *which* model to use, this research focuses on the consequences of *how* a model switch, or "handoff," is managed. It explores the implications of inheriting a "non-native trajectory"—a sequence of actions, reasoning, and tool uses generated by a different model—and the effect of varying the information transferred during such a handoff. This emphasis on the *handoff interface* and its specific impact on end-to-end quality and monetary cost across different directions (escalation and downshift) and timings represents a focused contribution to understanding the economics and performance dynamics of LLM agent deployment in complex, multi-step tasks.

#### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the practical considerations users face when deploying LLM coding agents for long-running tasks. These tasks can span dozens to hundreds of model calls, making the choice of LLM an economic decision alongside a technical one. Users frequently encounter a dilemma: either initiate a task with a more expensive, high-capability (HC) model to ensure quality, or start with a cheaper, low-capability (LC) model and only escalate if difficulties arise. Conversely, once complex reasoning is complete, users might consider switching from an HC model to an LC model to reduce remaining costs. This dynamic gives rise to two common model switching patterns:

1.  **Escalation (LC→HC):** A user starts with a low-cost, low-capability model, and if the agent struggles or fails to make sufficient progress, they switch to a high-cost, high-capability model to attempt to rescue the task.
2.  **Downshift (HC→LC):** A user starts with a high-cost, high-capability model for critical initial reasoning, and once the most challenging aspects of the task are perceived to be complete, they switch to a low-cost, low-capability model to finish the task more economically.

While model switching is a feature supported by several contemporary coding agent interfaces (e.g., `/model` commands in various platforms), the cost-quality implications of these capability handoffs remain insufficiently understood.

The core challenge in such handoffs is that the receiving model must continue a trajectory that was generated by a different model. Unlike scenarios where a model extends its own work, a handoff requires the receiver to inherit a "non-native" context. This inherited trajectory might contain reasoning paths, hypotheses, tool-use idioms, or even dead ends that the receiving model would not have produced on its own. The paper hypothesizes that this non-native context could either assist the receiver (e.g., an LC model benefiting from an HC model's groundwork) or hinder it (e.g., an HC model being anchored by an LC model's suboptimal choices). The impact is also expected to be direction-dependent.

To address this gap in understanding, the research sets out to investigate two primary questions:

1.  **Handoff Impact:** How does inheriting a non-native trajectory affect the cost-quality trade-off in long-horizon coding tasks? This question seeks to quantify the performance and cost changes associated with switching models mid-task, compared to single-model runs.
2.  **Handoff Interface:** How does the specific information transferred at the handoff—beyond just the edited repository state—shape this effect? This objective probes the optimal ways to convey the prior model's work to the successor, exploring different methods of trajectory transfer.

By systematically studying these questions across various dimensions (direction, timing, and interface), the researchers aim to uncover the underlying dynamics of mid-trajectory model handoffs and provide insights into optimizing agentic workflows.

#### 4. Methodology and Approach

The study employed a systematic experimental framework to investigate mid-trajectory model handoffs in long-horizon coding tasks.

**4.1 Task Environment and Benchmark**
The primary evaluation was conducted using **SWE-bench Verified**, a benchmark comprising real-world GitHub issues requiring code resolution, complete with executable pass/fail evaluations and difficulty annotations. All experimental conditions utilized the `mini-swe-agent` scaffold, ensuring consistent tools and prompts across runs. The agent environment maintained a single agent loop and Docker environment, ensuring that the repository state (edited files on disk) persisted across model handoffs.

**4.2 Model Pairs**
The study utilized two distinct families of LLM pairs, each consisting of a lower-cost, lower-capability (LC) model and a higher-cost, higher-capability (HC) model:
*   **Claude Family:** Claude Haiku 4.5 (LC) and Claude Opus 4.7 (HC) from Anthropic.
*   **GPT Family:** GPT-5.6 Luna (LC) and GPT-5.6 Sol (HC) from OpenAI.
Temperature for Haiku was set to 0, while GPT-5.6 and Opus 4.7 used provider defaults (medium and high reasoning effort, respectively).

**4.3 Handoff Conditions**
The experimental design varied handoffs along three axes:
*   **Directions:**
    *   **Escalation (LC→HC):** Start with LC, switch to HC. This tests HC's ability to "rescue" a trajectory initiated by LC and the associated cost.
    *   **Downshift (HC→LC):** Start with HC, switch to LC. This tests the retention of HC's quality advantage and its impact on cost.
*   **Handoff Strategies (Interfaces):** All strategies preserved the edited working tree ($W_K$) at the point of switch, differing only in the trajectory information ($T_{1:K}$) passed to the suffix model:
    *   **Raw:** The full trajectory $T_{1:K}$ (system prompt, task, prefix reasoning, tool calls, tool observations) was transferred verbatim. No message announced the model change.
    *   **Compact_pre:** The prefix model summarized $T_{1:K}$, and only this summary was passed to the suffix model.
    *   **Compact_suf:** The suffix model summarized $T_{1:K}$ itself and then continued from that summary. This isolated the effect of summary authorship.
    *   **Traj-drop:** No trajectory information was transferred. The suffix model received only the system prompt, original task, a static continuation message, and $W_K$.
*   **Control Conditions (for Escalation):** To assess if any continuation was better than abandoning LC's work, two restart controls were included:
    *   **Abort + HC fresh:** Charged for LC's work up to the switch point (K) but discarded its trajectory and working-tree edits, restarting HC from the original task state.
    *   **LC-full + HC-full:** Charged for a complete LC run followed by a complete HC run from the original task state. Quality for both controls was based on the matched HC-only outcome.
*   **Switch Points:** Handoff timing was calibrated using seven percentiles ({5, 10, 15, 25, 35, 45, 50}) of the starting model's step-count distribution, calculated separately within easy, medium, and hard task-difficulty buckets to avoid bias.

**4.4 Evaluation and Metrics**
*   **Switched Subset:** Comparisons were restricted to the intersection of instances that actually underwent a handoff under all four strategies at a given switch point, ensuring a fair, matched evaluation.
*   **Raw Metrics:** Pass rate, mean monetary cost (USD, based on provider pricing, including cache reads/writes), and mean step count.
*   **Normalized Metrics:**
    *   **Quality Recovery (QRec):** Percentage of HC's quality advantage over LC that a strategy recovered. ($0\%$ at LC quality, $100\%$ at HC quality).
    *   **Cost-Savings Retention (CSRet):** Percentage of LC's cost advantage over HC that a strategy retained. ($100\%$ at LC cost, $0\%$ at HC cost). Values outside $[0, 100]$ indicated performance beyond these anchors.

**4.5 Experimental Scale**
The study involved 58 configurations per model family (two single-model baselines + 56 handoff configurations across seven switch points, four interfaces, two directions). This led to 58,000 agent runs across 500 SWE-bench Verified instances for both model families, processing 2 million LLM API calls and 36 billion tokens.

**4.6 Beyond the Coding-Agent Setting**
To explore the generalizability of findings, two additional studies were conducted using "Raw" handoffs to vary information dynamics:
*   **LiC (Lost in Conversation):** Evaluated Raw handoffs on 535 tasks across five families where requirements were revealed incrementally, impacting when the task became fully specified and solvable.
*   **BrowseComp:** Evaluated Raw handoffs on 200 web browsing questions where the task was upfront, but evidence accumulated through search actions.

#### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding mid-trajectory model handoffs, demonstrating a "handoff tax" and its dependence on direction and interface.

**5.1 General Handoff Patterns and the Handoff Tax**

*   **Raw Escalation (LC→HC):** Across both Claude and GPT model families, raw escalation (transferring the full trajectory) recovered less than half of the high-capability (HC) model’s quality advantage over the low-capability (LC) model (QRec = 47% for Claude, 36% for GPT). This limited quality recovery often came with a substantial cost premium.
    *   For the Claude pair, raw escalation was notably expensive, costing over twice as much as simply restarting with the HC model from scratch ($1.61 vs. $0.72), and was strictly dominated by restarting HC in terms of both cost and accuracy.
    *   For the GPT pair, raw escalation remained cheaper than HC-only ($0.36 vs. $0.47) but still provided limited quality improvement.
*   **Raw Downshift (HC→LC):** In contrast to escalation, raw downshift provided a more favorable cost-quality trade-off. LC receivers retained substantial quality gains over LC-only while remaining cheaper than HC-only.
    *   Claude downshift retained 80% of LC's cost advantage.
    *   GPT downshift retained 79% of HC's quality advantage.

**5.2 Impact of Handoff Interfaces**

The choice of handoff interface significantly influenced outcomes, with direction-dependent optimal strategies.

*   **Escalation (LC→HC):** Reducing inherited LC trajectory information generally improved the trade-off.
    *   **Compaction:** Both `Compact_pre` (prefix model summary) and `Compact_suf` (suffix model summary) interfaces showed slightly higher pass rates than Raw. `Compact_pre` provided the highest cost retention among escalation interfaces, raising QRec from 47% to 60% and improving CSRet from -285% to -11% for Claude, and from 26% to 49% for GPT.
    *   **Trajectory Removal (`Traj-drop`):** This interface, which removed the trajectory while preserving LC's working-tree edits, led to the largest quality gains. QRec rose from 47% to 64% for Claude and from 36% to 84% for GPT. However, `Traj-drop` remained costlier than HC-only in both families (negative CSRet), indicating that quality recovery came at a cost premium over an HC-only run.
    *   **Difficulty-dependent effectiveness:** For Claude, on hard tasks, reduced-context interfaces (`Compact_pre`, `Compact_suf`, `Traj-drop`) became cheaper than HC-only while recovering 65-74% of the quality gap, a shift not observed for Raw escalation.
*   **Downshift (HC→LC):** Removing the HC trajectory harmed downshift quality.
    *   **Trajectory Removal (`Traj-drop`):** This interface became the lowest-quality and most expensive downshift strategy for Claude (28% QRec vs. 50% for Raw, 56% for `Compact_pre`), and similarly reduced quality for GPT (53% QRec vs. 79% for Raw). This indicates that the HC trajectory provides valuable guidance for the LC receiver.
    *   **Directional Duality:** The findings suggest that LC trajectories can burden HC receivers during escalation, whereas HC trajectories are beneficial guides for LC receivers during downshift.

**5.3 Cost Mechanics by Handoff Direction**

The study identified distinct computational mechanisms underlying the handoff tax for different directions:

*   **Raw Escalation:** The cost premium primarily stemmed from inflated *cost per HC step* (2.2x for Claude, 1.6x for GPT compared to `Compact_pre`), rather than an increase in the number of HC steps. This suggests that the full LC trajectory made individual HC calls more expensive.
*   **Traj-drop Downshift:** The cost premium primarily arose from an increase in *LC step count* (1.6x for Claude, 2.0x for GPT compared to `Compact_pre`), rather than more expensive individual calls. This is consistent with the LC model needing to perform additional work to reconstruct missing HC context.

**5.4 Beyond the Coding-Agent Setting (Raw Handoffs)**

Exploratory studies in other information dynamics settings using only Raw handoffs revealed:

*   **LiC (Lost in Conversation - Late-arriving requirements):** This setting, where task requirements are revealed incrementally, favored escalation. Escalation recovered 86% of HC's quality advantage (vs. 31% for downshift), suggesting that assigning the critical, fully-specified stage to an HC model is beneficial when information accrues late.
*   **BrowseComp (Progressive search):** Escalation nearly closed the HC quality gap (QRec = 95.8% for GPT) but without producing cost savings (CSRet = -30.0%). Downshift, conversely, offered a useful middle ground (QRec = 56.7%, CSRet = 76.8%). Inherited search progress from the LC model in escalation did shorten the HC continuation, but not enough to offset the overall cost.

#### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic examination of mid-trajectory model handoffs in long-horizon LLM agent tasks, particularly within the context of coding agents. Its findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how to manage the cost-quality trade-offs inherent in using diverse LLMs for complex, multi-step operations.

The concept of a "handoff tax" highlights that simply switching models is not without consequences, and the manner of this transition significantly affects overall performance and cost. A primary implication is that **model handoffs should be conceptualized and treated as a distinct inference problem**, rather than merely an extension of existing model routing strategies. While routing determines *which* model acts next, the handoff interface dictates *what information* that model inherits, and this distinction is shown to be critical.

Key areas of significance and potential impact include:

*   **Optimizing Agent Workflows:** The demonstration of direction-dependent value in inherited trajectories (LC trajectories burdening HC receivers, HC trajectories guiding LC receivers) suggests that current default handoff mechanisms (e.g., raw transfer of full trajectory) are often suboptimal. This insight can inform the design of more intelligent agentic systems that adapt their handoff strategies based on the direction of the switch.
*   **Interface Design for LLM Agents:** The findings underscore the importance of **jointly optimizing handoff interface design with receiver selection and switch timing**. For escalation, methods that reduce or summarize the preceding model's trajectory (e.g., `Compact_pre`, `Traj-drop`) can improve quality recovery and/or cost efficiency. Conversely, for downshift, preserving the prior model's context is crucial for maintaining quality. This implies that agent platforms should offer configurable or intelligently selected handoff interfaces.
*   **Beyond Conversational Context:** The research emphasizes that in long-horizon coding agents, a handoff involves not only transferring conversational context but also inheriting a **persistent repository state**. How trajectory information is transferred alongside this persistent state is shown to substantially affect both quality and cost, suggesting that developers must consider this dual state transfer.
*   **Adaptive Handoff Policies:** The study, by using fixed switch points, lays groundwork for future development of **adaptive, progress-aware policies** that dynamically decide when to switch models and how to manage the interface based on real-time agent performance and task state.
*   **Structured Handoffs:** The results encourage future work on developing **structured handoffs** that go beyond simple compaction or removal, selectively preserving, summarizing, or discarding specific components of the trajectory to maximize utility for the receiving model.
*   **Economic Implications:** The detailed cost analysis, particularly the demonstration of how raw escalation can be more expensive and less effective than restarting, has direct implications for cloud resource management and the financial viability of certain LLM agent deployment patterns.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond basic model routing by dissecting the mechanics and consequences of mid-task model handoffs. It provides foundational knowledge for building more robust, efficient, and cost-effective LLM agents by highlighting that the "handoff tax" is a quantifiable factor that can be mitigated through deliberate design of the handoff process.
