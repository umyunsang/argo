## Detailed Report: LEDGER: Claim-to-Evidence Trace Graphs for Auditing LLM Agents

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

*   **Daehong Kim**: Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA
*   **Haichao Miao**: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Livermore, CA

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The proliferation of large language model (LLM) agents has enabled them to execute complex, long-horizon technical workflows that involve extensive tool use, code execution, file modifications, and the generation of diverse artifacts. This increased autonomy and capability introduce a shift in the primary challenge for users: rather than focusing solely on output generation speed, the bottleneck moves to verifying the correctness and trustworthiness of the produced outputs. This necessitates robust auditing mechanisms for agent-generated work.

Existing research has addressed aspects of this problem through various approaches. Agent observability systems, such as Langsmith [8], aim to make fine-grained execution events visible by recording prompts, tool calls, model responses, errors, and intermediate outputs. Similarly, provenance systems (e.g., W3C PROV, scientific workflow provenance, and recent ML/agent-workflow provenance systems [9–12]) focus on tracking how data products and outputs are generated. While these systems provide a foundational layer of capture and visibility, the authors argue that visibility alone does not equate to auditability. A reviewer still needs to manually reconstruct which specific events, actions, artifacts, and validation steps are relevant to a particular conclusion or claim. Human review is frequently evidence-centered, beginning with a reported claim and then seeking its supporting evidence, the artifacts containing that evidence, the actions that produced or altered them, and any associated validation checks. Performing this reconstruction from a flat event record becomes increasingly costly and complex as session lengths grow.

The field of agent evaluation has recognized the importance of assessing an agent's entire run, not just its final output [1, 3]. Systems like ScienceAgentBench [13], Graphectory [14], and AgentAuditor [15] analyze agent trajectories, compute process metrics, or score safety and security behaviors across steps. Explanation-faithfulness research [4, 16] further underscores the necessity of inspecting execution evidence directly, rather than relying solely on agent-generated explanations. These approaches provide metrics or judgments but typically do not offer a structured pathway for human auditors to directly trace claims back to their supporting evidence.

Interfaces for inspecting model reasoning or generated work, such as ReasonGraph [19] and DeepVIS [22], provide visual structures for understanding agent behavior. Graph of Trace [24] is particularly close to the present work, organizing scientific-agent execution events into an updating directed graph. However, the authors differentiate LEDGER by stating that it operates as a sidecar tracer on *unmodified* interactive agent sessions, unlike systems that might require the agent to self-report. Furthermore, LEDGER emphasizes direct and interactive traversal to the underlying artifacts (e.g., files, plots, tables), which is critical for execution review involving mutable artifacts.

In this context, LEDGER positions itself as a system that builds upon existing observability and provenance foundations by adding a specialized *review layer*. It addresses the specific challenge of evidence-centered auditing by transforming raw execution records into a structured, traversable graph, thereby explicitly linking claims to their supporting evidence and reducing the manual effort required for verification.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The increasing deployment of LLM agents as interactive workers in technical and scientific domains, performing complex, multi-step tasks, motivated the development of LEDGER. These tasks often involve intricate sequences of planning, data and code inspection, tool invocations, code execution, file modifications, and the generation of diverse artifacts suchs as scripts, plots, and reports. As these agent sessions become longer and more intricate, their final outcomes depend on a growing array of intermediate decisions, actions, and artifacts.

This complexity shifts the effort required from users. While an agent can rapidly produce a plausible analysis, patch, report, or conclusion, accepting such results necessitates a thorough check of the underlying work. Users need to ascertain specific details: which inputs were examined, what hypotheses or implementation choices were explored, which files were altered, what artifacts were generated, what checks were performed, and, critically, which pieces of evidence substantiate the final claim. In this operational context, the utility of LLM agents is determined not only by their generation speed but also by the user's capacity to audit the recorded work effectively.

The primary motivations for LEDGER are:
1.  **Addressing the Auditability Gap**: Existing agent observability systems provide event-level records (e.g., prompts, tool calls, model responses). However, these flat event streams show *what happened* without explicitly indicating *which events are critical* for validating a specific conclusion. This requires human reviewers to manually reconstruct task structure and evidence relationships, a process that becomes expensive and difficult with increasing session length and complexity.
2.  **Supporting Evidence-Centered Review**: Human review processes often begin with a reported claim and then seek its supporting evidence. This involves identifying relevant artifacts, tracing back to the actions that produced or modified them, and determining if any validation steps were executed. LEDGER aims to automate the construction of these evidence pathways.

The key objectives of this work are:
1.  **To develop a claim-to-evidence trace graph construction method**: This involves building a sidecar tracer that runs alongside an unmodified interactive agent session. This tracer captures messages, tool calls, outputs, file interactions, and artifacts, parsing them into structured Trace Records. These records are then organized into a layered semantic trace graph, where artifact nodes represent inspectable evidence objects, and typed semantic edges connect claims to supporting work and validation steps.
2.  **To create an interface for evidence-centered agent review**: The system's interface is designed to expose the hierarchical structure of Workflow Nodes, Evidence Nodes, and raw Trace Records. It also displays graph updates, artifacts, and tracer-agent call records within a single dashboard. This allows reviewers to navigate efficiently between high-level workflow structures and the underlying source records, enabling direct inspection of details pertinent to audit.

By achieving these objectives, LEDGER aims to enhance the trustworthiness and usability of LLM agents by making their complex workflows transparent and auditable, thereby shifting the productivity bottleneck from manual reconstruction to structured evidence traversal.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

LEDGER operates as a sidecar tracer, capturing events from an observed agent session to construct a layered trace graph. This graph is designed to facilitate evidence-centered review by organizing captured records into a hierarchical structure that explicitly links claims to their supporting evidence.

**4.1 Capturing and Structuring Trace Records**
The foundational layer of LEDGER is the **Trace Record**, which comprises preserved, raw observations of events during a session. These records are not interpretations of the agent's reasoning but a stable substrate for graph construction, retaining message content, tool calls and results, lifecycle boundaries, artifact references, timing information, and links to the original transcript. This ensures that reviewers can distinguish between raw captured evidence and inferred graph structure.

*   **Capture Mechanism**: The prototype implementation for Codex agents utilizes lifecycle hooks (e.g., `SessionStart`, `UserPromptSubmit`, `PreToolUse`, `PostToolUse`, `PermissionRequest`, `Stop`) to record events. Each hook receives and logs a JSON payload. A `transcript_path` is used to refresh per-session dumps of the current transcript, capturing live snapshots during a turn and a final snapshot upon session completion. The transcript dump includes session metadata, turn contexts, user/assistant messages, tool-call records, tool-result records, reasoning summaries, and lifecycle events. This dual approach ensures comprehensive capture, with hook records providing near-live boundary points and copied transcripts serving as the source of truth for complete reconstruction.
*   **Trace Record Extraction**: Captured hook payloads and transcript JSONL are processed to create a `trace.json` summary. This process normalizes transcript rows into predefined record families: session and turn context, visible messages, tool calls and results, lifecycle events, reasoning summaries, parse errors, and coverage records. This step indexes transcript rows into stable record types while maintaining links to the original transcript, providing a structured yet uninterpreted view of the session. Each run stores the hook payloads, transcript, and trace summary in a dedicated session directory, allowing subsequent layers to reference this evidence substrate.

**4.2 Constructing Layered Review Structure**
LEDGER builds a hierarchical graph over these Trace Records, comprising **Evidence Nodes** and **Workflow Nodes**, connected by typed **Semantic Edges**. This layering balances readability with access to granular detail. The Trace Records are deterministically generated, while the Evidence and Workflow Layers are interpretations produced by the tracer agent, which the system acknowledges by keeping underlying records accessible for validation.

*   **Evidence Nodes**: These nodes group closely related Trace Records into inspectable units of work. For instance, a tool-call request and its corresponding result, sharing a group ID, are combined into a single Evidence Node.
    *   **Types and Categories**: Evidence Nodes have a `type` (e.g., `action`, `artifact`) and a `category`. Action categories describe observable session activities (e.g., `control` for lifecycle events, `user_message`, `assistant_message`, `tool_call`).
    *   **Artifact Nodes**: These are a specific type of Evidence Node representing inspectable objects generated or used during the session, such as code snippets, plots, tables, patches, or command outputs. They are recovered from tool-call Trace Records and their results, with the tracer extracting file paths and interaction types from command text and output. When an object is represented as an artifact node, the graph can link higher-level claims to concrete evidence.
*   **Workflow Nodes**: These nodes group several related Evidence Nodes into higher-level workflow phases, providing a summary view of long sessions. This layer helps abstract away numerous small actions that only make sense as part of a larger phase (e.g., data inspection before analysis).
    *   **Categories**: Workflow Nodes are categorized by the logical phase they represent (e.g., `context`, `plan`, `inspect`, `execute`, `validate`, `claim`).

**4.3 Linking Artifacts and Audit Paths**
**Semantic Edges** encode relationships between graph elements, operating at both the Evidence and Workflow Layers. These directed edges are added in trace order only when a semantic relation exists.

*   **Edge Types**: Common edge types include:
    *   `frames`: A requirement, constraint, or plan sets context for a target.
    *   `uses`: A work unit uses an artifact or resource as input.
    *   `produces`: A work unit creates or changes an artifact, output, or state.
    *   `informs`: Evidence or results shape analysis, plan, or action.
    *   `checked_by`: A change or result is validated by a target step.
    *   `supports`: Evidence or findings justify a claim or committed choice.
*   **Audit Path Functionality**: These edges enable reviewers to traverse the graph from a final claim back to supporting work and artifacts, or from an artifact to the action that produced it. Cross-layer edges maintain accountability by linking Workflow Nodes back to their constituent Evidence Nodes and underlying Trace Records. This turns the graph into an interpretable map of evidence relations overlaid on a visual summary of the session.

**4.4 LEDGER Interface**
The system includes a local dashboard designed for reviewing traced sessions.
*   **Graph-Guided Workflow Review**: The interface presents a two-level graph, allowing reviewers to first see a compact view of workflow phases (Workflow Nodes) and then drill down into specific messages, tool calls, outputs, files, and artifacts (Evidence Nodes). This facilitates checking workflow coverage and then inspecting supporting work units.
*   **Evidence Inspection**: Selecting a node reveals its category, status, description, and links to underlying Trace Records and related artifacts. Artifact views enable direct inspection of saved terminal output, patches, referenced files, generated plots, and result tables. The graph acts as an index to the recorded work, allowing direct access to source evidence.
*   **Trace-Construction Audit**: Event and log views provide access to raw Trace Records, graph updates, Evidence Layer edges, tracer events, model-call records, and any tracing errors. This allows reviewers to audit the graph construction process itself, distinguishing agent errors from tracing errors and verifying how captured records were transformed into the layered graph structure.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of LEDGER was demonstrated through two distinct case studies, each conducted using Codex agents powered by GPT-5.5 with live tracing enabled, and subsequently reviewed via the LEDGER dashboard. These case studies focused on showcasing how LEDGER's trace graph and dashboard facilitate human review by exposing workflow phases, key decisions, artifact lineage, validation steps, and claim-support paths.

**5.1 Case Study 1: Tabular Data Analysis**
This case study involved analyzing an hourly air-quality dataset. The agent's task was to clean the data (parse timestamps, convert numerics, handle missing values) and then characterize the typical daily pattern of main pollutant measurements, culminating in a Markdown report, a missingness table, an hourly summary table, and a plot.

*   **Artifact Lineage and Grounding**: The Workflow Node view revealed a clear data-analysis flow. The cleaning and summary plan emerged logically after initial file inspection, indicating that the agent's analysis choices were grounded in the observed file format. The Evidence Layer allowed inspection of specific steps like CSV preview, row-count checks, and environment checks that preceded script generation.
*   **Error and Repair Sequence Visibility**: The trace effectively captured an error-and-repair cycle. An initial script execution failed due to a missing optional dependency (Markdown-table call). LEDGER's Evidence Layer explicitly linked the failed command output to subsequent repair steps, including script patches, a successful rerun, and the validation of the regenerated report, tables, and plot. This allowed reviewers to observe the agent's self-correction process.
*   **Output-Centered Review Path**: The dashboard enabled a central review path focused on the output-checking phase. It linked the final reported claim to the generated report, tables, and plot. From there, the trace extended backward to the script creation, the failed execution, the repair patch, the successful rerun, and the preview commands responsible for producing or checking these artifacts. This demonstrated how LEDGER provides a traceable chain from a final claim (e.g., the daily pollutant pattern) to the supporting artifacts and the process that generated them. Additionally, quality-check artifacts, such as the missingness table, were placed alongside the reported results, allowing reviewers to verify if the report was adequately supported by the available data.

**5.2 Case Study 2: Feature Addition in an Existing Codebase**
This case study involved adding a new shortest-path utility function to the NetworkX Python graph library. The function was required to find a path between a source and target while visiting a given sequence of intermediate nodes in order, building on existing functions, supporting weighted/unweighted graphs, and adhering to NetworkX conventions.

*   **Code Grounding and Decision Transparency**: The trace explicitly separated the agent's initial implementation design from its later repair steps. An initial implementation passed its tests, but the trace subsequently showed a phase where the agent checked how existing shortest-path functions handled missing nodes, leading to a guard patch, a regression-test patch, and a focused second test run. This allowed a reviewer to distinguish which behaviors were part of the initial design and which were later addressed as edge cases, even though in the final diff, these changes might appear as one function.
*   **Pre-Edit Inspection and Design Rationale**: Before any code edits, the Workflow Node view detailed the agent's repository inspection phase, including searches for shortest-path exports, reads of relevant modules, inspection of nearby tests, and checks of documentation. These phases directly informed the agent's decision to place the new utility in the generic shortest-path module. The trace linked the implementation patch back to the prompt requirement for ordered intermediate nodes and to the reused shortest-path functions, making the design choice (ordered segment composition over existing calls) explicit and auditable.
*   **Validation Coverage and Claim Support**: The trace recorded which specific checks covered particular behaviors. For example, the first test run covered the ordered-visit behavior on weighted and unweighted graphs, while the regression test specifically checked the newly added guard for missing nodes. The final completion claim, when reviewed through LEDGER, traversed back through its `supports` edges to the changed files, the documentation update, the focused test run, the import check, and the final diff review, providing a comprehensive view of the evidence supporting the claim, rather than just a pass/fail status.

In both case studies, LEDGER effectively transformed a linear stream of agent actions into a structured, traversable graph. This facilitated evidence-centered auditing by exposing artifact lineage, detailing repair sequences, clarifying validation coverage, and explicitly linking claims to their supporting actions and artifacts, thereby enabling human reviewers to understand and verify complex agent workflows.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of LEDGER represents a significant contribution to the field of LLM agent auditing and transparency, addressing a growing bottleneck in the deployment and trustworthy utilization of advanced AI agents.

**Addressing a Critical Productivity Bottleneck**: As LLM agents become increasingly capable of executing long-horizon, complex technical workflows, the primary challenge for users shifts from generating outputs to thoroughly auditing their correctness and trustworthiness. LEDGER directly tackles this issue by providing a structured, traversable graph representation of agent sessions, thereby transforming manual, time-consuming evidence reconstruction into an efficient, evidence-centered review process. This has the potential to substantially increase the productivity of human users interacting with LLM agents.

**Enhancing Trustworthiness and Transparency**: By explicitly mapping claims to the actions, artifacts, and checks that support them, LEDGER increases the transparency of agent operations. This enhanced transparency can foster greater trust in agent-generated outputs, which is crucial for their adoption in sensitive applications such as scientific discovery, software development, and critical infrastructure management. Reviewers can move beyond simply accepting an agent's conclusion to understanding *why* that conclusion is supported by verifiable evidence.

**Improving Audit Efficiency and Quality**: The system's layered graph structure and interactive interface enable reviewers to quickly ascertain workflow coverage, identify key decisions, observe artifact lineage, and pinpoint validation steps. This reduces the cognitive load associated with sifting through raw event logs, leading to more efficient and potentially higher-quality audits. The ability to directly inspect artifacts and trace records from the graph further empowers reviewers to delve into granular details when necessary.

**Facilitating Debugging and Agent Improvement**: LEDGER's interface allows for the auditing of the trace construction itself, enabling reviewers to distinguish between an agent's operational errors and errors in the tracing or graph construction process. This capability is valuable for debugging agent behavior, understanding unexpected outcomes, and iteratively improving agent design and reliability. The case studies showcased how LEDGER can highlight error-and-repair sequences and reveal the rationale behind agent design choices, offering insights for future agent development.

**Foundational for Future Research and Development**: The work identifies several avenues for future enhancements, suggesting a roadmap for developing more robust and sophisticated auditing tools:
*   **Deterministic Trace Construction**: Future efforts could focus on making the trace construction process, particularly the grouping of records into Evidence and Workflow Nodes and the inference of semantic edges, more deterministic. This might involve stronger instrumentation, more structured tool records, or integrating with data provenance backends for finer-grained dependency tracking (e.g., table-, column-, and transformation-level provenance in data analysis workflows).
*   **Stability and Reliability Metrics**: The paper proposes conducting repeated-run studies to measure the stability of the generated nodes, edges, and claim-support paths, which would quantify the reliability of the trace graph as a consistent audit aid.
*   **Refined Graph Vocabulary and Visual Design**: There is room to improve the clarity and distinctness of edge types, explicitly distinguish between temporal, dataflow, validation, and evidential relations, and encode uncertainty or missing support directly within the graph. Advanced visual encodings could further separate deterministic from inferred structure, highlight unsupported claims, or reduce clutter through context-specific subgraph views.

In summary, LEDGER represents a practical step towards addressing the critical need for auditable LLM agents. By transforming raw execution data into an organized, navigable evidence graph, it aims to enhance the trust, efficiency, and transparency required for LLM agents to become fully integrated and reliable partners in complex technical and scientific workflows.
