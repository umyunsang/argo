### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly utilized as foundational components for agentic systems that interact with complex environments, employ tools, and undertake multi-step tasks. The behavior of these LLM agents is influenced not only by their underlying model parameters but also by an "agent harness." This external, editable harness encompasses components such as prompts, skills, memories, and tools, which guide the model's reasoning, actions, and error recovery without altering its internal weights.

Traditionally, these harnesses are designed manually, a process that can be brittle in dynamic deployment environments where task distributions may shift or new operational procedures emerge. To address this, the concept of "harness self-evolution" has gained prominence. In this paradigm, agents automatically update their external harnesses based on execution evidence, such as failures, feedback, successful trajectories, or newly discovered procedures. Recent research in self-evolving agents has demonstrated improvements in end-task performance through this approach, updating various harness components. These studies typically evaluate methods by measuring the overall improvement in agent performance.

This paper identifies a gap in this existing research by highlighting that end-to-end performance metrics conflate multiple sources of improvement. Specifically, it distinguishes between the capability of an LLM to *produce* useful harness updates (the "evolver" role) and its capability to *benefit* from these updates during task-solving (the "task-solving agent" role). Prior work has generally focused on the aggregate outcome, without systematically disentangling the contributions of the evolver and the agent. This work positions itself as a foundational analysis to clarify these distinct capabilities, investigating whether an LLM's intrinsic task-solving prowess predicts its performance in these two self-evolutionary roles.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically disentangle and analyze two distinct capabilities of LLM agents within the context of harness self-evolution:
1.  **Harness-updating capability**: The ability of an LLM, acting as an "evolver," to generate valuable and persistent harness updates from observed execution evidence.
2.  **Harness-benefit capability**: The ability of an LLM, acting as a "task-solving agent," to effectively leverage and benefit from these updated harnesses during subsequent task execution.

The motivation for this disentanglement stems from several practical and theoretical considerations:
*   **Clarifying Sources of Improvement**: Current end-to-end evaluations of self-evolving agents do not reveal whether performance gains originate from the evolver generating higher-quality updates or from the task-solving agent more effectively utilizing those updates. Understanding these distinct contributions is crucial for targeted system design and improvement.
*   **Informed Resource Allocation**: If certain models excel as evolvers while others excel as task-solving agents, or if there is a decoupling between base capability and evolution capabilities, this knowledge can inform where computational and developmental resources should be allocated. For instance, it addresses whether it is more beneficial to invest in a highly capable evolver or a highly capable task-solving agent.
*   **Guiding Agent Training and Development**: By identifying specific failure modes in harness activation or adherence, the research aims to provide actionable insights for training LLMs to become more effective agents in self-evolving systems. This includes understanding why weaker models may not benefit from updates despite having significant headroom for improvement.
*   **Understanding the Relationship with Base Capability**: A core motivation is to investigate whether an LLM's fundamental task-solving ability (its "base capability") directly predicts its performance in producing or benefiting from harness updates. The paper hypothesizes that this relationship might not be monotonic or straightforward.

To address these objectives, the study formally defines and empirically measures these two capabilities across a range of LLMs and agentic benchmarks.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an empirical analysis framework centered on a controlled evolution protocol and specific capability metrics.

**4.1 Evolution Protocol**
The study adopts an iterative "solve-evolve" loop for harness self-evolution. Starting with an initial harness (H₀), an agent (Aₜ₋₁) attempts to solve a batch of tasks (Xₜ) at each step (t). During task execution, the agent generates an execution trajectory (τₜ,ₓ) and final output (yₜ,ₓ). This execution evidence (Dₜ) is then fed to an evolver (e), which proposes and applies updates (ΔHₜ) to the previous harness (Hₜ₋₁) to produce the next harness (Hₜ). The LLM backbone (f) of the agent remains fixed throughout this process; only the external harness is updated. This loop continues for a predefined number of steps (T), yielding a final harness (Hₜ).

**4.2 Capability Metrics**
To quantify the various aspects of agent and evolver performance, three metrics are defined:
*   **Base Capability (Mbase(f))**: This measures the task-solving performance of a model *f* with the initial harness (H₀) on a given task set (X). It represents the agent's baseline performance without any evolution.
*   **Pairwise Evolution Gain (Δ(f, e))**: This quantifies the improvement of a specific agent-evolver pairing (f, e) by comparing the agent's performance with the final evolved harness (H(f,e)T) against its base capability (Mbase(f)).
*   **Harness-updating Capability (Δupdate(e))**: This metric assesses an evolver *e*'s ability to produce useful updates. It is calculated as the mean pairwise gain (Δ(f, e)) across a predefined set of "anchor agents" (F⋆).
*   **Harness-benefit Capability (Δbenefit(f))**: This metric evaluates a model *f*'s ability to benefit from updated harnesses. It is estimated as the maximum pairwise gain (Δ(f, e)) achieved by agent *f* across a predefined set of "anchor evolvers" (E⋆).

**4.3 Experimental Setup**
*   **Datasets**: Three diverse agentic benchmarks are used:
    *   **SWE-bench Verified (SWE)**: 500 tasks involving software engineering problems from real GitHub issues, requiring code patching.
    *   **MCP-Atlas (MCP)**: 500 tasks focused on multi-server tool-use competency, requiring orchestration of 3-6 tool calls across 36 servers.
    *   **SkillsBench (SB)**: 86 tasks spanning 11 domains, focusing on skill-based execution with deterministic verifiers.
*   **Models**: Seven LLM backbones are selected, representing various capability tiers from open-source and closed-source families:
    *   Closed-source: Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5.
    *   Open-source: Qwen3-235B-A22B, Qwen3-32B, Qwen3.5-9B, GPT-OSS-120B.
    *   Six models (excluding Qwen3.5-9B) are used as task-solving agents, while all seven are used as evolvers.
*   **Evaluation Protocol**: Pass rate is the primary scoring metric (Jₓ) for all benchmarks. An in-situ evaluation setting is employed, where each task is scored with the harness *before* its execution evidence is used for an update. Scores are aggregated over the task stream.
*   **Implementation Details**:
    *   A fixed solve-evolve loop is used, with consistent prompt templates for both agents and evolvers, and a fixed trajectory window.
    *   Evolvable components vary by benchmark: skills for SWE-bench and SkillsBench; skills, prompts, and memories for MCP-Atlas. Other components like tool interfaces are fixed.
    *   All agent-evolver pairs start with the same initial harness and task stream.

**4.4 Analysis Approach**
The analysis is structured into two main parts:
*   **Evolver-side analysis**: This investigates harness-updating capability by fixing the task-solving agent (using Opus 4.6, Sonnet 4.6, and Qwen3-235B as anchor agents, F⋆) and varying the evolver across all seven LLMs.
*   **Agent-side analysis**: This explores harness-benefit capability by fixing the evolvers (using Opus 4.6, Sonnet 4.6, and Qwen3-235B as anchor evolvers, E⋆) and varying the task-solving agent across the six capable LLMs.
*   **In-depth failure analysis**: For the agent-side analysis, specific qualitative and quantitative investigations (e.g., Skill-Load Rate, Harness-Following Rate, phase-level adherence scores) are conducted on SkillsBench to diagnose the reasons for low harness-benefit in weak-tier models.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The analysis revealed systematic decouplings between an LLM's base capability and its harness evolution capabilities.

**5.1 Evolver-side Analysis: Harness-updating Capability**
*   **Observation 1: Harness-updating is largely independent of base capability.**
    *   The variation in harness-updating gain (Δupdate) across different evolvers was narrow, with a maximum difference of 3.1 percentage points (pp) on any benchmark. This suggests that the quality of harness updates produced does not substantially improve with a more capable evolver.
    *   No single evolver consistently outperformed others across all benchmarks. For example, Qwen3-235B led on SWE-bench (8.2 pp gain) but ranked last on MCP-Atlas (0.6 pp gain).
    *   Model scale did not predict evolver performance. The smallest model tested, Qwen3.5-9B (9B parameters), achieved the highest gain on SkillsBench (3.8 pp), surpassing models like Claude Opus 4.6 (2.3 pp) and Qwen3-235B (1.5 pp).
    *   A case study on SkillsBench showed that Qwen3.5-9B could produce a skill procedurally isomorphic to one generated by Claude Opus 4.6, leading to identical downstream task-solving success when used by the same task-solving agent.
*   **Observation 2: Post-evolution performance is predominantly determined by the task-solving agent's base capability, not the evolver's identity.**
    *   For a fixed task-solving agent, the spread in performance across different evolvers was small (at most 5.1 pp for Qwen3-235B on MCP-Atlas).
    *   In contrast, the performance gap between different task-solving agents (based on their inherent base capabilities) was significantly larger (e.g., 36.0 pp between Opus 4.6 and Qwen3-235B on MCP-Atlas).
    *   Even when pairing a weaker anchor agent with its best-performing evolver against a stronger anchor agent with its worst-performing evolver, the stronger agent consistently maintained a lead of 18.6 to 35.2 pp across all benchmarks.

**5.2 Agent-side Analysis: Harness-benefit Capability**
*   **Observation 1: Harness-benefit is non-monotonic with respect to base capability.**
    *   Mid-tier models (e.g., Qwen3-235B, GPT-OSS-120B) generally exhibited the highest harness-benefit (Δbenefit). On SWE-bench, Qwen3-235B achieved the largest gain (19.3 pp), while the weaker Qwen3-32B gained 4.4 pp and the stronger Opus 4.6 gained 2.6 pp. A similar pattern was observed on MCP-Atlas, where GPT-OSS-120B benefited most (7.0 pp).
    *   Strong-tier models (e.g., Claude Opus 4.6) showed smaller gains, consistent with a ceiling effect, as their high base capability left less room for improvement.
    *   Weak-tier models (e.g., Qwen3-32B) also showed limited gains, despite having significant headroom for improvement above their low base capabilities. This indicated a different bottleneck than the ceiling effect.
*   **Observation 2: Weak-tier models exhibit low harness-benefit due to two primary failure modes.**
    *   **Harness Activation Failure**: Weak models frequently failed to integrate relevant harness artifacts into their active working context.
        *   On SkillsBench, the skill-load rate (SLR) for strong models like Opus 4.6, Sonnet 4.6, and Qwen3-235B was approximately 96%. However, for GPT-OSS-120B, it dropped to 44.6%, and for Qwen3-32B, it was only 25.1%.
        *   A case study showed Qwen3-32B failing to load a skill because it embedded the `load_skill` request within a multi-key JSON action, which the environment parser rejected, preventing the skill body from entering the context.
    *   **Harness Adherence Failure**: Even when relevant harness artifacts were loaded, weak-tier models often failed to consistently follow the provided guidance during task execution.
        *   The Harness-Following Rate (HFR) for Opus 4.6 was 75.7%, whereas for Qwen3-32B, it was 14.2%.
        *   Qwen3-235B, despite having a high skill-load rate (0.961), exhibited a low HFR (0.350), indicating that merely loading the harness does not guarantee effective utilization. Its pass rate among skill-loaded trajectories (LPR) was 0.022, compared to 0.177 for Opus 4.6.
        *   A case study illustrated Qwen3-32B treating a procedural skill (with a TTS fallback chain) as a literal script. After an initial failure, it terminated without attempting the alternative steps prescribed by the skill.
    *   **Diagnosis: Weak instruction following over long-horizon execution.**
        *   A phase-level adherence analysis demonstrated that adherence in weak models decayed significantly over the trajectory. Qwen3-32B's adherence score dropped from 0.52 immediately after harness loading to 0.13 by the final validation phase. In contrast, Opus 4.6 maintained stable adherence (0.89 to 0.80) over the same phases. This suggests that weaker models struggle with sustained instruction following rather than just initial misinterpretation.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides empirical evidence that separates the performance of LLMs in updating external harnesses from their ability to benefit from those updates. The findings have several implications for the design, development, and training of self-evolving LLM agent systems:

*   **Strategic Allocation of Computational Resources**: The observation that harness-updating capability is relatively flat across different LLM tiers suggests that investing significant computational resources or using frontier models specifically for the evolver role may yield diminishing returns. The post-evolution performance is primarily bottlenecked by the task-solving agent's base capabilities. This implies that developers should prioritize allocating capability budget towards strengthening the core task-solving agent rather than solely focusing on the evolver.
*   **Targeted Agent Training for Harness Invocation**: The identification of "harness activation failure" in weak-tier models highlights a critical area for agent training. Future agent development could focus on explicitly teaching models to reliably identify, parse, and load relevant harness artifacts (e.g., skills, memory entries) into their working context. This could involve training on diverse interaction protocols and robust error handling for external inputs.
*   **Enhancing Long-Horizon Instruction Following**: The "harness adherence failure" and the observed decay in adherence over time underscore the need to improve LLMs' ability to sustain instruction following across complex, multi-step tasks. Agent training paradigms could incorporate reinforcement learning or fine-tuning objectives that specifically penalize deviations from loaded harness guidance over extended execution trajectories, promoting robust and persistent adherence to procedural instructions.
*   **Informed Model Selection**: The non-monotonic relationship between base capability and harness-benefit indicates that simply using the most powerful LLM as an agent does not guarantee the highest gains from self-evolution. Mid-tier models may represent a "sweet spot" where they have sufficient base capability to understand and execute complex tasks but still possess significant headroom to benefit from harness updates. This understanding can guide the selection of appropriate LLM backbones for different roles within an agentic system.

Overall, this study shifts the focus from monolithic end-to-end evaluations to a decomposed understanding of self-evolutionary capabilities, offering specific, actionable guidance for advancing the efficacy and robustness of LLM agents.
